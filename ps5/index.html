The method, every question

  1. Model: what is the system, what is fixed, what is simplified (rigid weightless rods, point masses, small angles).
  2. Name every quantity, and say where it comes from.
  3. Force map: every outside force, where it acts, which way.
  4. Choose the law (balance for rest, Newton's 2nd law for motion).
  5. Write, approximate, solve.
  6. Check units and simple limits.

Problem 1(a) · pendulum held at an angle

Find the equilibrium angle θ (≪ 1 rad) when F pushes at A. Then find the force F′ at m that gives the same angle.

Torque. How strongly a force twists an object about a pivot: $$\tau = (\text{force})\times(\text{lever arm}).$$ The lever arm is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line the force acts along. Far from the pivot and across the rod means a big twist. Aimed at the pivot means no twist.
Equilibrium. At rest and staying at rest: the twists cancel, $$\textstyle\sum \tau = 0 \text{ about the pivot.}$$ The pivot's own force acts at the pivot, so it has no lever arm and drops out.
Small angles. For θ ≪ 1 rad: $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and $\cos\theta\approx 1$.

Where the lever arms come from: point A is $a\cos\theta$ below the pivot and $a\sin\theta$ to the side. The mass is $L\cos\theta$ below and $L\sin\theta$ to the side. (Right triangle with the rod as the hypotenuse.) A horizontal force's lever arm is the vertical distance. A vertical force's lever arm is the sideways distance.

Steps

  1. Twist from $F$ = twist from $mg$. Write it with the small-angle forms and solve for θ.
  2. Twist from $F'$ = twist from $F$ (same angle, so gravity's twist is unchanged). Solve for $F'$.
Check. θ should grow with $F$ and $a$ and shrink with $m$ and $L$. Is $F'$ bigger or smaller than $F$? It should be smaller, because $F'$ pushes with a longer lever arm.

Problem 1(b) · equations of motion, two coupled pendulums

Two identical pendulums (mass $m$, rod $L$) joined by a spring $k$ at distance $a$ below each pivot. The spring is unstretched when both rods hang vertically. Write the equations for θ₁ and θ₂.

Newton's 2nd law for turning. Net twist produces angular acceleration: $$I\,\ddot\theta = \textstyle\sum\tau.$$ Here $I$ is the rotational inertia, how hard the object is to spin up. For a point mass $m$ on a weightless rod of length $L$, $I = mL^2$.
Spring (Hooke's law). Force = $k$ × (change in length). It points so as to restore the natural length: a compressed spring pushes its ends apart, a stretched spring pulls them together.
Sign convention. Twist toward larger θ (to the right) is positive.

Check. Set $k=0$. Each equation should become the plain pendulum, $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$. Also, the spring terms in the two equations should be equal and opposite (Newton's 3rd law).

Problem 1(c) · describe the normal modes

Normal mode. A motion where every part oscillates at the same single frequency, all in step: they reach their extremes together and pass through vertical together. Two coupled objects have two normal modes. Any motion of the system is a mix of them.
Symmetry shortcut (the problem's hint). The two pendulums are identical, so guess the two simplest patterns:
  • $\theta_1 = \theta_2$: they swing together, side by side.
  • $\theta_1 = -\theta_2$: mirror images, swinging toward and away from each other.

Steps

  1. Put $\theta_1=\theta_2$ into both equations from 1(b). Do both reduce to the same single equation? What happens to the spring term, and why physically (is the spring ever stretched)?
  2. Put $\theta_1=-\theta_2$ into both. Same questions: is the spring stretched now, and does it add to gravity's restoring twist?
  3. Describe each mode in words: how the pendulums move relative to each other, and what the spring does.

Equivalent algebra: add the two equations to get one for $q_+ = \theta_1+\theta_2$, and subtract them to get one for $q_- = \theta_1-\theta_2$. Each is a single pendulum-type equation.

Problem 1(d) · frequencies of the normal modes

Simple harmonic motion. Any equation of the form $$\ddot x = -(\text{positive constant})\cdot x$$ describes oscillation at angular frequency ω, with $\omega^2 = \text{that constant}$. Example: the single pendulum, $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\,\theta$, so $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$.

Steps

  1. Each mode in 1(c) gives an equation of this form. Read off ω² for each.
  2. Which mode is faster, and why? (Hint: in which one does the spring add to gravity's pull back to vertical?)
Check. With $k\to 0$ or $a\to 0$ (no coupling), both frequencies should become $\sqrt{g/L}$. Units: $ka^2/(mL^2)$ must be 1/s², the same as $g/L$.

Problem 2 · N coupled oscillators, driven at one end

French 5-16: driven at ω < 2ω₀ with $y_0 = 0$ and $y_{N+1} = h\cos\omega t$. Find the amplitudes.

The setup (French Ch. 5). Each bead is pulled sideways by the string on its two sides. For small displacements, the pull on bead $p$ depends on how far it sits from its neighbours: $$\ddot y_p = -2\omega_0^2\,y_p + \omega_0^2\,(y_{p+1}+y_{p-1}),\qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{T}{m\,l}.$$ Check the notation against your book's section on N coupled oscillators.

$\omega_0^2 = T/(ml)$string tension ÷ (bead mass × spacing). Sets how stiffly the beads are coupled.
$-2\omega_0^2 y_p$bead $p$ pulled back by both string segments
$+\omega_0^2(y_{p+1}+y_{p-1})$its neighbours' displacements dragging it along
$y_0=0$left end fixed
$y_{N+1}=h\cos\omega t$right end shaken with amplitude $h$ at frequency ω
Steady state. After start-up, every bead moves at the driving frequency: $y_p = A_p\cos\omega t$, with $A_p$ bead $p$'s amplitude. Then $\ddot y_p = -\omega^2 A_p\cos\omega t$.
Identities you'll need. $$\sin(x+y)+\sin(x-y) = 2\sin x\cos y,\qquad 1-\cos\alpha = 2\sin^2(\alpha/2).$$

Steps

  1. Substitute $y_p = A_p\cos\omega t$ into the equation of motion. Cancel $\cos\omega t$. You get a relation between $A_{p-1}$, $A_p$ and $A_{p+1}$.
  2. Try $A_p = C\sin(\alpha p)$ (the hint). Use the first identity on $A_{p+1}+A_{p-1}$. Everything should be proportional to $\sin(\alpha p)$; cancel it to get an equation linking ω and α.
  3. Rewrite it with the second identity. Why does ω < 2ω₀ guarantee a real α? (What is the largest value $\sin^2$ can take?)
  4. Left end: does $A_0 = C\sin 0$ satisfy $y_0 = 0$ automatically?
  5. Right end: set $A_{N+1} = h$. Solve for $C$.
  6. Write $A_p$ in terms of $h$, α, $p$ and $N$, with α fixed by step 2.
Check. At $p = N+1$ your $A_p$ should equal $h$. Also look at what happens when $\sin(\alpha(N+1))\to 0$. That is resonance: the driving frequency matches one of the system's normal-mode frequencies.
