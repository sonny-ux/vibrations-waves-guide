The method

  1. Model: the system, what is fixed, what is simplified.
  2. Name: every quantity, with where it comes from.
  3. Force map: every outside force, where it acts, which way.
  4. Law: balance (at rest) or Newton's 2nd law (moving), for forces or torques.

  6. Check: units and simple limits.

Problem 1(a) · pendulum held at an angle

A force F is applied at point A of a pendulum. At what angle θ (≪ 1 rad) is the new equilibrium? What force F′, applied at m, would produce the same result?

1 · Model

  • System: rod + mass, one rigid object that can only turn about the pivot.
  • Rod rigid and weightless; mass is a point at the end.
  • "Equilibrium": at rest at angle θ and staying there.
Choosing the system: why rod + mass together?

Forces between parts of the system (the rod pulling the mass) are internal: they cancel in pairs and never appear. Taking rod + mass together leaves only outside forces. If you took the mass alone, $F$ would not even act on it (it pushes the rod), and the rod's unknown pull would enter instead.

3 · Force map

Extended-body force map: why forces are drawn where they act

For an object that can turn, where a force acts decides how much it twists the object. So draw the outline of the object and attach each arrow at its real point, not all at one dot.

4 · Law

Why torque. The pivot holds the top of the rod in place, so the rod cannot move anywhere. It can only turn. So "equilibrium" here means the turning effects of the forces cancel. A force's turning effect about the pivot is its torque, so the law is

$$\textstyle\sum\tau = 0\quad\text{(about the pivot).}$$

Why about the pivot. $P$ acts at the pivot itself, at distance 0, so it has no torque about that point. The one force we know nothing about drops out.

Torque: what it is

Split a force, at the point where it acts, into a part along the rod and a part perpendicular to the rod. The part along the rod only pushes or pulls on the pivot, so it can't turn anything. The perpendicular part turns the rod, and more so the farther from the pivot it acts (think of a door handle far from the hinge). So:

$$\tau = (\text{perpendicular part of the force})\times(\text{distance from the pivot}).$$

5 · Solve

Split each force at its point into its part along the rod and its part perpendicular to the rod. Only the perpendicular part (solid) turns the rod.

ForcePerpendicular partWhy that partDistanceTorque
$F$ at A$F\cos\theta$$F$ is horizontal; the rod's perpendicular is tilted θ from horizontal$a$$Fa\cos\theta$, toward larger θ
$mg$ at mass$mg\sin\theta$$mg$ is vertical; the rod is tilted θ from vertical$L$$mgL\sin\theta$, back toward vertical
Why $\cos\theta$ for one and $\sin\theta$ for the other

A force at angle φ to a line has part $F\cos\varphi$ along the line and $F\sin\varphi$ across it. Gravity points straight down and the rod is θ from straight down, so gravity is at angle θ to the rod: across part $mg\sin\theta$. $F$ is horizontal, at angle $90^\circ-\theta$ to the rod: across part $F\sin(90^\circ-\theta)=F\cos\theta$.

Equilibrium: the two torques turn opposite ways, so for them to cancel they must be equal in size:

$$F\,a\cos\theta = mg\,L\sin\theta$$

Small angles (the problem says θ ≪ 1): replace $\sin\theta\to\theta$ and $\cos\theta\to1$:

$$F\,a = mg\,L\,\theta \qquad\text{solve this for }\theta.$$
Small angles: why $\sin\theta\approx\theta,\ \cos\theta\approx1$

For $\theta\ll1$ rad, the Taylor series $\sin\theta=\theta-\theta^3/6+\dots$ and $\cos\theta=1-\theta^2/2+\dots$ are dominated by their first terms.

F′: horizontal at the mass, so by the same split its perpendicular part is $F'\cos\theta$, at distance $L$: torque $F'L\cos\theta$. Same angle means gravity's torque is unchanged, so $F'$ must supply exactly the torque $F$ supplied:

$$F'L\cos\theta = F\,a\cos\theta \qquad\text{solve this for }F'.$$

6 · Check

θ grows with $F$ and $a$, shrinks with $m$ and $L$. $F' < F$ (since $a<L$): pushing farther from the pivot takes less force. Units: $Fa/(mgL)$ is N·m / N·m, a pure number, as an angle in radians must be.
Write-up 1(a)

Take rod + mass as the system. The outside forces are $F$ at A, $mg$ at the mass, and the pivot force. Torques about the pivot remove the pivot force. At equilibrium the torque of $F$ (lever arm $a\cos\theta$) balances that of gravity (lever arm $L\sin\theta$):

$$Fa\cos\theta = mgL\sin\theta.$$

For $\theta\ll1$, $\cos\theta\approx1$ and $\sin\theta\approx\theta$:

$$\boxed{\theta = \frac{Fa}{mgL}}$$

A horizontal $F'$ at the mass has lever arm $L\cos\theta$. Producing the same θ requires the same torque as $F$:

$$F'L\cos\theta = Fa\cos\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{F' = \frac{a}{L}F}$$

(This one holds exactly, not just for small θ.)

Problem 1(b) · equations of motion

Two identical pendulums (equal masses, rigid weightless rods) coupled by a light spring, unstretched when both rods are vertical. Write the differential equations for small-amplitude oscillations in terms of θ₁ and θ₂. Neglect damping.

1 · Model

  • Two systems: pendulum 1 and pendulum 2, each rod + mass turning about its own pivot.
  • The spring is outside each pendulum, so it is an outside force on both: it pushes or pulls each at its attachment point, distance $a$ down the rod.
  • Now they move, so torques do not cancel. The net torque produces angular acceleration.
  • Small amplitude: θ₁, θ₂ ≪ 1.

3 · Force map (pendulum 1)

Hooke's law: the spring force, and why it is $k a(\theta_1-\theta_2)$

A spring pushes or pulls with force $k\times$(change in length), always toward restoring its natural length. Here each end moves sideways by $a\sin\theta_i\approx a\theta_i$. The vertical shifts are of order $a\theta^2$, too small to matter. The length changes by the difference of the two shifts: $a\theta_1-a\theta_2$. If θ₁ = θ₂ the ends move together and the spring does nothing.

4 · Law

Why a different law from 1(a). In 1(a) nothing moved, so the torques cancelled. Now the pendulums swing, so the torques don't cancel, and the leftover torque changes how fast each one turns. That is Newton's 2nd law in turning form, applied to each pendulum about its own pivot:

$$I\,\ddot\theta = \textstyle\sum\tau$$

Where $I = mL^2$ comes from. The mass moves along an arc of length $s = L\theta$, so its acceleration along the arc is $L\ddot\theta$. Newton's 2nd law along the arc: (perpendicular force) $= m\,L\ddot\theta$. Multiply both sides by $L$ to turn force into torque: $\tau = mL^2\ddot\theta$. So for a point mass on a weightless rod, $I = mL^2$.

5 · Solve

Find each torque on pendulum 1 the same way as in 1(a): perpendicular part of the force × distance from the pivot. Sign: + means toward larger θ.

Gravity. Same as 1(a): perpendicular part $mg\sin\theta_1$ at distance $L$, pulling back toward vertical (−), then small angles:

$$\tau_{g,1} = -mgL\sin\theta_1 \approx -mgL\,\theta_1$$

How much the spring is squeezed. Each spring end sits a distance $a$ down its rod, so it moves sideways by $a\sin\theta_i\approx a\theta_i$ (same triangle as 1(a)). The spring's length changes by the difference of the two moves:

$$\Delta\ell = a\theta_1 - a\theta_2 = a(\theta_1-\theta_2)\qquad(\text{shorter when positive})$$
Why the difference, and why only sideways

If both ends move right by the same amount, the spring just slides along and keeps its length. Only the difference changes its length. The ends also rise slightly, by about $a\theta^2/2$, but that is second order in a small angle and is dropped.

Spring force, from Hooke's law (force = $k$ × change in length, pushing to restore the natural length):

$$F_s = k\,a(\theta_1-\theta_2)$$

Spring torque. $F_s$ is horizontal at distance $a$. As with $F$ in 1(a), its perpendicular part is $F_s\cos\theta\approx F_s$. A squeezed spring pushes pendulum 1 back (−) and pendulum 2 forward (+), equal and opposite:

$$\tau_{s,1} = -ka^2(\theta_1-\theta_2),\qquad \tau_{s,2} = +ka^2(\theta_1-\theta_2)$$

Assemble $mL^2\ddot\theta_i = \tau_{g,i}+\tau_{s,i}$ for each pendulum. Then divide by $mL^2$ so each equation reads (angular acceleration) + (restoring terms) = 0.

6 · Check

With $k=0$, each equation becomes the single pendulum $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$. The spring terms are equal and opposite (Newton's 3rd law). Units: $ka^2/(mL^2)$ has units (N/m)·m²/(kg·m²) = 1/s², the same as $g/L$.
Write-up 1(b)

Apply $I\ddot\theta=\sum\tau$ about each pivot, with $I=mL^2$. For small angles the spring's ends shift by $a\theta_1$ and $a\theta_2$, so it is compressed by $a(\theta_1-\theta_2)$. It exerts a horizontal force $ka(\theta_1-\theta_2)$ with lever arm $\approx a$, pushing pendulum 1 back and pendulum 2 forward. Gravity gives restoring torque $-mgL\theta_i$:

$$mL^2\ddot\theta_1 = -mgL\,\theta_1 - ka^2(\theta_1-\theta_2)$$ $$mL^2\ddot\theta_2 = -mgL\,\theta_2 + ka^2(\theta_1-\theta_2)$$

Dividing by $mL^2$:

$$\boxed{\ddot\theta_1 + \frac{g}{L}\theta_1 + \frac{ka^2}{mL^2}(\theta_1-\theta_2)=0,\qquad \ddot\theta_2 + \frac{g}{L}\theta_2 - \frac{ka^2}{mL^2}(\theta_1-\theta_2)=0}$$

Problem 1(c) · the normal modes

Describe the motion of the pendulums in each of the normal modes.

1 · Model

Same system and equations as 1(b). The question: which motions keep a single frequency?

Normal mode: what it is and why it's here

A normal mode is a motion in which every part oscillates at the same frequency, in step. All parts pass through equilibrium together and reach their extremes together. Two coupled pendulums have two such modes, and any motion is a combination of them. The problem asks for these two special motions.

Symmetry: why guess θ₁ = θ₂ and θ₁ = −θ₂

The two pendulums are identical, so swapping them changes nothing. The motions that respect that symmetry are "the same" ($\theta_1=\theta_2$) and "mirror images" ($\theta_1=-\theta_2$). The hint says to use this, then confirm in the equations.

2–3 · Name, picture

4–5 · Test each guess in the equations

Why substitute. In a mode both pendulums follow one and the same equation. Putting the guess into both equations of 1(b) tests that: the guess is a mode if both equations turn into the same one.

Guess 1, $\theta_1=\theta_2=\theta$. Then $\theta_1-\theta_2=0$, so the spring term vanishes from both equations, which become

$$\ddot\theta + \frac{g}{L}\,\theta = 0\qquad(\text{both}).$$

Guess 2, $\theta_1=\theta,\ \theta_2=-\theta$. Then $\theta_1-\theta_2=2\theta$. The first equation becomes the line below. The second becomes the same line multiplied by −1, so it is the same equation:

$$\ddot\theta + \frac{g}{L}\,\theta + \frac{2ka^2}{mL^2}\,\theta = 0\qquad(\text{both}).$$

Equivalent algebra: add and subtract the two equations of 1(b) to get separate equations for $\theta_1+\theta_2$ and $\theta_1-\theta_2$.

6 · Check

A guess is a true mode only if both equations reduce to the same equation. Otherwise the two pendulums would need different motions.
Write-up 1(c)

Mode 1 (in phase), $\theta_1=\theta_2$: the two pendulums swing together, side by side, with equal amplitude. The spring's ends move together, so it never stretches or compresses and exerts no force. Each pendulum swings as if the spring were absent. Substituting gives $\ddot\theta + (g/L)\theta = 0$ for both.

Mode 2 (out of phase), $\theta_1=-\theta_2$: the pendulums are mirror images, swinging toward each other and then apart. The spring is compressed and stretched by $2a\theta$, which adds to gravity's restoring torque. Substituting gives $\ddot\theta + \left(\dfrac{g}{L}+\dfrac{2ka^2}{mL^2}\right)\theta=0$ for both.

Problem 1(d) · normal-mode frequencies

Calculate the frequencies of the normal modes of the system.

Law and solve

Why ω is the square root of the constant. Both mode equations have the form $\ddot\theta = -C\,\theta$. Try $\theta = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ and differentiate twice: $\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\theta$. This matches $\ddot\theta = -C\theta$ exactly when

$$\omega^2 = C .$$

So read the constant multiplying θ in each mode equation from 1(c) and take its square root.

Check

With $k\to0$ or $a\to0$, both frequencies become $\sqrt{g/L}$ (uncoupled pendulums). Mode 2 is faster, because the spring adds to the restoring torque only in mode 2.
Write-up 1(d)

Each mode obeys $\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\theta$. Reading off from 1(c):

$$\boxed{\omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}},\qquad \omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}+\frac{2ka^2}{mL^2}}}$$

The in-phase mode has the free-pendulum frequency, because the spring is idle. The out-of-phase mode is higher, because the spring adds stiffness $2ka^2$.

Problem 2 · N coupled oscillators, driven

Consider N coupled oscillators driven at frequency ω < 2ω₀ (i.e. $y_0=0$, $y_{N+1}=h\cos\omega t$). Find the resulting amplitudes of the N oscillators. [Hint: the equations of motion are the same as in the undriven case; only the boundary conditions differ. Try $A_p = C\sin\alpha p$ and determine α and C.]

1 · Model

  • French's chain: N beads of mass $m$ on a light string under tension $T$, spaced $l$ apart, moving sideways only.
  • The left end ($p=0$) is fixed. The right end ($p=N+1$) is shaken: $y_{N+1}=h\cos\omega t$.
  • Small displacements: the tension stays $T$ and the string angles are small.

3 · Force map (bead p)

4 · Law

Sideways force from each segment. Each segment pulls bead $p$ along itself with tension $T$. Only the sideways part moves the bead sideways. For a small tilt that part is $T$ × (slope), and the slope is rise over run:

$$\text{right segment: } T\,\frac{y_{p+1}-y_p}{l},\qquad \text{left segment: } T\,\frac{y_{p-1}-y_p}{l}.$$
Why sideways part = T × slope

Sideways part $=T\sin\phi$, where φ is the segment's tilt. For small φ, $\sin\phi\approx\tan\phi$, and $\tan\phi$ is the slope, rise/run $=(y_{p+1}-y_p)/l$.

Newton's 2nd law for bead $p$, sideways: mass × acceleration = sum of these two forces:

$$m\ddot y_p = \frac{T}{l}(y_{p+1}-y_p) + \frac{T}{l}(y_{p-1}-y_p)$$

Divide by $m$ and name the constant $\omega_0^2 = T/(ml)$ (units 1/s², a frequency squared):

$$\ddot y_p = \omega_0^2\,(y_{p+1}+y_{p-1}-2y_p).$$

This is the same equation as the undriven chain. Only the ends differ.

5 · Solve

Steady state. A driven system, once start-up effects die away, moves at the driving frequency. So every bead follows

$$y_p = A_p\cos\omega t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ddot y_p = -\omega^2 A_p\cos\omega t .$$

Substitute into the law and cancel the common $\cos\omega t$. This leaves an equation linking neighbouring amplitudes:

$$-\omega^2A_p = \omega_0^2\,(A_{p+1}+A_{p-1}-2A_p).$$

Why try $A_p = C\sin\alpha p$. The hint suggests it. A sine shape along the chain is natural for a wave, and $\sin 0 = 0$ already satisfies the fixed left end. To test it, use the identity $\sin(x+y)+\sin(x-y)=2\sin x\cos y$:

$$A_{p+1}+A_{p-1} = C\sin\alpha(p+1)+C\sin\alpha(p-1) = 2C\sin\alpha p\,\cos\alpha .$$

Put it in. Every term now contains $C\sin\alpha p$. Cancel it, and what is left must hold for every $p$ at once. That fixes α in terms of ω:

$$-\omega^2 = \omega_0^2(2\cos\alpha-2)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega^2 = 2\omega_0^2(1-\cos\alpha) = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2\tfrac{\alpha}{2}.$$

The last step uses $1-\cos\alpha = 2\sin^2(\alpha/2)$. Take the square root and solve for α.

Why ω < 2ω₀ matters

$\sin(\alpha/2) = \omega/(2\omega_0)$, and a sine is at most 1, so a real α exists only if $\omega\le 2\omega_0$. Above that, α is complex and the motion dies off along the chain (the problem's note).

Boundary conditions fix $C$. Left end: $A_0 = C\sin 0 = 0$, as required. Right end: bead $N+1$ is the driver, so its amplitude must be $h$:

$$C\sin\alpha(N+1) = h \qquad\text{solve for } C,\ \text{then write } A_p.$$
Resonance

If $\sin\alpha(N+1)=0$, $C$ blows up. That happens when $\alpha = n\pi/(N+1)$, which are exactly the undriven chain's normal-mode frequencies. Driving at a natural frequency gives resonance.

6 · Check

At $p=N+1$ the formula gives $A_{N+1}=h$, and at $p=0$ it gives 0. With $N=1$, a single bead: $\alpha$ from $\sin(\alpha/2)=\omega/2\omega_0$ gives $A_1 = h\sin\alpha/\sin2\alpha = h/(2\cos\alpha)$. Use $\cos\alpha = 1-\omega^2/(2\omega_0^2)$ to compare with a direct solution of $-\omega^2A_1=\omega_0^2(h-2A_1)$.
Write-up 2

Each bead obeys $\ddot y_p=\omega_0^2(y_{p+1}+y_{p-1}-2y_p)$, with $\omega_0^2=T/ml$. In steady state $y_p=A_p\cos\omega t$:

$$-\omega^2A_p=\omega_0^2(A_{p+1}+A_{p-1}-2A_p).$$

Try $A_p=C\sin\alpha p$. Since $\sin\alpha(p+1)+\sin\alpha(p-1)=2\sin\alpha p\cos\alpha$,

$$-\omega^2 = \omega_0^2(2\cos\alpha-2)\ \Rightarrow\ \omega^2 = 2\omega_0^2(1-\cos\alpha)=4\omega_0^2\sin^2\frac{\alpha}{2},$$ $$\boxed{\alpha = 2\arcsin\frac{\omega}{2\omega_0}}\quad(\text{real because }\omega<2\omega_0).$$

Boundary conditions: $A_0=C\sin0=0$ satisfies $y_0=0$ automatically. $A_{N+1}=C\sin\alpha(N+1)=h$ gives $C=h/\sin\alpha(N+1)$. Therefore

$$\boxed{A_p = h\,\frac{\sin\alpha p}{\sin\alpha(N+1)},\qquad p=1,\dots,N.}$$

A negative $A_p$ means that bead moves opposite to the driver. The amplitudes diverge when $\alpha(N+1)=n\pi$, i.e. $\omega=2\omega_0\sin\frac{n\pi}{2(N+1)}$, the chain's normal-mode frequencies (resonance).
