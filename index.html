Glossary · every term, tap any underlined-dotted word in the guide to see it
amplitudeThe largest displacement from equilibrium, $A$ (m). The motion goes between $+A$ and $-A$.
angular frequencyRadians of phase per second, $\omega$ (rad/s). Since one cycle is $2\pi$ rad, $\omega = 2\pi f$.
chain ruleDerivative of a function of a function: $\frac{d}{dt}f(g(t)) = f'(g)\,g'(t)$. Differentiating $\cos(\omega t)$ brings out the factor $\omega$.
critical damping$\gamma = 2\omega_0$: the fastest return to equilibrium without overshooting.
damping rate$\gamma = b/m$ (s⁻¹): how fast friction removes energy. Energy decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$, amplitude as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
envelopeThe slowly changing curve that bounds an oscillation, e.g. $\pm A_0e^{-\gamma t/2}$ for a damped oscillator.
equilibriumThe position where the net force is zero and the system can rest.
frequencyNumber of complete cycles per second, $f$ (Hz = s⁻¹). $f = 1/T$.
heavy damping$\gamma/2 > \omega_0$: the system creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
light damping$\gamma/2 < \omega_0$: the system oscillates while its amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
log decrement$\ln(A_n/A_{n+1}) = \gamma T/2$: the natural log of the ratio of successive peaks.
mediumWhat the wave travels through (string, air, water). The medium sets the wave speed.
natural frequency$\omega_0$: the angular frequency the system oscillates at with no damping or driving, e.g. $\sqrt{k/m}$.
partial derivativeDerivative with respect to one variable while the others are held fixed. $\partial y/\partial t$: watch one point over time. $\partial y/\partial x$: the slope of the frozen shape.
periodTime for one complete cycle, $T$ (s). $T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega$.
phaseThe full angle inside the cosine, e.g. $kx-\omega t+\phi$. Points with the same phase are at the same stage of the cycle (e.g. all crests).
phase constantThe constant $\phi$ added inside the cosine. It slides the wave so it matches the starting condition.
plane waveA 3D wave whose crests are flat parallel planes, $\psi = A\sin(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)$, travelling along $\mathbf k$.
quadrantWhich quarter of the circle an angle lies in. $\tan$ cannot tell opposite quadrants apart, so check the signs of $\cos\phi$ and $\sin\phi$.
quality factor$Q = \omega_0/\gamma$: how many oscillations (roughly, ×$\pi$) happen before the energy fades. High $Q$ = lightly damped.
radianUnit of angle: $2\pi$ rad = one full cycle = 360°. Arguments of sin and cos in physics are in radians.
restoring forceA force that points back toward equilibrium, e.g. $F = -kx$. It is what makes a system oscillate.
SHMSimple harmonic motion: oscillation in which the restoring force is proportional to displacement, so $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ and $x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$.
sinusoidalShaped like a sine or cosine curve.
small-angle approximationFor small $\theta$ in radians, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and $\cos\theta\approx 1-\theta^2/2$. Turns nearly-SHM systems into exact SHM.
sourceWhat makes the wave (the hand moving the string end, a speaker). The source sets the frequency.
space patternThe shape of the whole wave at one frozen instant, like a photograph. It repeats every wavelength $\lambda$; its rate in space is the wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.
starting conditionWhere the system is and how fast it moves at one moment (usually $t=0$). They fix the constants $A$ and $\phi$.
Taylor expansionWriting a function near a point as a sum of powers: $f(x)\approx f(0)+f'(0)x+\tfrac12f''(0)x^2+\dots$. Keeping the first terms is the basis of every small-oscillation approximation.
time patternHow one point of the medium moves as time passes, watched at a fixed place. It repeats with period $T$; its rate is the frequency $f$ or angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$. Set by the source.
travelling waveA disturbance whose shape moves through a medium at speed $v$, $y = f(x \mp vt)$, while each point of the medium only oscillates in place.
wave equation$\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. Newton's second law for a small piece of the medium; any shape moving at $v$ satisfies it.
wave vector$\mathbf k = (k_1,k_2,k_3)$: points in the direction of travel; its length is the wavenumber $2\pi/\lambda$.
wavelengthDistance between neighbouring crests (or any two points in step), $\lambda$ (m). $\lambda = v/f$.
wavenumberRadians of phase per metre, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ (rad/m). Some books instead use $1/\lambda$; check which.

Waves: the core quantities
concept
$$\text{time pattern} \;\leftrightarrow\; \text{space pattern}$$
Two ways to look at the same wave
▸
Definition

A wave depends on two variables, position $x$ and time $t$. You can look at it two ways. Time pattern: stand at one place and watch that point move up and down as time passes. Space pattern: freeze time and look at the whole shape along $x$, like a photograph.

The two graphs

Time pattern: graph $y$ against $t$ at fixed $x$. It repeats every period $T$; its rates are $f$ and $\omega$. Space pattern: graph $y$ against $x$ at fixed $t$. It repeats every wavelength $\lambda$; its rate is $k$. For $y = A\cos(kx-\omega t)$: at $x = 0$ you get $A\cos\omega t$ (SHM in time); at $t = 0$ you get $A\cos kx$ (a cosine in space).

How they connect

The wave speed links them. In one period $T$ the shape slides forward one wavelength $\lambda$, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda = \omega/k$. Time quantities ($T, f, \omega$) come from the source; space quantities ($\lambda, k$) follow from the source and the medium together.

Repeat length$T$ (s)$\lambda$ (m)

concept
$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$$
Wavenumber — how fast the phase advances along $x$
▸
Definition

The wavenumber $k$ is the number of radians of phase the wave goes through per metre of distance, at a frozen instant. One wavelength is one full cycle, $2\pi$ radians, so $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Units: rad/m (often written m⁻¹).

Intuition

$k$ is the space version of $\omega$. $\omega$ says how quickly one point cycles in time; $k$ says how quickly the shape cycles as you walk along it. Large $k$: crests packed tightly (short wavelength). Small $k$: crests spread out (long wavelength).

Where it appears
  • Inside the wave: $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$. It multiplies $x$ so that $kx$ is an angle in radians.
  • Wave speed: $v = \omega/k$.
  • Wave equation: each $\partial/\partial x$ of $\cos(kx-\omega t)$ brings out a factor $k$, so $\partial^2y/\partial x^2 = -k^2 y$.
  • Reading an equation: $k$ is the number multiplying $x$.
Picture

At $t = 0$, $y = A\cos kx$. Walking from $x$ to $x + \lambda$ increases $kx$ by $k\lambda = 2\pi$: exactly one full cycle, so the shape repeats. That is why $k\lambda = 2\pi$.

Example

PS6 Problem 1: $\lambda = 5.0$ m, so $k = 2\pi/5.0 = 0.4\pi \approx 1.26$ rad/m. The phase advances 1.26 rad for every metre along the string.

Watch out
  • Not the spring constant. Chapter 1 uses $k$ for the spring constant (N/m) in $F = -kx$. Same letter, unrelated quantity. In Chapter 5, $k$ is almost always the wavenumber.
  • Two “wavenumbers”. Spectroscopy uses $\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda$ (cycles per metre). $k = 2\pi\tilde\nu$. State which you mean.
In 3D

The wavenumber becomes the wave vector $\mathbf k = (k_1, k_2, k_3)$: it points in the direction of travel, and its length is $2\pi/\lambda$.

Wavelength — the repeat length of the wave in space
▸
Definition

The distance between two neighbouring points that are at the same stage of the cycle, e.g. crest to crest. Units: m.

Intuition

In the time it takes one point to complete one oscillation (one period), the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength. That is the whole content of $v = f\lambda$.

What sets it

Neither the source nor the medium alone: the source fixes $f$, the medium fixes $v$, and $\lambda = v/f$ follows. When a wave enters a new medium, $f$ stays the same and $\lambda$ changes with $v$.

Typical sizes
  • Visible light: 400–700 nm.
  • Audible sound in air: about 17 mm (20 kHz) to 17 m (20 Hz).
  • AM radio: hundreds of metres.

$$\omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T}$$
Angular frequency — radians of phase per second
▸
Definition

How many radians of phase one point of the oscillation goes through per second. One cycle is $2\pi$ rad, so $\omega = 2\pi f$. Units: rad/s (often written s⁻¹).

Why radians

Calculus works cleanly only in radians: $\frac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t = -\omega\sin\omega t$. With $f$ inside the cosine you would carry $2\pi$ through every derivative. That is why equations use $\omega$ and $k$ rather than $f$ and $1/\lambda$.

Where it appears
  • SHM: $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ (spring) or $\sqrt{g/l}$ (pendulum).
  • Waves: the number multiplying $t$ in $\cos(kx - \omega t)$.
  • Wave speed: $v = \omega/k$.
Three omegas
  • $\omega_0$: natural angular frequency, with no damping (Ch. 2).
  • $\omega$ in a damped oscillator: $\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$, slightly lower.
  • $\omega$ of a driving force: whatever the driver imposes (Ch. 3).

Frequency — cycles per second
▸
Definition

The number of complete cycles per second. Units: hertz, Hz = s⁻¹.

What sets it

For a wave, the source. Shake the end of a string 10 times a second and every point of the string moves 10 times a second. Frequency does not change when the wave passes into a different medium.

Watch out

Problems give $f$ in Hz, but equations need $\omega$ in rad/s: multiply by $2\pi$ before putting it inside a cosine.

Period — the time for one cycle
▸
Definition

The time taken for one complete oscillation. Units: s.

Intuition

Watch one point: the time between two successive highest positions is $T$. During that time the wave shape moves one wavelength.

Watch out

$T$ is also used for tension in $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. Check the context.

Amplitude — the size of the oscillation
▸
Definition

The largest displacement from equilibrium. The motion ranges from $+A$ to $-A$. Units: those of the displacement (m for a string).

Properties
  • Energy goes as $A^2$: $E = \tfrac12kA^2$. Double the amplitude, four times the energy.
  • In SHM the frequency does not depend on $A$.
  • With damping, $A$ shrinks as $A_0e^{-\gamma t/2}$.

energy card · phase

concept
$$\underbrace{kx - \omega t + \phi}_{\text{phase}}$$
Phase and phase constant — where in the cycle you are
▸
Definition

The phase is the whole angle inside the cosine. It says where in its cycle a point is: phase 0 is a crest (for cosine), $\pi$ a trough, $\pi/2$ crossing zero. The phase constant $\phi$ is the fixed part, set by the starting condition.

Intuition

Points with the same phase are doing the same thing at the same moment, e.g. all the crests. Following a point of constant phase is following a crest, and that crest moves at $v$: set $kx - \omega t = \text{const}$, differentiate, $dx/dt = \omega/k$.

Finding φ

Evaluate at the given moment, usually $x = 0, t = 0$, where the phase is just $\phi$: $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. Maximum: $\phi = 0$. Minimum: $\phi = \pi$. Zero and moving up or down: $\phi = \pm\pi/2$ (check with the velocity).

Sine vs cosine

$\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$: a sine is a cosine with phase constant $-\pi/2$. Choosing one or the other only changes $\phi$.

Wave speed — how fast the shape moves
▸
Definition

The speed at which the wave shape (a crest, say) travels through the medium. Units: m/s.

What sets it

The medium. On a string, $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ (tension over mass per length). Light in vacuum, $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Sound in air, about 340 m/s.

Watch out

Wave speed is not the speed of the medium. Each point of a string moves up and down with velocity $\partial y/\partial t$, which changes all the time and is unrelated to $v$. The shape moves along; the string does not.

concept

Radian — the natural unit of angle
▸
Definition

The angle at which the arc of a circle equals its radius. A full turn is $2\pi$ rad. Radians are dimensionless.

Why physics uses it

Only in radians are $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ for small angles and $\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$. Every cycle-counting quantity becomes radian-based: $\omega = 2\pi f$, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial t},\quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}$$
Partial derivative — change one variable, hold the other
▸
Definition

For $y(x,t)$, $\partial y/\partial t$ is the derivative with respect to $t$ treating $x$ as a constant; $\partial y/\partial x$ treats $t$ as a constant.

Meaning for a wave
  • $\partial y/\partial t$: the velocity of one point of the medium (time pattern).
  • $\partial y/\partial x$: the slope of the frozen shape (space pattern).
  • Second derivatives: acceleration of a point, and curvature of the shape. The wave equation says these are proportional.
Example

$y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$: $\partial y/\partial t = -\omega A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, $\partial y/\partial x = kA\cos(kx-\omega t)$. The chain rule brings out the coefficient of the variable you differentiate by.

Damping
concept
$$\gamma = \frac{b}{m}$$
Damping rate
▸
Definition

Damping coefficient $b$ divided by mass. Units: s⁻¹. It is the rate at which energy decays: $E = E_0e^{-\gamma t}$.

Intuition

$b$ says how hard friction pushes per unit velocity; $m$ says how hard the object is to slow; their ratio is how quickly friction actually slows it. Amplitude decays at half this rate, $e^{-\gamma t/2}$, because energy goes as amplitude squared.

Quality factor
▸
Definition

Natural angular frequency divided by damping rate. Dimensionless. Equivalently, energy stored divided by energy lost per radian of oscillation.

Intuition

High $Q$: many oscillations before the motion dies (tuning fork, $Q\sim10^3$). Low $Q$: dies within a few swings. Critical damping is $Q = 1/2$.

Methods · how problems in this chapter are solved
method
Show a system is SHM and find ω
“Show that … executes simple harmonic motion”, “find the period / frequency of small oscillations”
▸
Recognize it

A system sits in stable equilibrium and is displaced a little: a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a floating body, a circuit. Any question about its period or frequency is this method.

Steps
  1. Model. Draw the system displaced by a small amount $x$ (or $\theta$). Name the coordinate and its sign convention.
    Why: you cannot write forces until you know what moves and which way is positive. Draw the system at a small displacement $x$ from equilibrium, not at equilibrium (there the forces balance and nothing shows). Pick the $+x$ direction; every sign that follows comes from this choice.
  2. Forces (or torques). Draw every force at the point where it acts. Find the net restoring force (or torque about the pivot) as a function of $x$.
    Draw each force at the point where it acts: gravity at the centre of mass, tension along the string, the spring force at the attachment. Only components along the motion matter. For a rotating body take torques about the pivot: the unknown pivot force has zero lever arm there, so it drops out.
  3. Newton's second law. Write $m\ddot x = F_{\text{net}}$ (or $I\ddot\theta = \tau_{\text{net}}$).
    Newton's second law turns forces into an equation for the motion: mass × acceleration = net force, with acceleration $\ddot x = d^2x/dt^2$. For rotation: moment of inertia × angular acceleration = net torque. The result is a differential equation for $x(t)$.
  4. Approximate. If the force is not yet linear in $x$, use the small-displacement approximation: $\sin\theta\approx\theta$, or a Taylor expansion of $U(x)$.
    SHM requires a force exactly proportional to $x$. Real forces often are not (the pendulum has $mg\sin\theta$). For small displacements keep only the first term: $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ with $\theta$ in radians. This is what “small oscillations” means.
  5. Match the form. Rearrange to $\ddot x = -(\text{constant})\,x$. The constant is $\omega^2$; take its square root.
    Divide both sides by $m$ (or $I$) so $\ddot x$ stands alone. Compare with $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$: whatever multiplies $-x$ is $\omega^2$. Example: $\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\theta$ gives $\omega^2 = g/l$; take the square root, $\omega = \sqrt{g/l}$.
  6. Finish. $T = 2\pi/\omega$, $f = 1/T$. Check units (s⁻¹) and that $\omega$ does not depend on amplitude.
    $\omega$ is in rad/s. One cycle is $2\pi$ rad, so the period is $T = 2\pi/\omega$ and $f = 1/T$. $\omega$ should contain only physical constants ($k, m, g, l$). If the amplitude appears, an approximation was missed.

Common mistakes
  • Leaving a minus sign out: the force must point back toward equilibrium, or there is no oscillation.
  • Using $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ in degrees; it holds only in radians.
  • Mixing $\omega$ (rad/s) with $f$ (Hz): $\omega = 2\pi f$.
Go deeper

Why this always works: near any stable minimum, the potential is approximately a parabola (1.26–1.27), so the restoring force is approximately linear. That is why “small oscillations” and “SHM” go together. The energy route reaches the same answer: write $E = \tfrac12\alpha\dot x^2 + \tfrac12\beta x^2$ and read off $\omega^2 = \beta/\alpha$ (1.43a–c). Use it when forces are awkward but energies are easy (rolling objects, fluids).

method
Fit A and φ to the starting conditions
“At t = 0 the mass is released from …”, “given x(0) and v(0), find the motion”
▸
Recognize it

You already know $\omega$ and are told where the system starts and how fast it moves at one moment.

Steps
  1. Write $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ and $v = -\omega A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$.
    You need both position and velocity because the starting data give both. $v = dx/dt$; differentiating $\cos$ gives $-\sin$, and the chain rule brings out $\omega$.
  2. Substitute $t=0$: $x_0 = A\cos\phi$ and $v_0 = -\omega A\sin\phi$.
    Setting $t = 0$ in both equations gives two equations for the two unknowns $A$ and $\phi$.
  3. Square and add (since $\cos^2+\sin^2=1$): $A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}$.
    From step 2: $A\cos\phi = x_0$ and $A\sin\phi = -v_0/\omega$. Square each and add: $A^2(\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi) = x_0^2 + v_0^2/\omega^2$. Since $\cos^2+\sin^2 = 1$, take the square root of both sides.
  4. Divide the second by the first: $\tan\phi = -v_0/(\omega x_0)$. Choose the quadrant from the signs of $x_0$ and $v_0$.
    Divide the sine equation by the cosine equation; $A$ cancels. $\tan$ repeats every $\pi$, so a calculator can return the wrong angle: choose $\phi$ so that $\cos\phi$ has the sign of $x_0$ and $\sin\phi$ the sign of $-v_0$.

Common mistakes
  • Taking $\phi$ from $\tan^{-1}$ without checking the quadrant.
  • Released from rest at $x_0$ means $v_0 = 0$, so $A = x_0$, $\phi = 0$: no calculation needed.

Two constants appear because the SHM equation is second order: it fixes the acceleration, so the position and velocity at one instant must be supplied from outside. Every second-order equation in this course (damped, driven, coupled) needs exactly this step.

1.2 · A Mass on a Spring
1.2
$F = -kx$
Hooke's law — the restoring force
▸
Parameters
  • $F$ restoring force on the mass (N)
  • $k$ spring constant — force per unit displacement (N m⁻¹)
  • $x$ displacement from equilibrium (m)
Origin

The minus sign is the entire physical content: the force always points back toward equilibrium, regardless of which side $x$ is displaced to. This — force proportional to displacement, directed opposite to it — is the defining property of every simple harmonic oscillator, not just springs.

1.6
$$\dfrac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\omega^2 x$$
The equation of SHM — boxed in the text; the master equation of the chapter
▸
Parameters
  • $\omega$ angular frequency of oscillation (rad s⁻¹)
  • $x$ displacement, as a function of time
Derivation
1. Newton's second law: $F = ma = -kx$.
2. Acceleration is the second derivative of displacement: $a = \mathrm d^2x/\mathrm dt^2$.
3. Substitute: $m\,\mathrm d^2x/\mathrm dt^2 = -kx$, then divide by $m$ and write the constant $k/m$ as $\omega^2$.

Every equation in this chapter is either deriving this equation for a new physical system, or solving it. If you can recognize a system reduces to this form, you already know its entire behavior.

1.7
$$\omega^2 = \dfrac{k}{m}$$
Angular frequency of a mass on a spring
▸
Parameters
  • $k$ spring constant (N m⁻¹)
  • $m$ mass (kg)

Key property: $\omega$ depends only on $k$ and $m$ — the physical properties of the oscillator — never on amplitude or how the motion was started. This is why a pendulum clock keeps accurate time regardless of how far you initially pull it.

1.14
$$x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$$
General solution for SHM — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $A$ amplitude — maximum displacement (m)
  • $\omega$ angular frequency, fixed by the oscillator's physical properties
  • $\phi$ phase angle (rad) — set by initial conditions
Why two constants

Equation 1.6 is a second-order differential equation, so its general solution always has exactly two free constants. Here they're $A$ and $\phi$; equivalently it can be written $x = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$ with $a = A\cos\phi$, $b=-A\sin\phi$ (Eq. 1.15–1.17) — same solution, different-looking constants.

1.12–1.13
$$v = -\omega A\sin\omega t, \qquad a = -\omega^2 A\cos\omega t = -\omega^2 x$$
Velocity and acceleration from the displacement
▸
Origin

Straightforward differentiation of $x=A\cos\omega t$ with respect to time, once and twice. Worth noticing directly from the graphs: velocity is zero exactly when displacement is at its extremes (the turning points), and maximum exactly when displacement passes through zero — a quarter-period phase shift between the two.

1.2.5 · Energy of a Simple Harmonic Oscillator
1.19
$$E = K + U = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$$
Total mechanical energy — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $K$ kinetic energy, $\tfrac12mv^2$
  • $U$ potential energy stored in the spring, $\tfrac12kx^2$ (from integrating $F=-kx$, Eq. 1.18)
Derivation sketch

Start from $m\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt = -kx$, multiply through by $\mathrm dx = v\,\mathrm dt$ to get $mv\,\mathrm dv = -kx\,\mathrm dx$, then integrate both sides — the left becomes $\tfrac12mv^2$, the right $-\tfrac12kx^2$, and together they sum to a constant: the total energy $E$.

1.23
$$E = \tfrac12 kA^2$$
Total energy in terms of amplitude alone
▸
Why this matters

Substituting the general solution into $K+U$, the time-dependent $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ terms combine to exactly $1$ (Eq. 1.21–1.23), leaving a constant — confirming energy conservation directly, and showing energy scales with the square of the amplitude. Double the amplitude, quadruple the energy.

1.2.6 · Why Small Oscillations Are Always SHM
1.26–1.27
$$U(x) \approx \frac{x^2}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dx^2}\right)_{x=0}, \qquad F = -x\left(\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dx^2}\right)_{x=0}$$
Taylor expansion near equilibrium — the universal justification for SHM
▸
Reasoning

Any smooth potential well $U(x)$ can be Taylor-expanded about its minimum (Eq. 1.25). The constant term is irrelevant (potential energy has no absolute zero), and the first-derivative term vanishes exactly because $x=0$ is a minimum. What survives, to leading order, is a term proportional to $x^2$ — i.e. a parabolic well, which always produces a linear restoring force. This is the actual reason SHM is universal: any system in stable equilibrium behaves like a harmonic oscillator for sufficiently small displacements, whether it's a pendulum, a diatomic molecule, or a marble in a bowl.

1.3 · The Pendulum
1.29, 1.31
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{g}{l}\sin\theta \;\; \xrightarrow{\text{small }\theta} \;\; \frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{g}{l}\theta$$
Simple pendulum equation of motion, exact then small-angle
▸
Parameters
  • $\theta$ angular displacement from vertical (rad)
  • $l$ length of the pendulum (m)
  • $g$ acceleration due to gravity
Why $\sin\theta$ needs replacing

The exact equation has $\sin\theta$, not $\theta$, so it isn't literally SHM. Expanding $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/3! + \cdots$ (Eq. 1.30) and keeping only the first term — valid once $\theta$ is small enough that higher powers are negligible — recovers the SHM form exactly.

Result: $\omega = \sqrt{g/l}$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$ (Eq. 1.32). Note $\omega$ depends on $g$ and $l$ only — not mass, not amplitude.

1.43a–c
$$E = \tfrac12\alpha v^2 + \tfrac12\beta x^2$$
The universal energy signature of SHM
▸
Parameters
  • $\alpha$ the "inertia" constant (mass for a spring, $\alpha=m$; moment of inertia for a rotating system)
  • $\beta$ the "stiffness" constant (spring constant $k$; or $mg/l$ for a pendulum)
Why this generalizes everything

Both the mass-on-spring energy ($\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$) and the pendulum energy ($\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12\frac{mg}{l}x^2$) are special cases of this one form. Differentiating it with respect to time always reproduces the SHM equation with $\omega=\sqrt{\beta/\alpha}$ — meaning you never need to redo this derivation for a new system: just identify its $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and read off $\omega$ directly.

1.4 · Oscillations in Electrical Circuits
1.54
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2q}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{1}{LC}q$$
The LC circuit — SHM in an electrical system
▸
Parameters
  • $q$ charge on the capacitor (C)
  • $L$ inductance (H)
  • $C$ capacitance (F)
Origin

Kirchhoff's voltage law, $V_C + V_L = 0$, with $V_C = q/C$ and $V_L = L\,\mathrm dI/\mathrm dt = L\,\mathrm d^2q/\mathrm dt^2$ (since $I=\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt$). Same mathematical form as Eq. 1.6, with $\omega = \sqrt{1/LC}$.

The correspondence (Eq. 1.57–1.58): $q \leftrightarrow x$, $L \leftrightarrow m$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$. Every mechanical SHM result translates directly — this is why electrical circuits can be built as analogue simulations of mechanical systems.

Chapter 2 adds one term to the master equation: a velocity-proportional damping force. Everything here is organized around a single comparison — $\gamma^2/4$ (the damping) versus $\omega_0^2$ (the spring-like restoring term) — and which one wins determines whether the system oscillates at all.
Methods · how problems in this chapter are solved
method
Write the damped equation and classify the damping
“A damping force −bv acts …”, “is the motion oscillatory?”, “what value of b gives critical damping?”
▸
Recognize it

SHM plus a force proportional to velocity. The first question is always which regime it is in.

Steps
  1. Newton's second law with the damping force: $m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x$.
    The damping force $-b\dot x$ always opposes the velocity; $b$ (kg/s) measures its strength. Add it to the spring force on the right side of Newton's second law.
  2. Divide both sides by $m$ to reach standard form $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$; read off $\gamma = b/m$ and $\omega_0^2 = k/m$.
    Dividing every term by $m$ leaves $\ddot x$ alone, and the coefficients get names: $\gamma = b/m$ (damping rate, s⁻¹) and $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ (natural angular frequency squared). In this standard form every damped system (spring, pendulum, circuit) uses the same results.
  3. Compare $\gamma/2$ with $\omega_0$: $\gamma/2 < \omega_0$ light (oscillates), $=\omega_0$ critical, $>\omega_0$ heavy (no oscillation).
    $\gamma/2$ says how fast the motion dies; $\omega_0$ says how fast it wants to swing. Dying slower than swinging: it swings several times while fading (light). Equal: it returns as fast as possible with no overshoot (critical). Dying faster: it creeps back without swinging (heavy).
  4. For light damping, the oscillation frequency is $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$ and the amplitude falls as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
    Damping slows the swing slightly: $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4$. The solution is a cosine (the swinging) multiplied by $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ (the shrinking envelope).
Uses

2.2 damping force · 2.5 standard form · 2.7–2.8 light · 2.16 critical · 2.13 heavy

Common mistakes
  • Amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$, not $e^{-\gamma t}$. The energy decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$.
  • Critical damping is $\gamma = 2\omega_0$, not $\gamma = \omega_0$.
Go deeper

Where the regimes come from: trying $x = e^{pt}$ turns the equation into $p^2 + \gamma p + \omega_0^2 = 0$, so $p = -\gamma/2 \pm\sqrt{\gamma^2/4 - \omega_0^2}$. A negative quantity under the root gives oscillation; zero gives the repeated root (critical); positive gives two decaying exponentials (heavy). The same quadratic decides everything.

method
Get γ, τ or Q from decay data
“The amplitude falls to half in …”, “after n oscillations the energy is …”, “find the Q of …”
▸
Recognize it

You are given how fast something dies away, and asked for a damping parameter (or the reverse).

Steps
  1. Decide whether the data are about amplitude ($\propto e^{-\gamma t/2}$) or energy ($\propto e^{-\gamma t}$).
    Energy goes as amplitude squared ($E = \tfrac12 kA^2$). Squaring $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ gives $e^{-\gamma t}$. So “the amplitude halves” and “the energy halves” give values of $\gamma$ that differ by a factor of 2.
  2. Write the ratio: e.g. $A(t)/A_0 = e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
    A ratio removes the unknown starting amplitude: $A(t)/A_0 = e^{-\gamma t/2}$. Substitute the given ratio (for example $\tfrac12$) and the time.
  3. Take the natural log of both sides and solve for $\gamma$: divide by $-t/2$.
    $\ln$ undoes $e$: $\ln(A/A_0) = -\gamma t/2$. Multiply both sides by $-2/t$: $\gamma = (2/t)\ln(A_0/A)$.
  4. Then $\tau = 1/\gamma$ and $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$. For successive peaks one period apart use the log decrement $\ln(A_n/A_{n+1}) = \gamma T/2$.
    $\tau = 1/\gamma$ is the time for the energy to fall to $1/e \approx 37\%$. $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$ compares swinging speed with dying speed: large $Q$ means many oscillations before the motion fades. Successive peaks one period $T$ apart have ratio $e^{\gamma T/2}$.
Uses

2.9 log decrement · 2.18–2.19 energy decay · 2.21–2.23 Q

Common mistakes
  • Mixing amplitude and energy decay (factor of 2).
  • Using $\log_{10}$ instead of $\ln$.
Go deeper

Physical meaning of $Q$: about $2\pi$ times the energy stored divided by the energy lost per cycle, or roughly the number of oscillations (times $\pi$) before the energy falls to $1/e$. A tuning fork has $Q$ in the thousands; a car suspension is near critical.

2.2 · The Equation of Motion with Damping
2.2
$$F_d = -bv$$
Damping force, proportional to velocity
▸
Parameters
  • $b$ damping constant — force per unit velocity (N s m⁻¹)
  • $v$ velocity of the mass

This linear-in-$v$ form is an approximation (valid for velocities that aren't too large), but it's the one that keeps the equation of motion linear and solvable — a damping force proportional to $v^2$ would be far harder to handle analytically.

2.5
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2} + \gamma\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$$
The damped harmonic oscillator equation — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $\omega_0^2$ $=k/m$ — the natural frequency: what the system would oscillate at with zero damping
  • $\gamma$ $=b/m$ — the damping parameter (Eq. 2.4)
Origin

Newton's second law with both forces present: $ma = -kx - bv$ (Eq. 2.3a), rearranged and divided through by $m$.

Everything else in this chapter is one of three cases determined by comparing $\gamma^2/4$ to $\omega_0^2$.

2.7–2.8
$$x = A_0\exp(-\gamma t/2)\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4$$
Light damping — boxed; oscillation with a decaying envelope
▸
Condition

$\gamma^2/4 < \omega_0^2$ — damping is weak enough that $\omega$ stays real, so the system still oscillates, just at a slightly lower frequency than $\omega_0$, with an amplitude that decays as $\exp(-\gamma t/2)$.

Reading the pieces
  • $A_0$ initial amplitude
  • $\exp(-\gamma t/2)$ the decaying envelope — not the oscillation itself, just the ceiling it decays within
  • $\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ the oscillation inside that envelope, at the (slightly reduced) angular frequency $\omega$
2.9
$$\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{\gamma T}{2}\right), \qquad \ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right) = \frac{\gamma T}{2}$$
Logarithmic decrement — ratio of successive amplitude peaks
▸
Parameters
  • $A_n$ amplitude of the $n$-th peak; $A_{n+1}$ the next one, one period $T$ later
Use

Since successive peaks always shrink by the same fractional amount, measuring any two consecutive peaks from a graph gives you $\gamma$ directly — a standard way to extract the damping parameter from experimental data.

2.13
$$x = A\exp[(-\gamma/2+\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2})t] + B\exp[(-\gamma/2-\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2})t]$$
Heavy (over) damping — no oscillation at all
▸
Condition

$\gamma^2/4 > \omega_0^2$ — damping dominates so strongly that the system just relaxes back to equilibrium, sluggishly, with two different exponential decay rates and no crossing back and forth.

Why no oscillation

Both exponents are real and negative (since $\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2} < \gamma/2$ always), so both terms are pure exponential decays — nothing in the solution oscillates.

2.16
$$x = A\exp(-\gamma t/2) + Bt\exp(-\gamma t/2)$$
Critical damping — fastest return to equilibrium, no overshoot
▸
Condition

$\gamma^2/4 = \omega_0^2$ exactly — the boundary case between light and heavy damping.

Why this case is special

Of all three damping regimes, this is the one that returns the system to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without ever oscillating past it. That's why it's the design target for shock absorbers, door closers, and analogue meter needles — anything that needs to settle quickly without bouncing.

2.3 · Rate of Energy Loss
2.18–2.19
$$E(t) = E_0\exp(-\gamma t) = E_0\exp(-t/\tau)$$
Exponential energy decay — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $E_0$ total energy at $t=0$
  • $\tau = 1/\gamma$ the decay time (or time constant) — the time for energy to fall by a factor of $e$
Derivation sketch

Substitute the light-damping solution into $E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$; for very light damping the oscillating $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ terms again combine to $1$ (as in Ch. 1), leaving a clean exponential decay envelope.

2.21, 2.23
$$Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma} = \frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy dissipated per radian}}$$
The quality factor Q — boxed in the text
▸
Why it exists

$Q$ is a dimensionless "figure of merit" that works for any kind of oscillator — mechanical, electrical, or otherwise — regardless of what units its damping is measured in. Comparing the decay time $\tau$ (how long energy takes to dissipate) to the period $T$ (how long one oscillation takes) is the essential idea: many oscillations before dying out means high $Q$.

Practical readings
  • high $Q$ many oscillations before the energy is significantly reduced (quartz crystal: $Q\sim10^6$)
  • low $Q$ only a few oscillations, or none (paperweight on a rubber band: $Q\sim10$)
2.24–2.25
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2} + \frac{\omega_0}{Q}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad \omega = \omega_0(1-1/4Q^2)^{1/2}$$
The damped oscillator equation, rewritten in terms of Q
▸
Why rewrite it this way

Same physics as Eq. 2.5, with $\gamma$ replaced by $\omega_0/Q$ — useful because $Q$ is often the quantity actually measured or quoted for a real oscillator. Note that even a fairly low $Q=5$ only shifts $\omega$ from $\omega_0$ by about 0.5%, confirming that $\omega\approx\omega_0$ is an excellent approximation whenever damping is light.

2.4 · Damped Electrical Oscillations
2.26
$$L\frac{\mathrm d^2q}{\mathrm dt^2} + R\frac{\mathrm dq}{\mathrm dt} + \frac{q}{C} = 0$$
The damped LCR circuit — the electrical analog of Eq. 2.3b
▸
The correspondence
  • $q\leftrightarrow x$, $L\leftrightarrow m$, $R\leftrightarrow b$, $1/C\leftrightarrow k$

Since the differential equations are identical in form, every mechanical damping result carries over directly: light/heavy/critical damping, energy decay, and $Q$ all apply unchanged with these substitutions.

2.29–2.30
$$\omega^2 = \frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{4L^2}, \qquad Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$$
Oscillation frequency and Q of a damped LCR circuit
▸
Reading it

Direct substitution of the mechanical-to-electrical correspondence above into Eq. 2.6 and Eq. 2.21. Larger resistance $R$ means more damping (smaller $Q$), exactly as a larger $b$ would mechanically.

Chapter 5 moves from one oscillator to a whole medium. A travelling wave is a disturbance whose shape moves at speed $v$ while each point of the medium only oscillates in place. Almost every equation below either describes that moving shape, links its space pattern ($\lambda$, $k$) to its time pattern ($T$, $f$, $\omega$) through $v$, or says which functions obey the wave equation. Methods · how problems in this chapter are solved
method
Write the equation of a travelling wave
“Obtain an equation describing the wave” — PS6 Problem 1
▸
Recognize it

You are given an amplitude, a frequency (or period), a speed (or wavelength), a direction, and something about the start.

Steps
  1. Direction. Toward $+x$: use $kx - \omega t$. Toward $-x$: $kx + \omega t$.
    A crest sits where the phase is zero: $kx - \omega t = 0$, so $x = (\omega/k)t$. As $t$ grows, $x$ grows: the crest moves toward $+x$. With $kx + \omega t$, $x = -(\omega/k)t$ and the crest moves toward $-x$.
  2. Time pattern. $\omega = 2\pi f$ (or $2\pi/T$).
    The time pattern is what one point of the medium does: stand at one place and watch it go up and down. It repeats $f$ times a second, the frequency of the source. Inside cos the time term must be in radians, and one cycle is $2\pi$ rad, so multiply: $\omega = 2\pi f$.
  3. Space pattern. $\lambda = v/f$, then $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Check $\omega/k = v$.
    The space pattern is the shape of the whole wave at one frozen instant, like a photograph: a cosine curve that repeats every wavelength $\lambda$. In one period the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda$; divide by $f$ to get $\lambda$. Inside cos the space term must be in radians: $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Then $\omega/k$ must equal $v$.
  4. Phase. Substitute the starting condition into $A\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi)$ to fix $\phi$ (maximum at the start → cosine, $\phi=0$).
    $\phi$ slides the wave so it matches the stated start. At $x = 0,\ t = 0$ the argument of cos is just $\phi$, so $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. Set this equal to the given value and solve. Maximum upward means $\cos\phi = 1$, so $\phi = 0$.
  5. Check. Set $x=0$: you should recover the source's SHM.
    At $x = 0$ the wave formula should reduce to the motion of the source (the end of the string): SHM with the given amplitude, frequency and starting point. If it does not, a sign or a factor of $2\pi$ is wrong.
Uses

sine sinusoidal wave · defs definitions · speed speed · shape direction rule

Common mistakes
  • Writing $f$ where $\omega$ belongs: the argument of cos must be in radians.
  • Getting the direction backwards: $x - vt$ moves toward $+x$.
Go deeper

Route B, from the source. The end at $x=0$ does SHM, $y(0,t) = A\cos\omega t$. A point at distance $x$ does the same thing a time $x/v$ later, so $y(x,t) = y(0, t - x/v) = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. This shows why a wave has the form it does: a delay that grows with distance turns oscillation in time into a pattern in space.

method
Show a function solves the wave equation
“Show that the following are solutions to the wave equation” — PS6 Problem 2
▸
Recognize it

You are handed $y(x,t)$ (or $\psi(x,y,z,t)$) and asked to verify it, or to find the condition that makes it work.

Steps
  1. Compute $\partial y/\partial t$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2$ (hold $x$ fixed; chain rule brings out $\omega$).
    Hold $x$ fixed and differentiate in $t$. For $y = A\sin(kx-\omega t)$ the inside has $t$-derivative $-\omega$, so $\partial y/\partial t = -\omega A\cos(kx-\omega t)$. Again: $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx-\omega t) = -\omega^2 y$.
  2. Compute $\partial y/\partial x$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$ (hold $t$ fixed; chain rule brings out $k$).
    Hold $t$ fixed and differentiate in $x$. The inside has $x$-derivative $k$: $\partial y/\partial x = kA\cos(\dots)$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = -k^2 y$.
  3. Substitute both into $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$.
    Put both results into the wave equation: $-\omega^2 y = v^2(-k^2 y)$.
  4. Cancel the common function; what is left is the condition, usually $\omega^2 = v^2k^2$, i.e. $\omega/k = v$.
    Divide both sides by $-y$: $\omega^2 = v^2k^2$, so $\omega/k = v$. The function is a solution exactly when its own speed $\omega/k$ equals the $v$ in the equation.
Uses

1d wave equation · complex complex form · 3d 3D

Common mistakes
  • Losing the chain-rule factor ($\omega$ or $k$) on each derivative.
  • Shortcut worth stating: if $y$ depends on $x$ and $t$ only through $x \pm vt$, it is a solution automatically.
Go deeper

Why the condition is always $\omega/k = v$: the wave equation contains $v$ and nothing else, so it can only accept shapes that move at $v$. A sinusoid moves at $\omega/k$, so it qualifies exactly when the two speeds agree. In 3D the same step gives $k_1^2+k_2^2+k_3^2 = \omega^2/v^2$: the speed is fixed, the direction is free.

method
Convert between λ, f, k and v
“Calculate the frequency of a wave of wavelength …” — PS6 Problem 3
▸
Recognize it

One of $\lambda$, $f$, $k$ is given; the medium tells you $v$.

Steps
  1. Identify the medium. Light, radio, X-rays: $v = c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Sound in air: $v\approx 340$ m/s. String: $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$.
    The speed belongs to what the wave travels through (the medium), not to the source. EM waves in vacuum or air: $c$. Sound in air: about 340 m/s.
  2. Convert to SI. nm $=10^{-9}$ m, mm $=10^{-3}$ m, kHz $=10^3$ Hz.
    The formulas assume metres, seconds and hertz. Example: $500\ \text{nm} = 500\times10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$.
  3. Apply $v = f\lambda$: divide by whichever quantity is known.
    $v = f\lambda$. For $f$, divide both sides by $\lambda$: $f = v/\lambda$. For $\lambda$, divide by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.
  4. Sanity check the size against what you know (visible light ~$10^{14}$ Hz; audible sound has wavelengths from cm to m).
    Known sizes catch power-of-ten errors: visible light is about $4$–$8\times10^{14}$ Hz; a 20 Hz sound has $\lambda \approx 17$ m; a 15 kHz sound about 2 cm.
Uses

Common mistakes
  • Using $c$ for sound.
  • Not stating which “wavenumber” ($1/\lambda$ or $2\pi/\lambda$) you assumed.
Go deeper

The frequency belongs to the source and does not change when a wave enters a new medium; the speed belongs to the medium; the wavelength adjusts so that $v = f\lambda$ still holds.

method
Read the wave off a given equation

    Match term by term with $A\sin(kx-\omega t+\phi)$: the number in front is $A$, the coefficient of $x$ is $k$, the coefficient of $t$ is $\omega$.
  2. $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, $f = \omega/2\pi$, $T = 1/f$, $v = \omega/k$.

Describing a travelling wave
shape
$$y = f(x - vt) \quad\text{or}\quad y = f(x + vt)$$
Any shape moving without change — the definition of a travelling wave
▸
Parameters
  • $y$ displacement of the medium at position $x$, time $t$ (m)
  • $f$ any function: the shape of the wave
  • $v$ wave speed (m s⁻¹)
Origin

At $t=0$ the shape is $f(x)$. At time $t$, the same value of $f$ occurs where $x - vt$ has the same value, i.e. at $x$ shifted by $vt$. So the whole shape slides a distance $vt$ to the right. Direction rule: $x - vt$ → moves toward $+x$; $x + vt$ → moves toward $-x$.

sine
$$y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$$
The sinusoidal (harmonic) travelling wave — the standard form used in problems
▸
Parameters
  • $A$ amplitude, the maximum displacement (m)
  • $k$ wavenumber: radians of phase per metre (rad m⁻¹)
  • $\omega$ angular frequency: radians of phase per second (rad s⁻¹)
  • $\phi$ phase constant (rad), set by the starting condition
Reading it

Factor out $k$: $kx-\omega t = k(x - \tfrac{\omega}{k}t)$, so this is $f(x-vt)$ with $v=\omega/k$, moving toward $+x$. Use $kx+\omega t$ for $-x$. Fix one $x$ and you get SHM in time (Ch. 1); fix one $t$ and you get a sine pattern in space. Sine or cosine is a choice: $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$, so they differ only by $\phi$.

Finding φ

Substitute the given condition, usually at $x=0,\ t=0$: $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. Maximum upward displacement gives $\cos\phi=1$, $\phi=0$; zero displacement moving up or down gives $\phi=\pm\pi/2$ (check the sign with $\partial y/\partial t$).

defs
$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}$$
Converting between the space pattern and the time pattern
▸
Parameters
  • $\lambda$ wavelength: distance between neighbouring crests (m)
  • $f$ frequency: oscillations per second (Hz = s⁻¹)
  • $T$ period: time for one oscillation (s)
Origin

One full cycle is $2\pi$ radians. Moving one wavelength along $x$ advances the phase by $2\pi$, so $k\lambda = 2\pi$. Waiting one period advances it by $2\pi$, so $\omega T = 2\pi$, and with $T=1/f$, $\omega = 2\pi f$.

speed
$$v = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k}$$
Wave speed — links every space quantity to every time quantity
▸
Origin

In one period $T$ each crest moves forward exactly one wavelength, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda$. Substituting $f=\omega/2\pi$ and $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ gives $v=\omega/k$.

Use

Any two of $v$, $f$ (or $\omega$), $\lambda$ (or $k$) give the third. The speed is set by the medium; the frequency is set by the source; the wavelength follows from both.

wavenum
$$\tilde\nu = \frac{1}{\lambda} \quad\text{vs.}\quad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$$
Two quantities both called “wavenumber” — check which one a problem means
▸
Parameters
  • $\tilde\nu$ spectroscopic wavenumber: wavelengths per metre (m⁻¹)
  • $k$ angular wavenumber: radians per metre (rad m⁻¹)
Caution

They differ by $2\pi$. A value given in plain m⁻¹ can be either; state which you assume in a write-up. Then $f = v\tilde\nu$ or $f = v k/2\pi$.

The wave equation
1D
$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$$
The one-dimensional wave equation — the master equation of the chapter
▸
Parameters
  • $\partial/\partial t$ partial derivative: differentiate in $t$ holding $x$ fixed
  • $\partial/\partial x$ partial derivative: differentiate in $x$ holding $t$ fixed
  • $v$ wave speed
Reading it

Left side: the acceleration of one point of the medium. Right side: the curvature of the shape at that point. Where the string curves most, it accelerates most. This is Newton's second law applied to a small piece of the medium, just as $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ was for one mass.

Why f(x ± vt) solves it

Let $u = x - vt$. Chain rule: $\partial y/\partial x = f'(u)$ and $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = f''(u)$; $\partial y/\partial t = -v f'(u)$ and $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = v^2 f''(u)$. So $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$ for any shape $f$. Same for $x+vt$.

Checking a candidate

1. Take $\partial^2y/\partial t^2$. 2. Take $\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. 3. Substitute both and see what condition makes the two sides equal (usually $\omega/k = v$). A sum of solutions with the same $v$ is also a solution (the equation is linear).

complex
$$y = A\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$$
Complex form — same wave, easier derivatives
▸
Reading it

The physical wave is the real part, $A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. Each $\partial/\partial t$ brings down $i\omega$; each $\partial/\partial x$ brings down $-ik$. So $\partial^2/\partial t^2 \to -\omega^2$ and $\partial^2/\partial x^2 \to -k^2$. Direction: $\omega t - kx$ moves toward $+x$; $\omega t + kx$ toward $-x$.

3D
$$\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial z^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial t^2}$$
Three-dimensional wave equation
▸
Parameters
  • $\psi$ the disturbance at point $(x,y,z)$ and time $t$
  • $k_1,k_2,k_3$ components of the wave vector $\mathbf k$; it points along the direction of travel
Plane waves

$\psi = A\sin(k_1x+k_2y+k_3z-\omega t)$ is a wave whose crests are flat planes, travelling along $\mathbf k$. Its wavelength is $\lambda = 2\pi/|\mathbf k|$ with $|\mathbf k| = \sqrt{k_1^2+k_2^2+k_3^2}$. (PS6 Problem 2(b) asks you to derive the relation between $k_1,k_2,k_3,\omega,v$ from the equation above.)

Wave speeds in particular media
string
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$
Transverse wave on a stretched string
▸
Parameters
  • $T$ tension in the string (N) — not the period
  • $\mu$ mass per unit length (kg m⁻¹)
Reading it

Same pattern as $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$: a restoring quantity over an inertia quantity. Tighter string → faster wave; heavier string → slower wave.

EM
$$c = f\lambda, \qquad c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$
Electromagnetic waves (radio, light, X-rays) in vacuum
▸
Use

All EM waves travel at $c$ in vacuum, so $f = c/\lambda$. Radio, visible light and X-rays differ only in $\lambda$ and $f$.

sound
$$v_{\text{sound}} \approx 340\ \text{m s}^{-1}\ \text{(air)}$$
Sound in air — a longitudinal wave
▸
Use

Use $v = f\lambda$ with this $v$, not $c$. Sound is a pressure wave in a medium; it does not travel at the speed of light. The value is given in problems when needed (King 5-6 uses 340 m s⁻¹).

Worked problems
PS6 · 1
$$y = 0.15\cos(0.4\pi x - 20\pi t)\ \text{m}$$
King 5-2 — writing the equation of a travelling wave
▸
Given
  • $A$ 0.15 m
  • $f$ 10 Hz
  • $v$ 50 m s⁻¹, toward $+x$
  • start $y(0,0)=+A$ (maximum upward at $x=0$, $t=0$)
Steps
  1. Wave moves toward $+x$, so use $y = A\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi)$.
  2. $\omega = 2\pi f$ (one cycle is $2\pi$ rad): $\omega = 20\pi$ rad s⁻¹.
  3. $v = f\lambda$; divide by $f$: $\lambda = 50/10 = 5.0$ m.
  4. $k = 2\pi/\lambda = 0.4\pi$ rad m⁻¹. Check: $\omega/k = 50$ m s⁻¹ ✓.
  5. Set $x=0,t=0$: $A\cos\phi = A$, so $\phi=0$.
  6. Substitute: $y = 0.15\cos(0.4\pi x - 20\pi t)$ m. At $x=0$ this is SHM, 10 Hz, max at $t=0$ ✓.
Route B — from the source
  1. The end does SHM starting at its maximum, so $y(0,t) = 0.15\cos(20\pi t)$ (cosine because $\cos 0 = 1$ is its largest value).
  2. A point at distance $x$ copies the end a time $x/v$ later: $y(x,t) = y(0,\,t - x/v)$.
  3. Replace $t$ by $t - x/50$: $y = 0.15\cos(20\pi t - 0.4\pi x)$, the same wave.
