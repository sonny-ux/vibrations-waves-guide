The whole picture: what waves physics is made of
Systems have properties; parts exert forces on each other; laws turn those forces into motion; a motion is described by a few numbers.
▸
The kinds of thing
  • Systems: the things that exist and move. A mass on a spring, a pendulum, a stretched string, a column of air in a pipe, an electron in a box, an LC circuit. A system is something; everything else on this list is said of a system.
  • Properties: what a system has, fixed by what it is made of and how it is built. Mass $m$, length $L$, mass per unit length $\mu$, spring constant $k$, density $\rho$, bulk modulus $B$. Properties do not change while the system moves.
  • Set-up conditions: what is imposed on a system from outside and held fixed. The tension $T$ a peg maintains, a clamped end, an open end of a pipe, a finger touching a string. They are not properties of the material: the same string can be put under any tension.
  • Interactions: what one thing exerts on another. A force is always exerted by something on something: the spring exerts $-kx$ on the mass; each piece of string exerts a tension force on its neighbour; the air exerts a drag force. Nothing "has" a force the way it has a mass.
  • Laws: the rules that turn interactions into motion. Newton's second law, $F = ma$, applied to a mass or to one small piece of a medium, gives an equation of motion: $\ddot x = -\omega_0^2 x$ for the spring, the wave equation $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$ for the string. A law governs; a system obeys it.
  • Motions: what a system does; the solutions of its equation of motion. Simple harmonic motion, damped oscillation, a travelling wave, a standing wave, a normal mode, and sums of these.
  • Descriptors: the numbers that describe a motion. Amplitude $A$, frequency $f$, period $T$, angular frequency $\omega$, wavelength $\lambda$, wavenumber $k$, phase $\phi$, wave speed $v$. They belong to the motion, not to the system: the same string carries waves of many frequencies.
  • Moves: what we do to get answers. Superpose two motions, match a wave across a boundary, break a motion into normal modes, approximate (small angles, $\sqrt{1+x}\approx 1+x/2$). These are steps in reasoning, not things in the world.
The verbs that go with each kind
  • A system has a property: "the string has mass per unit length $\mu$."
  • A force is exerted by one thing on another: "the left neighbour exerts a tension force on this piece." A thing experiences a force; it does not own one.
  • A string is under tension $T$: tension is the size of the pull each piece exerts on the next, kept up by whatever stretches the string.
  • A wave travels along (propagates through) a medium. The medium does not travel: each piece oscillates in place, and the pattern moves.
  • A wave carries (transports) energy and momentum; power is the rate at which it carries energy past a point.
  • Energy is stored (kinetic in moving pieces, potential in stretched ones), transferred from one place to another, and dissipated by damping.
  • A boundary selects which motions are allowed; a touch filters out the modes that move at that point; initial conditions select amplitudes and phases.
Who sets what
  • Wave speed $v$ is set by the medium: its properties and set-up conditions. String: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. Sound: $v = \sqrt{B/\rho}$. Change the frequency and $v$ does not change.
  • Frequency $f$ is set by the source: whatever shakes the medium. At a junction between two media the frequency carries across unchanged, because both sides of the joint are the same point and move together.
  • Wavelength $\lambda$ follows: $\lambda = v/f$. It is the one descriptor that is not set directly; it is what the medium and the source agree on.
  • Boundaries select the allowed wavelengths of standing waves: on a string fixed at both ends, only $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ fit. Then $f_n = v/\lambda_n$: here the system, not the source, decides the frequencies.
  • Initial conditions set amplitude and phase: how the motion was started (pulled aside, plucked, struck).
  • Transverse velocity $\partial y/\partial t$ is not the wave speed. $v$ describes how fast the pattern moves along the string; $\partial y/\partial t$ is how fast one piece of string moves up and down. They are different kinds of thing: a descriptor of the wave versus the velocity of a piece of the system.
What a problem asks

Almost every problem names two of these kinds and asks how one fixes the other. Recognizing the pair tells you which relations to bring in before you start.

  • Properties → descriptors. "A string of mass 0.01 kg, length 2.5 m, under tension 10 N: find its fundamental." Properties and set-up give $v$; the boundaries give $\lambda_1$; then $f_1$.
  • Motion → descriptors. "Given $y(x,t) = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$, find $\lambda$, $T$, $v$, the direction." Read the descriptors off the formula.
  • Descriptors → properties. "Two strings at the same tension sound two octaves apart: what diameter?" Work backwards from $f$ to $\mu$.
  • Boundary → allowed motions. "Fixed ends: which $k_n$, $\omega_n$?" or "open and closed ends of a pipe."
  • Two media → matching. "A wave meets a heavier string: how much reflects?" Match displacement and slope at the joint.
  • A property that varies in space → a descriptor that varies. "$\mu(x)$ grows along the string: how long does a pulse take?" Use the local relation at each point, then add up (integrate).
  • Motion → energy and power. "Half the amplitude, twice the frequency: compare the power." Use $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2$ and say which factors changed.
Where things sit in the course
  • Ch. 1–2: one system with one coordinate (a mass, a pendulum, a charge). Interaction: a restoring force, plus a damping force in Ch. 2. Motion: SHM or damped oscillation.
  • Ch. 5: a medium, a continuous system. Interaction: each piece pulled by its neighbours. Law: Newton for one piece gives the wave equation. Motion: travelling waves.
  • Ch. 6: the same medium with boundaries. The boundaries select standing waves and normal modes; superposition builds every other motion from them.
  • PS8 and Midterm 2: the same ideas carried to new systems (air columns, an electron in a box) and to joints and changing media.

The mathematical picture
One function holds the whole motion; derivatives read its rates; the law is an equation between them; everything else is a parameter, a condition or an operation.
▸
One function holds the motion

For a string, the entire motion is one function $y(x,t)$: the height of the piece at $x$ at time $t$. Fix $t$ and you have a snapshot, the shape at that moment; fix $x$ and you have the history of one piece. Every question about the motion is a question about this function.

Derivatives read the rates
  • $\partial y/\partial t$: how fast one piece moves (its velocity); $\partial^2y/\partial t^2$: its acceleration.
  • $\partial y/\partial x$: the slope of the string at a point; $\partial^2y/\partial x^2$: its curvature, how sharply it bends.
The law is an equation between rates

The wave equation $\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}$ says: acceleration is proportional to curvature. A piece where the string bends sharply is pulled hard by its two neighbours (their tension forces no longer cancel), so it accelerates. $v^2 = T/\mu$ is the constant of proportionality: tension pulls, mass resists.

Sinusoids are the simplest solutions

$A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$ solves the wave equation whenever $\omega = vk$. Its parameters are the descriptors: $A$ the size, $k$ how fast it repeats in space, $\omega$ how fast in time, $\phi$ where it starts. Because the equation is linear (no products or powers of $y$), any sum of solutions is a solution: that is superposition, and it is why the moves work.

Conditions turn a continuum into a list

Without ends, every $k$ is allowed. Requiring $y = 0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ leaves only $\sin kL = 0$, i.e. $k_n = n\pi/L$: a list. Every motion of the fixed string is then a sum of these modes (Fourier), each with its own amplitude and phase set by how the string was started.

Energy is an average of squares

Energies come from $\tfrac12m(\text{velocity})^2$ and from stretching, both squares of the motion. Averaged over a cycle, $\langle\sin^2\rangle = \langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$, which is where the $\tfrac12$ in $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2$ comes from, and why energy goes as $A^2\omega^2$.

How to work a problem: bring in, set parameters, apply
The order every homework page in this guide follows.
▸
Steps

  1. Name the system and place the problem on the map.

    What is the thing, and which two kinds of thing does the question connect (properties to descriptors, a boundary to allowed motions, …)?

  2. Bring in the relations.

    List the concepts and equations that connect those two kinds, each with one line saying why it applies here. Say where each comes from: a law, a definition, or a result derived earlier.

  3. Set the parameters.

    Write every given quantity with its symbol, value and unit, and say what kind of thing it is (a property of the string, a set-up condition, a descriptor of the wave). Convert to SI units now. Write what is wanted the same way.

  4. Apply.

    Substitute and rearrange, one operation at a time, each with its reason ("divide both sides by $2L$", "square both sides").

  5. Check.

    Units; size (is a guitar string's tension about 100 N? is an electron's energy a few eV?); limits (what happens if the two strings are the same, so nothing should reflect?).

Why this order

Bringing in the relations before the numbers keeps the physics separate from the arithmetic: you decide what governs the situation first, and the parameters then only fill the slots. It also makes partial credit easy to earn, because the reasoning is on the page before any number appears.

The problem types and their methods
  • A wave formula is given (find $\lambda$, $T$, $v$, direction): read the wave off its equation. Sketch at two times: sketch a travelling wave at two times.
  • How fast a piece of string moves: transverse velocity, $\partial y/\partial t$ with $x$ held fixed. Not the wave speed.
  • A second wave on the same string (half the amplitude, twice the frequency…): compare two waves on the same string. The medium sets $v$; everything else goes through $A\omega$.
  • Two strings joined (reflection, transmission, power): match a wave across a junction, six steps.
  • Fixed ends (allowed $k_n$, $\omega_n$, sketches of modes): find the modes from the boundary conditions.
  • A sum of modes (which modes, a value, points at rest, spectrum): analyse a motion made of several modes.
  • A property that varies along the string (speed trend, travel time): travel time through a varying medium.
Equations to know cold
  • Reading a wave: $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, $T = 2\pi/\omega$, $v = \omega/k = f\lambda$; $kx - \omega t$ moves toward $+x$ (definitions).
  • Speed on a string, set by the medium: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ (string speed).
  • Velocity of a piece: $\partial y/\partial t$; its largest value is $A\omega$ (transverse velocity).
  • Energy and power carried: $\langle dE/dx\rangle = \tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2$, $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2$; $\mu v = \sqrt{\mu T}$ (energy and power).
  • Junction: $y$ and $\partial y/\partial x$ continuous; $r = \frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}$, $t = \frac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2}$; $\mathcal R = r^2$, $\mathcal T = \frac{\mu_2v_2}{\mu_1v_1}t^2$ (junction).
  • Fixed ends: $k_n = n\pi/L$, $\omega_n = vk_n = n\omega_1$, mode shape $\sin(n\pi x/L)$, nodes at $mL/n$ (modes, normal mode).
  • Standing wave = two opposite travelling waves: $A\cos(kx - \omega t) + A\cos(kx + \omega t) = 2A\cos kx\cos\omega t$ (standing and travelling, sum to product).
  • Varying medium: $v(x) = \sqrt{T/\mu(x)}$, $t = \int dx/v(x)$ (nonuniform string).
Checks that catch mistakes
  • Units on every answer: $\lambda$ in m, $T$ in s, $\omega$ in s⁻¹, $v$ in m s⁻¹, power in W.
  • Junction: equal strings give $r = 0$; a heavier second string gives $r \lt 0$ (inverted); $\mathcal R + \mathcal T = 1$.
  • Modes: $n$ starts at 1; mode $n$ has $n$ humps and $n + 1$ nodes counting the ends.
  • Travel time lies between $L/v_{\max}$ and $L/v_{\min}$.
Common mistakes
  • Giving the wave speed $v$ when $\partial y/\partial t$ is asked for (or the reverse).
  • Letting the speed change with frequency on the same string.
  • Changing $\omega$ across a junction; forgetting the $\mu_2v_2/\mu_1v_1$ factor in $\mathcal T$.
  • Calling a node of one mode a point at rest when another mode moves there.
  • Using $L/v$ with a single speed when the speed varies.

(b)

(c)

Bring in: the relations between them
  • Wavelength and period from the angular quantities: $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ (wavelength from wavenumber) and $T = 2\pi/\omega$ (period from angular frequency), because the cosine repeats when its argument grows by $2\pi$.
  • Wave speed $v = \omega/k$: how far the pattern goes per unit time; one wavelength per period.
  • Direction from the sign: in $kx - \omega t$ the pattern moves toward $+x$; in $kx + \omega t$, toward $-x$.
  • Transverse velocity $\partial y/\partial t$: the rate of change of the displacement $y$ with time, at a fixed position $x$.
  • Energy and power: energy per metre $\langle dE/dx\rangle = \frac12\mu\omega^2A^2$ and power $\langle P\rangle = \frac12\mu v\omega^2A^2$, in terms of linear mass density $\mu$, speed $v$, angular frequency $\omega$ and amplitude $A$.
  • Same string means the same tension $T$ and linear mass density $\mu$, hence the same speed $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$: the medium sets the speed.

Split off the part of the incoming wave that the reflection can pair with

Problem 4 · Open and closed ends of an air column (French 6-8)
▸
The problem

“Show that for vibrations of an air column:

(a)

Given

  • $p(x,t)$pressure change: how far the pressure at $x$ is above or below atmospheric

  • (a)why an open end has $p = 0$, and what follows for $\xi$
  • (b)why a closed end has $\xi = 0$, and what follows for $p$
Plan: the route

  2. Set-up → features of the motion: (a) open end $p = 0$; (b) closed end $\xi = 0$
Methods used
  • Ends of an air column (4 steps)
Bring in: the relations between them
  • Sound's two descriptions.
  • Pressure from displacement $p = -B\,\partial\xi/\partial x$.
  • Pipe modes.
Work it through, part by part
Before (a) and (b): the two descriptions and the link between them.
Method: Ends of an air column · step 1 of 4

Describe the sound by how far each layer of air moves: a standing wave in the pipe

$\xi(x,t)$ is how far the layer of air at $x$ is moved along the tube. The air obeys the same wave equation as a string, with $v = \sqrt{B/\rho}$; sound reflects at both ends of the pipe, so the incoming and reflected waves form a standing wave: a shape in $x$ times an oscillation in $t$ (standing and travelling waves). The phase $\phi$ is left open because where the nodes fall depends on the ends, which is what the problem asks; by angle addition $\sin(kx+\phi)$ covers every mix of $\sin kx$ and $\cos kx$. $\xi_0$ is the amplitude.

$$\xi = \xi_0\sin(kx + \phi)\cos\omega t$$
Uses: sound's two descriptions · standing wave · $v = \sqrt{B/\rho}$
Method: Ends of an air column · step 2 of 4

The same sound described by pressure

Recall: $p = -B\,\partial\xi/\partial x$ (pressure from displacement). Differentiate with respect to $x$; the inside derivative is $k$.

$$p = -Bk\xi_0\cos(kx + \phi)\cos\omega t$$
Uses: $p = -B\,\partial\xi/\partial x$ (pressure change from how the air is squeezed)
Method: Ends of an air column · step 2 of 4

Where one description is still, the other swings most

Where $\sin(kx+\phi) = 0$, $|\cos(kx+\phi)| = 1$, and the reverse: displacement nodes are pressure antinodes and vice versa.

(a)
An open end represents a condition of zero pressure change … and hence a place of maximum movement of the air (an antinode).
Method: Ends of an air column · step 3 of 4

At an open end the atmosphere fixes the pressure

The air at the opening is in contact with the open atmosphere, which holds the pressure there at atmospheric: the pressure change is zero.

Uses: pipe modes
Method: Ends of an air column · step 3 of 4

So the air there moves the most

$p = 0$ at the end means $\cos(kx+\phi) = 0$ there, so $|\sin(kx+\phi)| = 1$: the displacement is as large as anywhere in the wave.

Answer (a). Open end: $p = 0$, so by the link $|\xi|$ is largest there, a displacement antinode.
(b)
A closed end is a place of zero movement (a node) and hence maximum pressure variation.”
Method: Ends of an air column · step 4 of 4

At a closed end the wall stops the air

Air next to a rigid wall cannot move into or out of it.

Uses: pipe modes
Method: Ends of an air column · step 4 of 4

So the pressure there swings the most

$\xi = 0$ at the end means $\sin(kx+\phi) = 0$, so $|\cos(kx+\phi)| = 1$: the pressure swings are largest. The layers pile up against the wall and pull away from it.

Answer (b). Closed end: $\xi = 0$, so by the link $|p|$ is largest there, a pressure antinode.
Check

Each part needs a physical reason for the first claim and the link for the "hence". Write both in sentences.

Clean solution
Show the clean solution

With $\xi = \xi_0\sin(kx+\phi)\cos\omega t$, $p = -B\partial\xi/\partial x = -Bk\xi_0\cos(kx+\phi)\cos\omega t$. (a) Open end: contact with the atmosphere gives $p = 0$, so $|\sin(kx+\phi)| = 1$: maximum movement. (b) Closed end: the wall gives $\xi = 0$, so $|\cos(kx+\phi)| = 1$: maximum pressure variation.

Glossary · every term, tap any underlined-dotted word in the guide to see it
amplitudeThe largest displacement from equilibrium, $A$ (m). The motion goes between $+A$ and $-A$.
angular frequencyRadians of phase per second, $\omega$ (rad/s). Since one cycle is $2\pi$ rad, $\omega = 2\pi f$.
antinodeA point of a standing wave that swings with the largest amplitude, midway between two nodes.
boundary conditionA condition the wave must satisfy at the edge of the medium for all time; a fixed end requires zero displacement.
chain ruleDerivative of a function of a function: $\frac{d}{dt}f(g(t)) = f'(g)\,g'(t)$. Differentiating $\cos(\omega t)$ brings out the factor $\omega$.
critical damping$\gamma = 2\omega_0$: the fastest return to equilibrium without overshooting.
damping rate$\gamma = b/m$ (s⁻¹): how fast friction removes energy. Energy decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$, amplitude as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
Doppler broadeningThe spread of frequencies emitted by atoms because they move toward or away at different thermal speeds; it gives a spectral line its width $\Delta\nu$.
envelopeThe slowly changing curve that bounds an oscillation, e.g. $\pm A_0e^{-\gamma t/2}$ for a damped oscillator.
equilibriumThe position where the net force is zero and the system can rest.
frequencyNumber of complete cycles per second, $f$ (Hz = s⁻¹). $f = 1/T$.
fundamentalThe lowest normal mode, $n = 1$: one loop, $\lambda = 2L$, $f_1 = v/(2L)$. Also called the first harmonic.
harmonicThe $n$th harmonic is the normal mode with frequency $nf_1$. The fundamental is the first harmonic.
heavy damping$\gamma/2 > \omega_0$: the system creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
light damping$\gamma/2 < \omega_0$: the system oscillates while its amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
log decrement$\ln(A_n/A_{n+1}) = \gamma T/2$: the natural log of the ratio of successive peaks.
mass per unit lengthMass of one metre of string, $\mu$ (kg m⁻¹).
mediumWhat the wave travels through (string, air, water). The medium sets the wave speed.
mode numberThe whole number $n$ labelling a normal mode: the number of half-wavelengths (loops) between the fixed ends.
natural frequency$\omega_0$: the angular frequency the system oscillates at with no damping or driving, e.g. $\sqrt{k/m}$.
nodeA point of a standing wave that never moves. Neighbouring nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart; a fixed end is a node.
normal modeA pattern in which every point of the system oscillates at the same single frequency. For a fixed–fixed string, mode $n$ has $n$ loops and frequency $nf_1$.
optical cavityTwo facing mirrors a distance $L$ apart in which light forms standing waves; its mode frequencies are $nc/(2L)$.
overtoneAny mode above the fundamental, counted from it: the first overtone is the second harmonic.
partial derivativeDerivative with respect to one variable while the others are held fixed. $\partial y/\partial t$: watch one point over time. $\partial y/\partial x$: the slope of the frozen shape.
periodTime for one complete cycle, $T$ (s). $T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega$.
phaseThe full angle inside the cosine, e.g. $kx-\omega t+\phi$. Points with the same phase are at the same stage of the cycle (e.g. all crests).
phase constantThe constant $\phi$ added inside the cosine. It slides the wave so it matches the starting condition.
plane waveA 3D wave whose crests are flat parallel planes, $\psi = A\sin(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)$, travelling along $\mathbf k$.
quadrantWhich quarter of the circle an angle lies in. $\tan$ cannot tell opposite quadrants apart, so check the signs of $\cos\phi$ and $\sin\phi$.
quality factor$Q = \omega_0/\gamma$: how many oscillations (roughly, ×$\pi$) happen before the energy fades. High $Q$ = lightly damped.
radianUnit of angle: $2\pi$ rad = one full cycle = 360°. Arguments of sin and cos in physics are in radians.
restoring forceA force that points back toward equilibrium, e.g. $F = -kx$. It is what makes a system oscillate.
SHMSimple harmonic motion: oscillation in which the restoring force is proportional to displacement, so $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ and $x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$.
sinusoidalShaped like a sine or cosine curve.
small-angle approximationFor small $\theta$ in radians, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and $\cos\theta\approx 1-\theta^2/2$. Turns nearly-SHM systems into exact SHM.
sourceWhat makes the wave (the hand moving the string end, a speaker). The source sets the frequency.
space patternThe shape of the whole wave at one frozen instant, like a photograph. It repeats every wavelength $\lambda$; its rate in space is the wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.
standing waveA wave pattern that stays in place: a fixed shape whose size oscillates, $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$. The sum of two equal waves travelling in opposite directions.
starting conditionWhere the system is and how fast it moves at one moment (usually $t=0$). They fix the constants $A$ and $\phi$.
superpositionWhen two waves occupy the same medium the displacement is their sum. Allowed because the wave equation is linear.
Taylor expansionWriting a function near a point as a sum of powers: $f(x)\approx f(0)+f'(0)x+\tfrac12f''(0)x^2+\dots$. Keeping the first terms is the basis of every small-oscillation approximation.
tensionThe pulling force along a string, $T$ (N). Sets the wave speed with $\mu$: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. Not the period.
time patternHow one point of the medium moves as time passes, watched at a fixed place. It repeats with period $T$; its rate is the frequency $f$ or angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$. Set by the source.
travelling waveA disturbance whose shape moves through a medium at speed $v$, $y = f(x \mp vt)$, while each point of the medium only oscillates in place.
wave equation$\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. Newton's second law for a small piece of the medium; any shape moving at $v$ satisfies it.
wave vector$\mathbf k = (k_1,k_2,k_3)$: points in the direction of travel; its length is the wavenumber $2\pi/\lambda$.
wavelengthDistance between neighbouring crests (or any two points in step), $\lambda$ (m). $\lambda = v/f$.
wavenumberRadians of phase per metre, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ (rad/m). Some books instead use $1/\lambda$; check which.

On the map: a state of the system: where it rests when nothing disturbs it
▸
Definition

Equilibrium is the position where the net force is zero and the system can rest. A restoring force is a force that, after a displacement, points back toward equilibrium.

In plain words

Pull it, it pulls back. The farther you pull, the harder it pulls back (for small pulls).

Why it matters

A restoring force proportional to displacement, $F = -kx$, is exactly what produces simple harmonic motion. Almost every system near a stable equilibrium behaves this way for small displacements.

Where it comes from

Near any stable equilibrium the potential energy is a minimum, shaped like a bowl. Expanding it in a Taylor series, the first term that matters is quadratic, $U \approx \tfrac12 kx^2$, and $F = -dU/dx = -kx$.

What depends on it
  • SHM and $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$.
  • Pendulum: gravity’s component along the arc, $-mg\sin\theta \approx -mg\theta$.
  • Waves: tension acts as the restoring force on each piece of string.
Where it sits

The “small oscillations” idea: every stable system, from molecules to bridges, is approximately a set of springs near equilibrium.

Example

Spring with $k = 20$ N/m pulled 0.1 m: restoring force 2 N back toward equilibrium.

Often confused with

Any force. Gravity on a hanging mass is not by itself restoring; the combination of gravity and spring force is, about the new equilibrium.

Simple harmonic motion (SHM)
On the map: a motion: what a system with a linear restoring force does
▸
Definition

Motion in which the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it: $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$. Its solution is a sinusoid of fixed amplitude and frequency.

In plain words

The smooth back-and-forth of a mass on a spring or a small pendulum swing: same rhythm forever, whatever the size of the swing.

Why it is defined this way

The equation $\ddot x = -\omega^2x$ says “the function’s second derivative is minus a constant times itself”. Only sine and cosine do that, so the motion must be sinusoidal.

Where it comes from

Newton’s second law with a linear restoring force: $m\ddot x = -kx$; divide by $m$: $\ddot x = -(k/m)x$, so $\omega^2 = k/m$.

Key properties
  • Period independent of amplitude (isochronous).
  • Energy $\tfrac12kA^2$ constant, moving between kinetic and potential.
  • Velocity is a quarter cycle ahead of displacement.
What depends on it
  • Damped (Ch. 2) and driven (Ch. 3) oscillators add terms to this equation.
  • Each point of a wave does SHM.
  • Each normal mode of a string is an SHM at its own frequency.
Where it sits

The most important model in physics: atoms in solids, LC circuits, molecular vibrations, and the quantum harmonic oscillator all start here. SHM is the shadow of uniform circular motion (trig).

Example

0.5 kg on a 50 N/m spring: $\omega = \sqrt{100} = 10$ rad/s, $T = 0.63$ s.

Often confused with

Any oscillation. A large-angle pendulum or a damped spring oscillates but is not SHM.

concept
$$E = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2$$
Energy of an oscillator
On the map: what a motion stores and carries: kinetic and potential energy
▸
Definition

The total of kinetic energy (from motion) and potential energy (stored in the spring, the height, or the stretch of a string).

In plain words

At the ends of the swing the mass stops: all energy is stored. At the middle it moves fastest: all energy is motion. The total stays the same.

Why it matters

Energy conservation often gives answers without solving equations of motion: the maximum speed $\omega A$, or the energy of a plucked string from the work done to deform it (PS7 · 5a).

Where it comes from

Multiply $m\ddot x + kx = 0$ by $\dot x$: $\frac{d}{dt}(\tfrac12m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12kx^2) = 0$, so the bracket is constant. At $x = A$, $\dot x = 0$, giving $\tfrac12 kA^2$.

What depends on it
  • Damping: $E = E_0e^{-\gamma t}$ (Ch. 2).
  • Quality factor (energy lost per cycle).
  • Energy of a vibrating string, $T\Delta L$ for a pluck (Ch. 6).
Where it sits

Energy is conserved whenever there is no friction; the swap between two forms of energy is the engine of every oscillation and wave.

Example

$k = 50$ N/m, $A = 0.1$ m: $E = 0.25$ J.

Often confused with

Power or intensity (energy per time, or per time per area).

Natural frequency
On the map: a descriptor fixed by properties: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ comes from the system, not from a source
▸
Definition

The angular frequency at which a system oscillates when disturbed and left alone, with no damping and no driving force.

In plain words

The system’s own preferred rhythm.

Why it matters

Damping lowers the actual frequency slightly below $\omega_0$; driving at $\omega_0$ produces resonance (Ch. 3). A string has a whole list of natural frequencies, its normal modes.

Where it comes from

From the equation of motion with damping and driving removed: $\ddot x = -\omega_0^2x$.

What depends on it
  • Damped frequency $\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$.
  • Quality factor $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$.
  • Resonance peak position.
Where it sits

Engineers avoid matching natural frequencies with vibrations (bridges, engines); musicians exploit them (instrument bodies, strings).

Example

Pendulum of length 1 m: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{9.8/1} = 3.13$ rad/s, $T = 2.0$ s.

Often confused with

The driving frequency $\omega$, which is imposed from outside.

Damping regimes: light, critical, heavy
On the map: an interaction: a drag force the surroundings exert, which dissipates energy
▸
Definition

The three kinds of behaviour of $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$, decided by comparing $\gamma$ with $2\omega_0$.

In plain words

Light: it still swings, but each swing is smaller. Critical: it returns to rest as fast as possible without overshooting. Heavy: it creeps back slowly without overshooting.

Why the dividing line is $2\omega_0$

Trying $x = e^{pt}$ gives $p^2 + \gamma p + \omega_0^2 = 0$, so $p = -\gamma/2 \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2/4 - \omega_0^2}$. The square root is imaginary (oscillation) when $\gamma < 2\omega_0$, zero when equal, real when greater.

What depends on it
  • Which formula describes the motion (Ch. 2 cards).
  • Design choices: car suspensions and door closers aim near critical damping.
Where it sits

The same three regimes appear in LCR circuits ($\gamma = R/L$) and any linear system with loss.

Example

$\omega_0 = 10$ rad/s: critical damping at $\gamma = 20$ s⁻¹.

Often confused with

Heavy damping is not “stops fastest”: critical damping returns quicker.

Waves: the core ideas (Ch. 5–6)
concept
$$y = f(x - vt)$$
Travelling wave
On the map: a motion: a pattern moving through a medium while each piece oscillates in place
▸
Definition

A disturbance whose shape moves through a medium at a fixed speed $v$ without changing shape, while each point of the medium only moves about its rest position.

In plain words

A ripple or a pulse on a rope: the bump moves along, the rope itself does not travel.

Why it is defined this way

Any function of $x - vt$ has the same value wherever $x - vt$ is the same, i.e. at positions shifted by $vt$. So $f(x - vt)$ is exactly “the shape $f$ slid right by $vt$”.

What depends on it
  • Sinusoidal waves $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$, a special case.
  • Standing waves: two travelling waves in opposite directions.
  • The wave equation, which every travelling wave at speed $v$ satisfies.
Where it sits

Sound, light, water waves, seismic waves and signals on cables are all travelling waves; they carry energy and information without carrying the medium along.

Example

PS6 · 1: $y = 0.15\cos(0.4\pi x - 20\pi t)$ m moves toward $+x$ at 50 m/s.

Often confused with

Motion of the medium. A point on the string moves up and down; the shape moves sideways.

Transverse and longitudinal waves: which way the medium moves
On the map: a property of a motion: the direction the pieces of the medium move
▸
Definition

In a transverse wave each piece of the medium moves perpendicular to the direction the wave travels: a string moves up and down while the wave runs along it. In a longitudinal wave each piece moves along the direction of travel: layers of air in a pipe move back and forth along the pipe, crowding together and spreading apart.

How it shows in the symbols
  • String (transverse): $y(x,t)$ is the sideways displacement of the piece at $x$; its velocity $\partial y/\partial t$ is the transverse velocity.
  • Sound (longitudinal): $\xi(x,t)$ is the displacement of the air along the tube (sound's two descriptions).
What does not change

The wave itself, the pattern, always travels along $x$ at speed $v$ in both kinds. Only the motion of the pieces differs. The same wave equation, the same $\lambda$, $f$, $k$, $\omega$, and the same standing-wave and mode results apply to both.

Examples

Transverse: strings, waves on a rope, light. Longitudinal: sound in air, a compression wave along a spring.

concept
$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$$
The wave equation
On the map: a law for a medium: Newton's second law applied to one small piece
▸
Definition

The partial differential equation obeyed by waves that move at speed $v$ without changing shape.

In plain words

How fast a piece of string accelerates (left side) is set by how curved the string is there (right side). More curve, bigger pull back toward straight.

Where it comes from

Newton’s second law for a small piece of string of length $dx$: mass $\mu\,dx$; net vertical tension force $T\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2\,dx$ (tension pulls differently at the two ends when the string curves). Divide by $\mu\,dx$: $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = (T/\mu)\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$, so $v^2 = T/\mu$.

Why it matters

It is linear: the sum of two solutions is a solution (superposition). That one fact gives interference, standing waves and normal modes.

What depends on it
  • Showing a function is a wave (PS6 · 2).
  • Speed formulas such as $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.
  • Standing waves and normal modes, once boundary conditions are added.
Where it sits

The same equation (with different $v$) governs sound, light (from Maxwell’s equations) and vibrations of solids; its 3D form uses $\nabla^2$.

Example

$y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$: left side $-\omega^2y$, right side $-v^2k^2y$; equal when $\omega/k = v$.

Often confused with

The SHM equation $\ddot x = -\omega^2x$, which has one variable (time) and no space derivative.

$$y = y_1 + y_2$$
Superposition
On the map: a move: adding motions, allowed because the equation of motion is linear
▸
Definition

When two or more waves are present in the same medium, the total displacement is the sum of the individual displacements.

Picture
In plain words

Waves pass through each other and simply add where they overlap.

Why it holds

The wave equation is linear: each term contains $y$ to the first power only. If $y_1$ and $y_2$ each satisfy it, adding the two equations shows $y_1 + y_2$ satisfies it too.

What depends on it
  • Standing waves (incoming + reflected).
  • Normal modes: any motion of a string is a sum of modes.
  • Interference and beats (later).
Where it sits

Superposition underlies Fourier analysis, interference of light, noise-cancelling headphones and the principle of superposition in quantum mechanics.

Example

Two equal pulses meeting from opposite directions: where crest meets crest the height doubles; crest meets trough, they cancel for an instant.

Often confused with

Waves bouncing off each other. They do not; they pass through and continue unchanged.

Limits

It fails for very large amplitudes, when the medium no longer responds linearly (the small-slope assumption breaks).

Waves: the core quantities
concept
$$\text{time pattern} \;\leftrightarrow\; \text{space pattern}$$
Two ways to look at the same wave
On the map: a motion: the shape that moves, $f(x - vt)$
▸
Definition

A wave depends on two variables, position $x$ and time $t$. You can look at it two ways. Time pattern: stand at one place and watch that point move up and down as time passes. Space pattern: freeze time and look at the whole shape along $x$, like a photograph.

Why it matters

Almost every quantity in the course belongs to one pattern or the other: $T$, $f$, $\omega$ to time; $\lambda$, $k$ to space. Knowing which pattern a quantity belongs to tells you what sets it (the source sets time, the source and medium together set space) and which equation links them ($v = f\lambda = \omega/k$).

Picture
The two graphs

Time pattern: graph $y$ against $t$ at fixed $x$. It repeats every period $T$; its rates are $f$ and $\omega$. Space pattern: graph $y$ against $x$ at fixed $t$. It repeats every wavelength $\lambda$; its rate is $k$. For $y = A\cos(kx-\omega t)$: at $x = 0$ you get $A\cos\omega t$ (SHM in time); at $t = 0$ you get $A\cos kx$ (a cosine in space).

How they connect

The wave speed links them. In one period $T$ the shape slides forward one wavelength $\lambda$, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda = \omega/k$. Time quantities ($T, f, \omega$) come from the source; space quantities ($\lambda, k$) follow from the source and the medium together.

Parallel table
Time patternSpace pattern
Repeat length$T$ (s)$\lambda$ (m)
Repeats per unit$f = 1/T$$1/\lambda$
Radians per unit$\omega = 2\pi/T$$k = 2\pi/\lambda$
What depends on it
  • Wave speed: v is the bridge between the two patterns.
  • Reading an equation: the coefficient of $t$ is time-pattern ($\omega$), the coefficient of $x$ is space-pattern ($k$).
  • Standing waves (Ch. 6): the separation $\sin kx\cdot\cos\omega t$ is literally a space pattern times a time pattern.
Where it sits

In physics generally, the pairing time ↔ space mirrors frequency ↔ wavenumber, and in quantum mechanics energy ↔ momentum ($E = \hbar\omega$, $p = \hbar k$). The two-graph habit (photograph vs. one point over time) is how waves are read in every field.

Often confused with

A graph of $y$ against $x$ and a graph of $y$ against $t$ look alike (both sinusoids). Check the axis: the repeat distance on an $x$-graph is $\lambda$; the repeat time on a $t$-graph is $T$.

concept
$$u = x - vt$$
Characteristic variable — why $f(x - vt)$ moves at speed $v$
On the map: a way of describing a motion: the argument $u = x - vt$ that the pattern depends on
▸
Definition

For a travelling wave $y = f(x - vt)$, call the combination inside $u = x - vt$. The height at a point depends only on $u$: any position and time with the same $u$ have the same height. Each feature of the shape (a crest, a trough, a point halfway up) belongs to one value of $u$.

Why it is defined this way

Grouping $x$ and $t$ into one variable $u = x - vt$ captures the defining property of a travelling wave in a single symbol: the wave depends on position and time only through this combination. Any function of $u$ alone is a shape moving at $v$.

Connection to phase

For a sine wave, the phase is the whole angle inside the cosine. Factor out $k$:

$$\underbrace{kx - \omega t}_{\text{phase}} = k\Big(\underbrace{x - \tfrac{\omega}{k}t}_{u}\Big) = k\,u, \qquad v = \frac{\omega}{k}.$$
  • Phase is the characteristic variable converted to radians. $u$ is measured in metres; multiplying by $k$ (radians per metre) turns it into an angle.
  • Same phase ⇔ same $u$ ⇔ same feature. A crest is a point of constant phase; following constant phase is following constant $u$.
  • Phase velocity. The speed at which a point of constant phase moves is $\omega/k$. That is the speed $v$ in $u = x - vt$, which is why it is called the phase velocity.
Names

Characteristic variable (or characteristic coordinate) is the standard term: d'Alembert's solution writes $\xi = x - vt$, $\eta = x + vt$, and every solution of the wave equation is $f(x - vt) + g(x + vt)$. Related names: comoving coordinate (position in a frame moving with the wave), phase (for a sine wave, the whole argument $kx - \omega t = k(x - vt)$), and retarded time $t - x/v$ (the same idea measured in time, as in PS6 2(i)). The letter $u$ is just our label.

Catching up

Follow the crest, say at $u = 0$. As time passes, $vt$ grows and pulls $u$ down. To keep $u = 0$, $x$ must grow by the same amount: $x = vt$. The crest is wherever $x$ has caught up with $vt$, so it moves forward at speed $v$. Every other feature does the same, so the whole shape slides without changing.

Riding along

Move toward $+x$ at speed $v$, starting at the origin. At time $t$ you are at $vt$, so a point at $x$ is a distance $x - vt$ ahead of you: $u$ is position measured from you. Since $y = f(u)$, the shape seen from your moving position never changes. A travelling wave is a fixed shape seen from a frame it is not moving with.

Direction

$u = x + vt$: to keep $u$ fixed as $t$ grows, $x$ must decrease, so the shape moves toward $-x$.

In problems

Sine waves: $kx - \omega t = k\left(x - \tfrac{\omega}{k}t\right)$, so $u$ appears with $v = \omega/k$; this is why the wave speed is $\omega/k$. Checking the wave equation: substitute $u$ and use the chain rule, $\partial u/\partial x = 1$ and $\partial u/\partial t = -v$; every $f(x \mp vt)$ solves it. See shape · 1D · method · time and space pattern.

What depends on it
  • Checking a wave solves the wave equation: with the chain rule through $u$, $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2 f''(u)$ and $\partial^2y/\partial x^2 = f''(u)$ (PS6 · 2).
  • Phase: $kx - \omega t = k(x - vt) = ku$.
  • General solution of the 1D wave equation: $y = f(x - vt) + g(x + vt)$ (d’Alembert).
Where it sits

In the theory of partial differential equations $x \pm vt$ are called characteristics: lines in the $(x, t)$ plane along which information travels. The same idea governs sound, light and traffic flow.

Often confused with

The displacement $y$. $u$ is not how far the string moved; it is a label for “which part of the shape” is at $x$ at time $t$.

$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \frac{\omega}{v}$$
Wavenumber — how fast the phase advances along $x$
On the map: a descriptor of a wave (spatial), $k = 2\pi/\lambda$
▸
Definition

The wavenumber $k$ is the number of radians of phase the wave goes through per metre of distance, at a frozen instant. One wavelength is one full cycle, $2\pi$ radians, so $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Units: rad/m (often written m⁻¹).

Why it is defined this way

Using radians per metre (not cycles per metre) makes $kx$ an angle, so it can go straight into a cosine and differentiate cleanly: $\partial_x\cos(kx) = -k\sin(kx)$, with no stray $2\pi$.

Every form of k, talked out

Each is radians of phase per metre.

  1. $k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$

    What it says: The definition: $2\pi$ radians in every wavelength.

    Use it when: You have a wavelength and need to write $\cos(kx - \omega t)$.

    Example: $\lambda = 5.0$ m: $k = 0.4\pi$ rad/m (PS6 · 1).

  2. $k = \dfrac{\omega}{v}$

    What it says: From $v = \omega/k$: multiply both sides by $k$, divide both sides by $v$. Radians per second divided by metres per second gives radians per metre.

    Use it when: You have $\omega$ and the medium’s speed, not the wavelength.

  3. $k = 2\pi\tilde\nu$

    What it says: Spectroscopy’s wavenumber $\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda$ counts cycles per metre; multiplying by $2\pi$ turns cycles into radians.

    Use it when: A source quotes “wavenumber” in cm⁻¹; check which one it means.

  4. $k_n = \dfrac{n\pi}{L}$

    What it says: For fixed ends, $\sin kL = 0$ forces $kL = n\pi$. Same content as $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, written for $k$.

    Use it when: Writing the mode shape $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ of a fixed–fixed string. modes

Intuition

$k$ is the space version of $\omega$. $\omega$ says how quickly one point cycles in time; $k$ says how quickly the shape cycles as you walk along it. Large $k$: crests packed tightly (short wavelength). Small $k$: crests spread out (long wavelength).

Where it appears
  • Inside the wave: $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$. It multiplies $x$ so that $kx$ is an angle in radians.
  • Wave speed: $v = \omega/k$.
  • Wave equation: each $\partial/\partial x$ of $\cos(kx-\omega t)$ brings out a factor $k$, so $\partial^2y/\partial x^2 = -k^2 y$.
  • Reading an equation: $k$ is the number multiplying $x$.
Picture

At $t = 0$, $y = A\cos kx$. Walking from $x$ to $x + \lambda$ increases $kx$ by $k\lambda = 2\pi$: exactly one full cycle, so the shape repeats. That is why $k\lambda = 2\pi$.

Example

PS6 Problem 1: $\lambda = 5.0$ m, so $k = 2\pi/5.0 = 0.4\pi \approx 1.26$ rad/m. The phase advances 1.26 rad for every metre along the string.

Watch out
  • Not the spring constant. Chapter 1 uses $k$ for the spring constant (N/m) in $F = -kx$. Same letter, unrelated quantity. In Chapter 5, $k$ is almost always the wavenumber.
  • Two “wavenumbers”. Spectroscopy uses $\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda$ (cycles per metre). $k = 2\pi\tilde\nu$. State which you mean.
In 3D

The wavenumber becomes the wave vector $\mathbf k = (k_1, k_2, k_3)$: it points in the direction of travel, and its length is $2\pi/\lambda$.

What depends on it
  • Wave speed $v = \omega/k$.
  • Allowed modes of a string: $k_n = n\pi/L$ (Ch. 6).
  • Wave vector and direction of travel in 3D.
  • Later: momentum of a quantum particle, $p = \hbar k$.
Where it sits

Wavenumber is to space what angular frequency is to time. In Fourier analysis, a shape is described by how much of each $k$ it contains, just as a sound is described by its frequencies.

Often confused with

Spring constant (also $k$), and the spectroscopic wavenumber $1/\lambda$ (see Watch out).

Wavelength — the repeat length of the wave in space
On the map: a descriptor of a wave; it follows from the medium's speed and the source's frequency
▸
Definition

The distance between two neighbouring points that are at the same stage of the cycle, e.g. crest to crest. Units: m.

Why it is defined this way

It is the most direct measurement of a wave’s space pattern: you can see or measure crest-to-crest distance. $k$ is the same information in a form that is convenient inside equations.

Where it comes from

The wave repeats in space; $\lambda$ is the smallest shift $\Delta x$ that leaves the shape unchanged at a frozen instant. Since $kx$ must advance by $2\pi$ over that shift, $k\lambda = 2\pi$.

Every form of λ, talked out

Each is the repeat length of the wave in space.

  1. $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f}$

    What it says: $v = f\lambda$ divided by $f$: the distance the wave covers in one second, shared out among the $f$ cycles made in that second.

    Use it when: Most problems: medium known, frequency known (PS7 · 1a, 1c).

    Example: 340 m/s sound at 262 Hz: $\lambda = 1.30$ m.

  2. $\lambda = vT$

    What it says: The distance the wave moves in one period. Same as $v/f$ because $T = 1/f$.

    Use it when: You are given a period instead of a frequency.

  3. $\lambda = \dfrac{2\pi}{k}$

    What it says: One cycle is $2\pi$ radians, and $k$ is radians per metre, so $2\pi/k$ metres make one cycle.

    Use it when: You read $k$ from an equation (the number multiplying $x$).

    Example: $\sin(3x - 12t)$: $\lambda = 2\pi/3 = 2.1$ m.

  4. $\lambda_n = \dfrac{2L}{n}$

    What it says: On a string (or cavity) with both ends fixed, only wavelengths that fit $n$ half-wavelengths into $L$ are allowed. The fundamental ($n = 1$) is $\lambda = 2L$.

    Use it when: Normal modes, successive modes (PS7 · 2a). modes

  5. $\lambda = 2d_{\text{nodes}}$

    What it says: Neighbouring nodes of a standing wave are half a wavelength apart, so the wavelength is twice their spacing.

    Use it when: Measured node positions: cold spots in a microwave oven (PS7 · 2b). nodes

Intuition

In the time it takes one point to complete one oscillation (one period), the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength. That is the whole content of $v = f\lambda$.

What sets it

Neither the source nor the medium alone: the source fixes $f$, the medium fixes $v$, and $\lambda = v/f$ follows. When a wave enters a new medium, $f$ stays the same and $\lambda$ changes with $v$.

Typical sizes
  • Visible light: 400–700 nm.
  • Audible sound in air: about 17 mm (20 kHz) to 17 m (20 Hz).
  • AM radio: hundreds of metres.
What depends on it
  • Which modes fit on a string: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ (Ch. 6).
  • Node spacing $\lambda/2$ in a standing wave.
  • Diffraction and interference (later): effects are large when obstacles are comparable to $\lambda$.
Where it sits

Wavelength sorts the electromagnetic spectrum (radio to gamma rays) and sets the resolution limit of microscopes and telescopes. In quantum mechanics a particle has a de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$.

Example

Angular frequency — radians of phase per second
On the map: a descriptor of a motion (in time), set by the source or by the system's properties
▸
Definition

How many radians of phase one point of the oscillation goes through per second. One cycle is $2\pi$ rad, so $\omega = 2\pi f$. Units: rad/s (often written s⁻¹).

Why it is defined this way

Radians make calculus clean (see Why radians), and $\omega$ appears directly as the square root in equations of motion: $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$. So the physics hands you $\omega$, not $f$.

Where it comes from

For a spring: Newton’s law $m\ddot x = -kx$, divide both sides by $m$: $\ddot x = -(k/m)x$. Compare with $\ddot x = -\omega^2x$: $\omega^2 = k/m$.

Every form of ω, talked out

All give radians of phase per second. $\omega$ is the form that goes inside a cosine and comes out of derivatives.

  1. $\omega = 2\pi f$

    What it says: Cycles per second times radians per cycle. The conversion you use most.

    Use it when: A problem gives Hz and you need to write $\cos(\omega t)$ or $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ (PS6 · 1).

    Example: 10 Hz: $\omega = 20\pi$ rad/s.

  2. $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}$

    What it says: $2\pi$ radians of phase in one period.

    Use it when: You are given a period.

  3. $\omega = vk$

    What it says: From $v = \omega/k$: multiply both sides by $k$. Time rate equals speed times space rate: walking along at $v$, you pass $k$ radians per metre, $v$ metres per second.

    Use it when: You know $k$ (or $\lambda$) and the medium, and want the $t$ coefficient of a wave equation.

  4. $\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}},\ \sqrt{\dfrac{g}{l}},\ \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$

    What it says: Natural angular frequencies of the Ch. 1 oscillators: each comes from writing the equation of motion as $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ and reading off $\omega^2$.

    Use it when: Spring, pendulum or LC circuit problems. 1.7

  5. $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$

    What it says: The angular frequency of a lightly damped oscillator: slightly lower than the undamped $\omega_0$ because friction slows each swing.

    Use it when: Damped oscillator (Ch. 2), when the problem asks how much damping shifts the frequency.

Why radians

Calculus works cleanly only in radians: $\frac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t = -\omega\sin\omega t$. With $f$ inside the cosine you would carry $2\pi$ through every derivative. That is why equations use $\omega$ and $k$ rather than $f$ and $1/\lambda$.

Where it appears
  • SHM: $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ (spring) or $\sqrt{g/l}$ (pendulum).
  • Waves: the number multiplying $t$ in $\cos(kx - \omega t)$.
  • Wave speed: $v = \omega/k$.
Three omegas
  • $\omega_0$: natural angular frequency, with no damping (Ch. 2).
  • $\omega$ in a damped oscillator: $\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$, slightly lower.
  • $\omega$ of a driving force: whatever the driver imposes (Ch. 3).
What depends on it
  • Period and frequency: $T = 2\pi/\omega$, $f = \omega/2\pi$.
  • Wave speed $v = \omega/k$.
  • Energy of SHM: $E = \tfrac12 m\omega^2A^2$.
  • Resonance (Ch. 3): response is largest when the driving $\omega$ matches $\omega_0$.
Where it sits

Angular frequency is the standard “rate” in all of oscillation and wave physics, electrical engineering (AC circuits) and quantum mechanics ($E = \hbar\omega$).

Example

10 Hz: $\omega = 2\pi(10) = 20\pi \approx 62.8$ rad/s.

Often confused with

Frequency $f$. They differ by the factor $2\pi$; Hz means cycles per second, rad/s means radians per second.

Frequency — cycles per second
On the map: a descriptor of a motion, set by the source (or by the system, for normal modes)
▸
Definition

The number of complete cycles per second. Units: hertz, Hz = s⁻¹.

Intuition

Stand at one place and count how many crests pass you each second. That count is $f$.

Why it is defined this way

It is what we hear and measure directly (pitch in Hz, radio stations in MHz). Equations prefer $\omega$; measurements and answers usually come in $f$.

Every form of f, talked out

All of these are the same number, cycles per second, reached from different starting information. Pick the form whose right-hand side you already know.

  1. $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$

    What it says: Frequency and period are reciprocals. If one cycle takes $T$ seconds, then $1/T$ cycles fit into one second. This is the definition; every other form leads back to it.

    Use it when: You are given a time for one cycle (or can measure one), e.g. a pendulum takes 2 s for a full swing there and back, so $f = 0.5$ Hz.

  2. $f = \dfrac{\omega}{2\pi}$

    What it says: The same rate counted in cycles instead of radians. One cycle is $2\pi$ radians, so dividing radians per second by $2\pi$ gives cycles per second.

    Use it when: You have $\omega$ from an equation (the number multiplying $t$ in $\cos(kx - \omega t)$) or from a formula like $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, and the question asks for hertz.

    Example: $y = 0.02\sin(3x - 12t)$: $\omega = 12$ rad/s, so $f = 12/2\pi = 1.9$ Hz.

  3. $f = \dfrac{v}{\lambda}$

    What it says: In one second the wave moves $v$ metres; each cycle takes up $\lambda$ metres of that; so $v/\lambda$ cycles pass a fixed point per second. It is $v = f\lambda$ divided by $\lambda$.

    Use it when: You know the medium (so $v$) and the wavelength. Light: $f = c/\lambda$. Cold spots in a microwave oven give $\lambda$, then $f = c/\lambda$ (PS7 · 2b).

    Example: 500 nm light: $f = 3.00\times10^8 / 5.0\times10^{-7} = 6.0\times10^{14}$ Hz.

  4. $f_n = \dfrac{nv}{2L} = nf_1$

    What it says: For a string fixed at both ends, only the frequencies that fit a whole number $n$ of half-wavelengths are allowed: $f = v/\lambda$ with $\lambda_n = 2L/n$. All of them are whole multiples of the lowest, $f_1$.

    Use it when: A string, cavity or pipe with fixed ends: harmonics, a fundamental, “successive modes” (PS7 · 1b, 3, 4). f_n

    Example: A string with $f_1 = 440$ Hz has harmonics 880, 1320, … Hz.

  5. $f_1 = \dfrac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$

    What it says: The fundamental written in terms of the string itself: substitute $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ into $f_1 = v/(2L)$. It shows how to tune: tighten (more $T$) to go up, use a heavier string (more $\mu$) or a longer one to go down.

    Use it when: The problem gives tension, mass per length and length rather than a speed (PS7 · 1b).

  6. $f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}},\quad f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{l}}$

    What it says: The natural frequency of an oscillator: $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ for a spring and mass, $\sqrt{g/l}$ for a pendulum (Ch. 1), divided by $2\pi$. Restoring strength on top, inertia underneath.

    Use it when: An oscillator is described by its physical parts, not by a time. 1.7

What sets it

For a wave, the source. Shake the end of a string 10 times a second and every point of the string moves 10 times a second. Frequency does not change when the wave passes into a different medium.

Watch out

Problems give $f$ in Hz, but equations need $\omega$ in rad/s: multiply by $2\pi$ before putting it inside a cosine.

What depends on it
  • Pitch of a note: the fundamental frequency (Ch. 6).
  • Wavelength in a medium: $\lambda = v/f$.
  • Energy of light (later): $E = hf$.
Where it sits

Frequency is the property of a wave that survives every change of medium. Spectra (what frequencies are present) identify atoms, musical instruments and radio signals.

Example

Middle C: 262 Hz. Concert A: 440 Hz. The violin harmonics in PS7 · 3 are $2\times440$, $3\times440$ Hz.

Often confused with

Angular frequency $\omega$ (a factor $2\pi$ larger) and the symbol $\nu$, which King uses for the same thing.

Period — the time for one cycle
On the map: a descriptor of a motion: the time for one cycle
▸
Definition

The time taken for one complete oscillation. Units: s.

Why it is defined this way

It is the natural unit of time for a repeating motion: everything about the motion is the same one period later.

Every form of T, talked out

Each is the time for one complete cycle, from different starting information.

  1. $T = \dfrac{1}{f}$

    What it says: The reciprocal of frequency: if $f$ cycles happen each second, each one takes $1/f$ seconds.

    Use it when: You have a frequency in Hz and want seconds per cycle.

    Example: 262 Hz: $T = 1/262 = 3.8$ ms.

  2. $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$

    What it says: One cycle is $2\pi$ radians; at $\omega$ radians per second it takes $2\pi/\omega$ seconds to get through them.

    Use it when: You have $\omega$ from an equation or from $\sqrt{k/m}$.

    Example: $\omega = 12$ rad/s: $T = 2\pi/12 = 0.52$ s.

  3. $T = \dfrac{\lambda}{v}$

    What it says: The time for the wave to move forward one wavelength. Watching one point, that is exactly the time until the next crest arrives, so it is the period. It is $v = \lambda/T$ rearranged (multiply by $T$, divide by $v$).

    Use it when: You know the wavelength and the medium’s speed.

  4. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}},\quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}$

    What it says: Natural periods of a spring–mass and a pendulum: $2\pi/\omega$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ or $\sqrt{g/l}$. Heavier mass or longer pendulum: slower.

    Use it when: An oscillator is described by its parts (Ch. 1).

  5. $T_1 = \dfrac{2L}{v}$

    What it says: The fundamental period of a string fixed at both ends: $1/f_1$ with $f_1 = v/(2L)$. Physically, the time for a disturbance to go to the far end and come back.

    Use it when: How often a released shape reappears on a string (PS7 · 5b). recur

Intuition

Watch one point: the time between two successive highest positions is $T$. During that time the wave shape moves one wavelength.

Watch out

$T$ is also used for tension in $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. Check the context.

What depends on it
  • Frequency $f = 1/T$.
  • How often a released shape reappears on a string: $T_1 = 2L/v$ (Ch. 6).
  • Damping comparisons: how many periods it takes to decay (quality factor).
Where it sits

Clocks are built on periods (pendulums, quartz crystals, atomic transitions). The pendulum’s period being independent of amplitude (for small swings) is why it was used for timekeeping.

Example

A 262 Hz string: $T = 1/262 = 3.82$ ms.

Amplitude — the size of the oscillation
On the map: a descriptor of a motion, set by how it was started
▸
Definition

The largest displacement from equilibrium. The motion ranges from $+A$ to $-A$. Units: those of the displacement (m for a string).

In plain words

How big the swing is: how far the point gets from where it rests.

Why it is defined this way

It is the one number that sets the size of a sinusoid without changing its timing. Separating size ($A$) from timing ($\omega$, $\phi$) is what makes the cosine form useful.

Where it comes from

Not from the equation of motion, which allows any size. It is set by the starting conditions: how far you pull and how hard you push at $t = 0$ (initial conditions).

Properties
  • Energy goes as $A^2$: $E = \tfrac12kA^2$. Double the amplitude, four times the energy.
  • In SHM the frequency does not depend on $A$.
  • With damping, $A$ shrinks as $A_0e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
What depends on it
  • Energy (as $A^2$).
  • Standing waves: each point has its own amplitude $2A|\sin kx|$ (Ch. 6).
  • Damping: the envelope $A_0e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
Where it sits

In acoustics, amplitude is loudness; in optics, brightness goes as amplitude squared (intensity). In quantum mechanics, the square of the amplitude is probability.

Example

PS6 · 1: $A = 0.15$ m, so the string moves between $+0.15$ m and $-0.15$ m.

Often confused with

Peak-to-peak height ($2A$) and displacement at a particular moment ($y$, which is between $-A$ and $+A$).

Phase and phase constant — where in the cycle you are
On the map: a descriptor of a motion: where in the cycle it is
▸
Definition

The phase is the whole angle inside the cosine. It says where in its cycle a point is: phase 0 is a crest (for cosine), $\pi$ a trough, $\pi/2$ crossing zero. The phase constant $\phi$ is the fixed part, set by the starting condition.

Why it is defined this way

One angle captures “where in the cycle” for every point and time at once. Comparing phases tells you whether two points or two waves are in step, which decides interference and standing waves.

Every way of writing the phase, talked out

Each is the same angle inside the cosine, written to show something different.

  1. $kx - \omega t + \phi$

    What it says: The standard form: space rate times position, minus time rate times time, plus a start.

    Use it when: Writing or reading a wave equation.

  2. $k(x - vt) + \phi$

    What it says: Factor out $k$, using $\omega = vk$. Shows the phase depends on $x$ and $t$ only through $x - vt$: the shape moves at $v$.

    Use it when: Proving a wave travels, or showing it solves the wave equation.

  3. $2\pi\left(\dfrac{x}{\lambda} - \dfrac{t}{T}\right) + \phi$

    What it says: Substitute $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\omega = 2\pi/T$. Counts cycles: $x/\lambda$ wavelengths along, $t/T$ periods elapsed.

    Use it when: A textbook writes the wave this way and you need $k$ and $\omega$.

  4. $\omega t - kx - \phi$

    What it says: The negative of the standard phase. Because $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$, it describes the same wave (with sine, the sign flips the wave over).

    Use it when: Matching an answer written with $\omega t$ first, as in Route B of PS6 · 1.

Intuition

Points with the same phase are doing the same thing at the same moment, e.g. all the crests. Following a point of constant phase is following a crest, and that crest moves at $v$: set $kx - \omega t = \text{const}$, differentiate, $dx/dt = \omega/k$.

Finding the phase constant

Evaluate at the given moment, usually $x = 0, t = 0$, where the phase is just $\phi$: $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. Maximum: $\phi = 0$. Minimum: $\phi = \pi$. Zero and moving up or down: $\phi = \pm\pi/2$ (check with the velocity).

Picture
Sine vs cosine

$\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$: a sine is a cosine with phase constant $-\pi/2$. Choosing one or the other only changes $\phi$.

What depends on it
  • Wave speed: following constant phase gives $v = \omega/k$.
  • Superposition: waves in phase add, out of phase ($\pi$ apart) cancel.
  • Driven oscillator (Ch. 3): the phase lag of the response behind the driver.
Where it sits

Phase is central to interference, diffraction, holography, AC circuits (voltage vs current phase) and quantum mechanics (phase of the wave function).

Often confused with

The phase constant $\phi$ alone. The phase is the whole angle $kx - \omega t + \phi$; $\phi$ is only its value at $x = 0$, $t = 0$.

Wave speed — how fast the shape moves
On the map: a descriptor of a wave, set by the medium's properties and set-up
▸
Definition

The speed at which the wave shape (a crest, say) travels through the medium. Units: m/s.

Why it is defined this way

It is the one number that connects the time pattern to the space pattern. Defined as the speed of a point of constant phase (a crest), it is what you would measure by timing a crest between two marks.

Where it comes from

From Newton’s second law applied to a small piece of string: the net tension force on a curved piece is $T\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2\,dx$, its mass is $\mu\,dx$, so $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = (T/\mu)\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. The constant $T/\mu$ is $v^2$.

How the three forms relate
$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k}$$
  1. $\lambda/T$ is the definition: in one period $T$ a crest moves one wavelength $\lambda$; speed is distance ÷ time.
  2. $f\lambda$ counts cycles: $f = 1/T$, so $\lambda/T = \lambda\cdot\tfrac{1}{T} = f\lambda$. (cycles per second) × (metres per cycle) = metres per second.
  3. $\omega/k$ counts radians: $\omega = 2\pi f$ and $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, so $\dfrac{\omega}{k} = \dfrac{2\pi f}{2\pi/\lambda} = f\lambda$. The $2\pi$ cancels: (rad/s) ÷ (rad/m) = m/s.
More forms of v, talked out

The three above are the general relations. These give $v$ from the medium or from other information.

  1. $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$

    What it says: Speed on a stretched string: restoring (tension) over inertia (mass per metre), the same pattern as $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$.

    Use it when: A string problem gives tension and mass per length (PS7 · 1a). string

    Example: 200 N, 0.04 kg/m: $v = \sqrt{5000} = 70.7$ m/s.

  2. $v = c,\quad v \approx 340\ \text{m/s}$

    What it says: Fixed speeds of particular media: light (any electromagnetic wave) in vacuum, and sound in air.

    Use it when: Light, microwaves, radio, lasers: $c$. Sound: 340 m/s (PS7 · 1c, 2b, 4).

  3. $v = 2Lf_1$

    What it says: From $f_1 = v/(2L)$, multiply both sides by $2L$. The fundamental and the length together reveal the speed, even when $T$ and $\mu$ are unknown.

    Use it when: A string’s fundamental and length are given and you need its other modes or a new length (PS7 · 3c).

  4. $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt}\Big|_{\text{phase const}}$

    What it says: Follow one crest: $kx - \omega t = $ const; differentiate with respect to $t$: $k\,dx/dt - \omega = 0$, so $dx/dt = \omega/k$. This is why $\omega/k$ is the speed of the shape.

    Use it when: Explaining why $v = \omega/k$, or finding the speed of a less obvious pattern.

What each variable does
  • $f$, $\omega$: time — how fast one point oscillates; set by the source.
  • $\lambda$, $k$: space — how the shape repeats along the medium.
  • $v$: set by the medium. So $\lambda = v/f$ follows; entering a new medium, $f$ stays and $\lambda$ changes.
Which form to use
  • Given $f$ or $\lambda$ in Hz and metres: $v = f\lambda$ (PS6 · 3).
  • Given an equation $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$: read $k$, $\omega$ and use $v = \omega/k$ (PS6 · 2).
What sets it

The medium. On a string, $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ (tension over mass per length). Light in vacuum, $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Sound in air, about 340 m/s.

Watch out

Wave speed is not the speed of the medium. Each point of a string moves up and down with velocity $\partial y/\partial t$, which changes all the time and is unrelated to $v$. The shape moves along; the string does not.

What depends on it
  • Wavelength from frequency, $\lambda = v/f$.
  • Normal-mode frequencies $f_n = nv/(2L)$ (Ch. 6).
  • Time for a shape to return, $2L/v$.
Where it sits

Every medium has its own wave speed: about 340 m/s for sound in air, 1500 m/s in water, $c$ for light. The pattern $\sqrt{\text{restoring}/\text{inertia}}$ recurs across all of them.

Example

PS7 · 1(a): $T = 200$ N, $\mu = 0.04$ kg/m: $v = \sqrt{200/0.04} = \sqrt{5000} \approx 70.7$ m/s.

Sine, cosine and tangent — what they actually describe
On the map: a mathematical tool used inside moves
▸
Definition

For an angle $\theta$ on a circle of radius 1: $\cos\theta$ is the horizontal coordinate of the point, $\sin\theta$ the vertical coordinate, and $\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta$ the slope of the radius.

The picture

Put a point on a circle of radius 1 and let it go around. The angle $\theta$ says how far around it has gone. Then $\cos\theta$ is the point's horizontal position and $\sin\theta$ is its height. That is the whole definition.

Why they describe oscillation

Let the point go around at a steady rate, so $\theta = \omega t$. Watch only its shadow on the horizontal axis: it slides back and forth between $+1$ and $-1$, fastest in the middle, stopping at the ends. That shadow is $\cos\omega t$, and it is exactly SHM. Simple harmonic motion is the shadow of steady circular motion. This is why sin and cos, and nothing else, describe springs, pendulums and waves.

Same motion, two shadows
  • $\cos$ is the shadow on the horizontal axis; $\sin$ is the shadow on the vertical axis. One circle, viewed from two sides a quarter-turn apart.
  • So $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$: the vertical shadow is the horizontal one a quarter cycle later. You were right that they are the same thing shifted; the circle is why.
  • When one is at its extreme, the other is passing through zero. For an oscillator: at the turning point the position is extreme and the speed is zero.
  • Each is the other's rate of change: $\frac{d}{d\theta}\cos\theta = -\sin\theta$, $\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$. The velocity of the horizontal shadow is set by the vertical position. That is why $x = A\cos\omega t$ has velocity $-A\omega\sin\omega t$.
  • $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ is Pythagoras on the triangle in the figure: the point is always distance 1 from the centre.
Tangent
  • $\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta$ = height ÷ horizontal position = the slope of the radius line.
  • The name: extend the radius until it hits the vertical line touching the circle at $x = 1$ (a tangent line). The height where it hits is $\tan\theta$.
  • Tangent is not an oscillation. As the radius turns toward vertical ($\theta \to \pi/2$), its slope grows without limit; past vertical it comes back from $-\infty$. It repeats every $\pi$, not $2\pi$.
  • Small angles: $\tan\theta \approx \sin\theta \approx \theta$ (radians), since for a tiny angle the arc, the height and the tangent segment are all nearly the same length.
Where tangent shows up in this course
  • Finding a phase from two shadows. A ratio of a sine part to a cosine part is a tangent. From $x_0 = A\cos\phi$ and $v_0 = -\omega A\sin\phi$: $\tan\phi = -v_0/(\omega x_0)$. See fit A and phi.
  • Driven oscillators (Ch. 3): the lag of the response behind the driving force is given by $\tan\delta = \gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$, again a ratio of two parts.
  • Slopes and angles: the slope of a string at a point is $\partial y/\partial x = \tan$ of the angle it makes with the horizontal.
  • Because $\tan$ repeats every $\pi$, $\tan^{-1}$ gives one of two angles; check the quadrant with the signs of the sine and cosine parts.
What depends on it
  • Every solution of SHM and every sinusoidal wave.
  • Sum identities, which turn two travelling waves into a standing wave (Ch. 6).
  • Phase and phase constants.
Where it sits

Trigonometric functions are the basic periodic functions; Fourier analysis builds every periodic shape from sines and cosines.

Often confused with

Sine and cosine are the same curve shifted by a quarter cycle: $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$.

Radian — the natural unit of angle
On the map: a unit: how angles (and phases) are measured
▸
Definition

The angle at which the arc of a circle equals its radius. A full turn is $2\pi$ rad. Radians are dimensionless.

In plain words

An angle measured by how much arc it sweeps on a circle of radius 1. Halfway round is $\pi$; all the way round is $2\pi$.

Where it comes from

Arc length $s = r\theta$ holds exactly when $\theta$ is in radians. That is the definition: $\theta = s/r$, a ratio of two lengths, hence dimensionless.

Why physics uses it

Only in radians are $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ for small angles and $\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$. Every cycle-counting quantity becomes radian-based: $\omega = 2\pi f$, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.

What depends on it
  • $\omega$ and $k$ (radians per second, per metre).
  • Derivatives of sin and cos without extra factors.
  • Small-angle approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ (only true in radians).
Where it sits

Radians are the default unit of angle in all of calculus and physics; degrees are a human convention (360 from ancient astronomy).

Example

$90° = \pi/2$ rad; $1$ rad $\approx 57.3°$.

Often confused with

Cycles. One cycle is $2\pi$ rad, which is why $\omega = 2\pi f$.

Partial derivative — change one variable, hold the other
On the map: a mathematical tool: the rate of change in one variable with the others held fixed
▸
Definition

For $y(x,t)$, $\partial y/\partial t$ is the derivative with respect to $t$ treating $x$ as a constant; $\partial y/\partial x$ treats $t$ as a constant.

Why it is defined this way

A wave depends on two variables, $x$ and $t$. To ask “how fast does this point move?” you must hold $x$ fixed and change only $t$. The partial derivative makes that precise.

Meaning for a wave
  • $\partial y/\partial t$: the velocity of one point of the medium (time pattern).
  • $\partial y/\partial x$: the slope of the frozen shape (space pattern).
  • Second derivatives: acceleration of a point, and curvature of the shape. The wave equation says these are proportional.
Example

$y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$: $\partial y/\partial t = -\omega A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, $\partial y/\partial x = kA\cos(kx-\omega t)$. The chain rule brings out the coefficient of the variable you differentiate by.

What depends on it

Definition

Damping coefficient $b$ divided by mass. Units: s⁻¹. It is the rate at which energy decays: $E = E_0e^{-\gamma t}$.

Why it is defined this way

Dividing by mass puts the damped equation in a standard form, $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$, where both constants are rates (s⁻¹ and s⁻²), so systems of very different size can be compared.

Where it comes from

Drag force $-b\dot x$ in Newton’s law: $m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x$. Divide every term by $m$: $\ddot x + (b/m)\dot x + (k/m)x = 0$. Name $\gamma = b/m$.

Intuition

$b$ says how hard friction pushes per unit velocity; $m$ says how hard the object is to slow; their ratio is how quickly friction actually slows it. Amplitude decays at half this rate, $e^{-\gamma t/2}$, because energy goes as amplitude squared.

Picture
What depends on it
  • Which regime: light ($\gamma < 2\omega_0$), critical ($=$), heavy ($>$).
  • Energy decay time $\tau = 1/\gamma$ and quality factor $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$.
  • Width of a resonance (Ch. 3).
Where it sits

Damping rates describe shock absorbers, the ring-down of a bell, the width of spectral lines and losses in circuits ($\gamma = R/L$ for an LCR circuit).

Example

A mass of 0.5 kg with $b = 0.1$ kg/s: $\gamma = 0.2$ s⁻¹, so energy falls by $e$ in 5 s.

The decay rate of the amplitude, which is $\gamma/2$.

Definition

A wave pattern that does not travel: a fixed shape $\sin kx$ whose size oscillates in time, $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$. It is the sum of two equal travelling waves going in opposite directions.

Picture
Why it matters

Any wave confined between boundaries settles into standing waves. That is why bounded systems (strings, pipes, cavities, atoms) have a discrete set of natural frequencies.

Where it comes from

Add $A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ and $A\sin(kx + \omega t)$ using $\sin P + \sin Q = 2\sin\frac{P+Q}{2}\cos\frac{P-Q}{2}$: $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$ (standing wave card).

Intuition

Shake a rope tied to a wall. Your wave goes in, comes back inverted, and meets the next one you send. At some points the two always cancel (nodes); midway between they always reinforce (antinodes). The crests no longer move along the rope; the rope just pulses up and down in loops.

Where it appears

Guitar and violin strings, organ pipes, laser cavities, microwave ovens, and (later in the course) the electron in a box, whose allowed energies come from the same “whole number of half-wavelengths” rule.

Often confused with

A travelling wave, $f(x - vt)$, where the shape moves. In a standing wave energy does not flow along the string on average; it sloshes between the stretch near nodes and the motion at antinodes.

What depends on it
  • Normal modes and harmonics.
  • Node spacing measurements ($\lambda/2$).
  • Laser cavity modes.
Example

The relation in one line

Two travelling waves with the same amplitude, wavelength and frequency, moving in opposite directions, add up to a standing wave. Read backwards, a standing wave is those two travelling waves. They are not two different things but two ways of describing the same motion.

Where it comes from

Recall (sum to product): a sum of two cosines equals 2 times the cosine of the average of the two angles, times the cosine of half their difference (sum to product). Our two angles are $kx - \omega t$ and $kx + \omega t$. Average: $\frac{(kx - \omega t) + (kx + \omega t)}{2} = kx$. Half the difference: $\frac{(kx - \omega t) - (kx + \omega t)}{2} = -\omega t$, and $\cos(-\omega t) = \cos\omega t$. So $$A\cos(kx - \omega t) + A\cos(kx + \omega t) = 2A\cos kx\,\cos\omega t.$$ The right side has $x$ and $t$ in separate factors: a fixed shape $\cos kx$ whose size $2A\cos\omega t$ grows and shrinks. Where $\cos kx = 0$ the string never moves: the nodes. Read in the other direction, the same identity writes one travelling wave as two standing waves: $\cos(kx - \omega t) = \cos kx\cos\omega t + \sin kx\sin\omega t$.

What they share, and what differs
  • Shared: the wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ and frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$. The standing wave's nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart, where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the travelling waves that make it.
  • Speed: each travelling wave moves at $v = \omega/k$, set by the medium. The standing pattern does not move anywhere; its "speed" is zero, but $v$ still links its $f$ and $\lambda$ through $v = f\lambda$.
  • Energy: a travelling wave carries energy along the string ($\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2$). In a standing wave the two travelling waves carry equal power in opposite directions, so on average nothing flows; energy sloshes between motion (at antinodes) and stretch (near nodes).
  • Every piece: in a travelling wave every piece swings with the same amplitude, one after another (the phase moves along). In a standing wave all pieces swing in step, with amplitude $2A|\cos kx|$ depending on position.
Why strings with ends make standing waves

A wave travelling toward a fixed end is reflected back, inverted. The incoming and reflected waves have the same $k$ and $\omega$ and move in opposite directions, so together they form a standing wave. On a string fixed at both ends the reflections keep bouncing; only wavelengths that fit a whole number of halves into $L$ build up consistently, which is why a clamped string vibrates in normal modes. Each mode is a pair of travelling waves bouncing back and forth.

Unequal amplitudes: partly standing

If the reflected wave is weaker ($RA$ with $R \lt 1$), the sum is a standing wave of amplitude $2RA$ plus a travelling wave of amplitude $(1-R)A$. There are no true nodes, only places of smallest swing, and the ratio of largest to smallest swing is $(1+R)/(1-R)$ (partial reflection, PS8 Problem 2). $R = 0$ is a pure travelling wave; $R = 1$ a pure standing wave.

Definition

Definition

A normal mode is a way the whole system can vibrate with every point moving at one single frequency. For a string fixed at both ends, mode $n$ has $n$ loops, wavelength $2L/n$ and frequency $nf_1$. The mode number $n$ counts the loops.

From the ground up

  1. A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in certain patterns.

    Pluck a guitar string. The ends are tied down and can never move. A wave runs along the string, reflects at each end and comes back; the outgoing and reflected waves add. Most wavelengths cancel themselves out after a few reflections. Only those that fit the string exactly survive as a steady pattern.

    Recall: a point that never moves is a node, and neighbouring nodes are half a wavelength apart. Both ends must be nodes, so a whole number of half-wavelengths must fit in $L$: $$L = n\,\frac{\lambda}{2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

  2. A mode is one of those allowed patterns.

    A normal mode (usually just “mode”) is a way of vibrating in which every point moves at one single frequency, all in step: the shape stays the same and only grows, shrinks and flips. Each mode is labelled by its mode number $n$, the number of loops (half-wavelengths) between the ends.

    ModePictureWavelengthFrequency
    1one loop$\lambda_1 = 2L$$f_1 = \dfrac{v}{2L}$
    2two loops, node in the middle$\lambda_2 = L$$f_2 = 2f_1$
    3three loops$\lambda_3 = \tfrac{2L}{3}$$f_3 = 3f_1$

    Where the frequencies come from: $f = v/\lambda$ with $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ gives $f_n = \dfrac{nv}{2L} = nf_1$.

  3. The fundamental is the first mode, $n = 1$.

    The simplest pattern: one loop, nodes only at the two ends. Its wavelength $2L$ is the longest that fits, so its frequency is the lowest the string can make. It is the base: every other mode is a whole-number multiple of it, $f_n = nf_1$, and it is the pitch you hear (a string “playing middle C” has its fundamental at 262 Hz).

  4. Harmonic is another name for the same modes.

    Mode $n$ is the $n$th harmonic; the fundamental is the first harmonic. A real plucked string vibrates in many modes at once: the fundamental sets the note, the mix of higher harmonics gives the instrument its sound.

  5. Using it in a problem.

    “Fundamental frequency of 262 Hz” means mode $n = 1$ is at 262 Hz. One loop means half a wavelength fits in the wire: $L = \lambda_1/2$, so $L = \dfrac{v}{2f_1}$ (PS7 · 1b).

The shape of mode $n$

If $n$ half-wavelengths fit in $L$, each is $L/n$ long, so $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, and a sine of that wavelength is $$\sin\left(\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda_n}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right).$$ At $x = L$ the argument is $n\pi$, and $\sin(n\pi) = 0$ for every whole $n$: both ends stay still. So $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ is the shape of mode $n$, the height of the string at $x$ up to an overall size; its nodes are where $n\pi x/L$ is a multiple of $\pi$, at $x = L/n, 2L/n, \dots$ Asking "does mode $n$ have a node at $x = a$?" is asking whether $\sin(n\pi a/L) = 0$ (used in touching a string).

Names

$n = 1$: the fundamental (first harmonic), the lowest frequency, which sets the pitch. $n = 2, 3, \dots$: the second, third, … harmonics. An overtone counts from the fundamental: the second harmonic is the first overtone.

Why only certain frequencies

Both ends must be nodes, and nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart, so only wavelengths that fit a whole number of halves into $L$ survive. Other wavelengths would need the end to move. Fixing the ends turns a continuous range of possible waves into a discrete list: this is the first appearance of “quantization” in the course.

Superposition

A general motion of the string (a pluck, a bow stroke) is a sum of normal modes, each oscillating at its own frequency. The mix of harmonics is what makes a violin and a flute sound different at the same pitch.

What depends on it
  • Every PS7 calculation: lengths from a fundamental, successive modes, counting harmonics, cavity modes.
  • Repeat time of a plucked shape, $2L/v$: all modes return together after one fundamental period.
  • Timbre: the mix of modes present.
Example

A string with $f_1 = 440$ Hz: modes at 440, 880, 1320, … Hz. Successive modes with $\lambda = 0.60$ m and $0.40$ m are $n = 2$ and $n = 3$ of a 0.60 m string.

Where it sits

Normal modes are how physics analyses anything that vibrates: bridges, buildings, molecules, drumheads, the electromagnetic field in a cavity. In quantum mechanics, the allowed energy levels of a particle in a box are the normal modes of its wave function.

Often confused with

A mode is a pattern of motion, not a single point; and mode number $n$ is not the number of nodes (a fixed–fixed string in mode $n$ has $n+1$ nodes counting the ends).

Doppler broadening (background for PS7 · 4)
On the map: an effect of motion: the frequency received changes when source or observer moves
▸
Definition

The spreading of a spectral line over a range of frequencies because the emitting atoms move at different speeds toward and away from the observer.

In plain words

An atom emits one exact frequency when it is at rest. Moving toward you, its light arrives slightly higher in frequency; moving away, slightly lower (the Doppler effect, the same shift as an ambulance siren passing). A hot gas has atoms moving every way at many speeds, so instead of one sharp line you see a bell-shaped spread.

Where the size comes from

For speeds much less than $c$, the Doppler shift is $\Delta\nu/\nu_0 \approx v/c$. Thermal speeds of atoms in a gas are about $10^2$–$10^3$ m/s, so $v/c \sim 10^{-6}$: for visible light ($\nu_0 \sim 5\times10^{14}$ Hz) the spread is around $10^9$ Hz, a few GHz. That matches PS7 · 4: $\Delta\nu = 4.55\times10^9$ Hz at $\nu_0 = 4.74\times10^{14}$ Hz, a ratio of about $10^{-5}$. Hotter gas or lighter atoms move faster and give a wider line.

Why it matters for a laser

The atoms can only amplify light inside this width. The cavity separately allows only its comb of modes, $c/(2L)$ apart. The laser runs on the modes that fall under the bell (method). To get a single frequency, make the cavity short enough that only one mode fits.

How important for this course

Background only. Problem 4 gives you $\Delta\nu$; you never compute it. The Doppler effect itself (moving sources and observers) is a standard wave topic and may come up later in King.

Where it sits

Astronomers use line widths to measure the temperatures of stars and gas clouds; line shifts give how fast galaxies recede (redshift). Laser cooling slows atoms to shrink exactly this broadening.

Often confused with

The Doppler shift of a whole source (one moving object: the line moves). Broadening is many atoms at many speeds: the line widens.

concept
$$\text{confined wave} \;\Rightarrow\; n\,\frac{\lambda}{2} = L \;\Rightarrow\; \text{a list, not a range}$$
Confinement makes things discrete
On the map: a set-up condition (walls, ends) that makes the allowed motions discrete
▸
Definition

A wave that is trapped between two boundaries can only take shapes that satisfy the boundary conditions at both ends. For two fixed ends this means a whole number of half-wavelengths must fit in the length $L$: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Every quantity that depends on the wavelength (frequency, momentum, energy) is then restricted to a list of separate values. This is what discrete or quantized means.

In plain words

A free wave can have any wavelength. Close it in, and it has to fit. Things that have to fit come in sizes: one half-wave, two half-waves, three; never two and a half.

One rule, four systems

The same line, $n\lambda/2 = L$, has appeared four times in this course:

  1. A guitar string (PS7, PS8): $f_n = nv/(2L)$. The notes a string can play.
  2. A laser cavity (PS7): $\nu_n = nc/(2L)$. The colours a laser can emit.
  3. A microwave oven (PS7): the standing wave of the field has fixed nodes, the cold spots.
  4. An electron in a box (PS8 Problem 5): $p_n = nh/(2L)$, so $E_n = n^2h^2/(8mL^2)$. The energies an electron is allowed to have.

Nothing about the boundary condition knows whether the wave is on steel, light, or an electron. The discreteness comes from the fitting, not from the material.

Why the ladders differ

The string's frequencies are evenly spaced ($f_n = nf_1$) and the electron's energies grow as $n^2$. Both start from $\lambda_n = 2L/n$; they differ in how frequency (or energy) depends on wavelength.

  • String: the speed $v$ is the same for every wavelength, so $f = v/\lambda \propto n$.
  • Electron: the energy is kinetic, $E = p^2/(2m)$, and $p = h/\lambda \propto n$, so $E \propto n^2$.

So the spacing of a ladder tells you how the medium relates frequency to wavelength. A medium where speed depends on wavelength is called dispersive; matter waves are dispersive, strings (ideally) are not. [Interpretation beyond King: this way of comparing the two is standard in quantum mechanics texts, not stated in Ch. 6.]

Consequences worth noticing
  • No zero. $n = 0$ would mean no half-wavelengths: no wave at all. So the lowest allowed energy is $E_1 \gt 0$. A confined particle can never be completely at rest. This leftover is called zero-point energy.
  • Smaller box, larger energy. $E_1 \propto 1/L^2$. Squeezing a wave into less room forces a shorter wavelength, hence more momentum. Confinement costs energy.
  • Size sets colour. Tiny semiconductor crystals (quantum dots) emit light whose colour is set by their size, because their electron levels are set by $L$. Smaller dots emit bluer light. [Real dots are three-dimensional and not perfect boxes; the $1/L^2$ trend is the box model's prediction.]
  • Spectra. Atoms absorb and emit only certain colours because their electrons occupy discrete standing-wave levels and jump between them. A real atom is a three-dimensional Coulomb well, not a box, but the reason the levels are discrete is the same: the electron's wave is confined.
Where it sits

"Quantum" means "comes in discrete amounts". The particle in a box shows that much of quantum discreteness is not a new principle added to physics: it is the old standing-wave condition of Ch. 6 applied to a wave that happens to describe matter (matter waves).

History

The rule that a string's pitch rises with tension and falls with length and mass was stated as laws by Marin Mersenne in Harmonie universelle (1636); the whole-number ratios of string lengths for musical intervals (2:1 for the octave) are traditionally credited to Pythagoras. In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed in his doctoral thesis that electrons have a wavelength $\lambda = h/p$, and suggested that the allowed electron orbits in Bohr's atom are those that fit a whole number of wavelengths. Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer observed electron diffraction in 1927, confirming the wavelength. Erwin Schrödinger's wave equation (1926) made "allowed energies = allowed standing waves" exact. Reading this as one line from strings to atoms is an interpretation of that history.

Partial reflection: a wave that is partly standing, partly travelling

Definition

When a boundary reflects only a fraction $R$ of the incoming amplitude ($0 \le R \le 1$), the incoming wave $A\cos(\omega t - kx)$ and the reflected wave $RA\cos(\omega t + kx)$ do not cancel completely anywhere. The sum is a mixture: a standing wave of amplitude set by $RA$, plus a leftover travelling wave $(1-R)A$ that keeps moving toward the boundary.

In plain words

Full reflection makes perfect nodes, points that never move. Partial reflection makes "weak spots" instead: places where the motion is small but not zero, because there is not enough reflected wave to cancel the incoming one.

The two limits
  • $R = 1$ (fixed end, perfect mirror): pure standing wave. Minimum amplitude $0$, maximum $2A$.
  • $R = 0$ (no reflection, all energy absorbed): pure travelling wave. The amplitude is $A$ everywhere.
  • In between: maximum $(1+R)A$, minimum $(1-R)A$. Their ratio, $(1+R)/(1-R)$, is the standing wave ratio (SWR).
Why it matters

The ratio can be measured by walking a detector along the wave, and it gives $R$ back: rearranging, $R = (\text{SWR}-1)/(\text{SWR}+1)$. Engineers use exactly this on radio antennas and cables (VSWR) to tell how much power is bounced back instead of delivered. A ratio near 1 means almost everything gets through.

A caution on energy

Splitting the sum into "standing" plus "$(1-R)A$ travelling" is a regrouping of terms, convenient for amplitudes. The net energy flowing toward the boundary is the incoming energy minus the reflected energy, proportional to $A^2 - (RA)^2 = A^2(1-R^2)$, not to $(1-R)^2A^2$.

concept
$$p = -B\,\frac{\partial \xi}{\partial x}$$
Sound has two descriptions: how far the air moves, and how hard it is squeezed
On the map: two descriptions of one motion: displacement of the air and pressure change
▸
Definition

In a sound wave each thin layer of air moves back and forth along the direction of travel. Its displacement is $\xi(x,t)$. Where neighbouring layers move by different amounts, the air between them is squeezed or stretched, and the pressure changes by $p(x,t)$ above or below atmospheric. The two are tied by $p = -B\,\partial\xi/\partial x$, where $B$ is the bulk modulus (the stiffness of air against compression).

In plain words

Pressure changes where the motion is uneven, not where it is large. If every layer moves by the same amount, the air just shifts and nothing is squeezed. If the layer in front moves less than the one behind, the gap shrinks and the pressure rises.

The consequence

In a standing sound wave the two descriptions are shifted by a quarter wavelength. Where the air moves most (displacement antinode), neighbouring layers move together, the slope $\partial\xi/\partial x$ is zero, and the pressure does not change (pressure node). Where the air does not move (displacement node), the layers on either side move toward it and away from it in turn, the slope is largest, and the pressure swings most (pressure antinode).

Ends of a pipe
  • Closed end: the wall stops the air, $\xi = 0$: displacement node, so pressure antinode.
  • Open end: the air meets the open atmosphere, which holds the pressure at atmospheric, $p = 0$: pressure node, so displacement antinode.

This is PS8 Problem 4 (method). It decides which notes a pipe can play (pipes).

Why it matters

Your eardrum responds to pressure, so you hear the pressure wave. A flute (open at both ends) and a clarinet (closed at the reed end) have the same kind of air column but different end conditions, and that is the main reason they sound different: the closed–open pipe has only odd harmonics.

Often confused with

"Node" without saying of what. In a pipe always say "displacement node" or "pressure node": they are at different places.

Touching a string: a new node acts as a filter on the modes
On the map: a set-up condition that filters the modes

Modes add up: a plucked string and a touch, animated

Definition

A plucked string vibrates in many normal modes at once. Touching it lightly at a distance $a$ from one end stops the motion at that point, so it forces a node there. Each mode either already has a node at $a$ (it is untouched and keeps ringing) or it moves at $a$ (the finger damps it out). The modes that survive are those with $\sin(n\pi a/L) = 0$, that is, those for which $na/L$ is a whole number.

In plain words

The finger asks every mode one question: "do you need this point to move?" Modes that answer no are left alone; the rest die.

Touching is not pressing

Pressing the string down to a fret makes a new fixed end: the shorter piece vibrates with a whole new set of modes (PS7 Problem 3). Touching lightly leaves the whole string vibrating on both sides of the finger; it only removes modes. Guitarists call the result "harmonics": touching at the midpoint removes all odd modes and leaves $2f_1, 4f_1, \dots$, a note one octave up.

The rule

Write $a/L$ as a fraction $p/q$ in lowest terms. Mode $n$ has a node at $a$ exactly when $n$ is a multiple of $q$. The surviving frequencies are $qf_1, 2qf_1, 3qf_1, \dots$, and the lowest one heard is $qf_1$.

concept
$$v_y = \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} \quad\text{vs}\quad v = \frac{\omega}{k}$$
Transverse velocity of a piece of string versus the speed of the wave
On the map: the velocity of a piece of the system (a motion), not a descriptor of the wave
▸
Definition

For a wave $y(x,t)$ on a string, the transverse velocity is $\partial y/\partial t$: how fast the piece of string at position $x$ moves up and down at time $t$. Take the derivative with respect to $t$ while holding $x$ fixed, because you are following one piece. The wave speed $v = \omega/k$ is how fast the pattern (a crest, say) moves along the string.

For a sinusoid

If $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$, the chain rule (the inside derivative with respect to $t$ is $-\omega$) gives $$\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = A\omega\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi).$$ Its largest value is $A\omega$. It depends on the amplitude and the frequency, which come from the source; $v$ does not.

Keep them apart
  • $v$ is a descriptor of the wave and is set by the medium: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.
  • $\partial y/\partial t$ is the velocity of a piece of the system. It changes from point to point and moment to moment, and is zero at a crest.
  • They are perpendicular: the pattern moves along $x$, the pieces move along $y$.

concept
$$\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\mu v\,\omega^2A^2$$
Energy and power carried by a travelling wave
On the map: what a motion carries: energy and power, transported by the wave
▸
Definition

A sinusoidal wave $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t)$ on a string carries, averaged over time, an energy per unit length $$\Big\langle \frac{dE}{dx}\Big\rangle = \tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2,$$ half of it kinetic (the pieces move) and half potential (the string is stretched where it slopes). That energy moves with the wave at speed $v$, so the average power past a point is $$\langle P\rangle = \Big\langle \frac{dE}{dx}\Big\rangle\, v = \tfrac12\mu v\,\omega^2A^2.$$

Where it comes from

Kinetic: a piece of length $dx$ has mass $\mu\,dx$ and speed $\partial y/\partial t = A\omega\sin(kx-\omega t)$, so kinetic energy $\tfrac12\mu\,dx\,A^2\omega^2\sin^2(\cdots)$. Recall: the average of $\sin^2$ over a cycle is $\tfrac12$ (average of sine squared). So the average kinetic energy per length is $\tfrac14\mu\omega^2A^2$. The potential energy averages the same, giving $\tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2$ in all.

Reading it
  • Energy and power go as $A^2$ and as $\omega^2$: double either and the power is four times larger.
  • The medium enters through $\mu v = \sqrt{\mu T}$, called the impedance $Z$ of the string: $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12 Z\omega^2A^2$.
  • Half the amplitude and twice the frequency: $A^2\omega^2$ is unchanged, so on the same string the power is unchanged.

concept
$$r = \frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2},\qquad t = \frac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2}$$
A wave reaching a junction between two strings: reflection and transmission
On the map: a move (matching across a boundary) applied where two media meet
▸
The situation

Two strings with mass per unit length $\mu_1$ (left) and $\mu_2$ (right) are joined at $x = 0$ under one tension $T$. An incident wave $A_1\cos(k_1x - \omega t)$ arrives from the left. Part of it continues as a transmitted wave $A_T\cos(k_2x - \omega t)$; part returns as a reflected wave $A_R\cos(k_1x + \omega t)$.

What carries across
  • Frequency is the same on both sides: the joint is one point, so both strings move with it, at the rate the source sets. $\omega_2 = \omega_1$.
  • Speed and wavenumber change: each string sets its own speed, $v_i = \sqrt{T/\mu_i}$, so $k_i = \omega/v_i = \omega\sqrt{\mu_i/T}$.
  • The reflected wave stays on the left string, so it has $k_1$ and $\omega$, moving in $-x$.
The two matching conditions

Displacement is continuous at $x = 0$: the string does not break, so the two sides are at the same height. Slope is continuous at $x = 0$: the joint is massless, so the tension forces on it from the two sides must balance at every instant, which needs the same slope on both sides (a kink would put a net force on zero mass).

At $x = 0$ these give $$A_1 + A_R = A_T,\qquad k_1(A_1 - A_R) = k_2A_T.$$ Solving: $$r = \frac{A_R}{A_1} = \frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2},\qquad t = \frac{A_T}{A_1} = \frac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2} = 1 + r.$$ Since $k \propto \sqrt{\mu}$ at fixed $T$ and $\omega$, you can write the same with $\sqrt{\mu_1}$, $\sqrt{\mu_2}$ in place of $k_1$, $k_2$.

Power: reflected and transmitted fractions

Power goes as $\tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2 = \tfrac12 Z\omega^2A^2$ with $Z = \mu v = \sqrt{\mu T}$. The reflected wave is on the same string, so the reflected fraction is $r^2$. The transmitted wave is on the other string, so its fraction carries the ratio of impedances: $$\mathcal R = r^2,\qquad \mathcal T = \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}\,t^2,\qquad \mathcal R + \mathcal T = 1.$$ The last line is energy conservation: what arrives either goes back or goes on.

Limits that check it
  • Same strings ($k_1 = k_2$): $r = 0$, $t = 1$. Nothing reflects.
  • Right side much heavier ($k_2 \to \infty$, like a wall): $r \to -1$. Total reflection with the wave inverted, as at a fixed end.
  • Right side much lighter ($k_2 \to 0$, a free end): $r \to +1$. Total reflection, upright.
  • Going into a heavier string, $r \lt 0$: the reflection is inverted. Going into a lighter one, $r \gt 0$.

The idea

If $\mu$ changes slowly along the string, each short stretch behaves like a uniform string with its own $\mu(x)$. The local wave speed is $v(x) = \sqrt{T/\mu(x)}$, with the same tension $T$ everywhere (the string is in equilibrium, so the pull is the same all along it).

Travel time

Over a short distance $dx$ the speed is nearly constant, so the time to cross it is $dt = dx/v(x)$ (distance divided by speed). The total time is the sum of these pieces, which is the integral $$t_{\text{travel}} = \int_0^L \frac{dx}{v(x)}.$$ You cannot use $L/v$: there is no single $v$.

Speeding up or slowing down

Where the string is heavier, the same tension force acts on more mass, so each piece accelerates less and the disturbance is passed on more slowly. Larger $\mu$ means smaller $v$.

On the map: a quantity a motion carries (energy), written with a descriptor (momentum)
▸
Definition

For a particle of mass $m$ moving with momentum $p = mv$, the kinetic energy written in terms of momentum is $E = p^2/2m$.

Where it comes from

Recall: $E = \tfrac12mv^2$ and $p = mv$. Divide $p = mv$ by $m$: $v = p/m$. Substitute: $E = \tfrac12m\,(p/m)^2 = \tfrac12m\,p^2/m^2 = p^2/2m$.

Why use it

Matter waves give a particle's momentum, not its speed: $\lambda = h/p$. This form turns that momentum straight into an energy. In a box the potential energy inside is zero, so this is the whole energy.

Watch out

Do not use the string's $f_n = nv/2L$ with $E = hf$ for a particle in a box: a particle has no fixed wave speed $v$ the way a string does, so the string formula for frequency does not carry over. Go through momentum.

concept

The infinite potential well: what "a particle in a box" means
On the map: a system (a confined particle) and its set-up condition (the walls)

Definition

A potential well is a region where a particle's potential energy $V(x)$ is low, bounded by regions where it is high. In an infinite well of width $L$, $V = 0$ for $0 \lt x \lt L$ and the walls are infinitely high: no amount of energy lets the particle get out.

What the walls do to the wave

The particle can never be found beyond the walls, so its wave is zero there and, the wave being continuous, zero at the walls themselves. Those are exactly the conditions of a string clamped at both ends: $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$. So the same standing waves fit, $n\lambda/2 = L$ (confinement).

Inside

With $V = 0$ inside, no force acts there: the particle moves freely between the walls and its energy is all kinetic, $E = p^2/2m$ (kinetic energy from momentum).

On the map

The particle is the system, its mass $m$ a property; the walls are the set-up condition that selects the allowed wavelengths; the energies are what each allowed motion carries. The same route as a string's harmonics, with energy at the end instead of frequency.

In words

A sum of two cosines equals 2 times the cosine of the average of the two angles, times the cosine of half their difference. The same pattern for sines: $\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\frac{A-B}{2}$; and $\cos A - \cos B = -2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\sin\frac{A-B}{2}$.

method
Show a system is SHM and find ω
“Show that … executes simple harmonic motion”, “find the period / frequency of small oscillations”
▸
Recognize it

A system sits in stable equilibrium and is displaced a little: a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a floating body, a circuit. Any question about its period or frequency is this method.

Steps
  1. Draw the system moved a small distance $x$ from equilibrium.

    Why displaced: at equilibrium the forces cancel, so you cannot see the restoring force. Move it a little first.

    Choose a positive direction: every sign in the following steps depends on this choice.

  2. Find the forces (or torques) on it.

    Where to draw them: each force at the point where it acts: gravity at the centre of mass, tension along the string, the spring force where the spring attaches.

    Which parts matter: only the components along the direction of motion.

    For something that swings or rotates: take torques about the pivot. The pivot force passes through the pivot, so it has no torque and drops out.

  3. Apply Newton's second law: $m\ddot x = F_{\text{net}}$ (or $I\ddot\theta = \tau_{\text{net}}$).

    What it says: mass × acceleration = total force. $\ddot x$ means the second time derivative of $x$, the acceleration.

    Result: an equation linking the acceleration to the position. This is the equation of motion.

  4. Approximate if the force is not exactly proportional to $x$.

    Why: SHM needs force $\propto x$ exactly. Some forces are not: the pendulum has $\sin\theta$.

    The fix: for small angles in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$. Replace it, and the force becomes proportional to $\theta$.

  5. Match the form $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ and read off $\omega$.

    How: divide both sides by $m$ so $\ddot x$ is alone. Whatever multiplies $-x$ is $\omega^2$.

    Example: $\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\,\theta$, so $\omega^2 = g/l$. Take the square root: $\omega = \sqrt{g/l}$.

  6. Get the period and frequency: $T = 2\pi/\omega$, $f = 1/T$.

    Why $2\pi$: $\omega$ is in radians per second, and one oscillation is $2\pi$ radians.

    Check: $\omega$ should contain only constants like $k$, $m$, $g$, $l$. If the amplitude appears, an approximation was missed.

Here they're $A$ and $\phi$; equivalently it can be written $x = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$ with $a = A\cos\phi$, $b=-A\sin\phi$ (Eq. 1.15–1.17) — same solution, different-looking constants.

1.12–1.13
$$v = -\omega A\sin\omega t, \qquad a = -\omega^2 A\cos\omega t = -\omega^2 x$$
Velocity and acceleration from the displacement
▸
Origin

Straightforward differentiation of $x=A\cos\omega t$ with respect to time, once and twice. Worth noticing directly from the graphs: velocity is zero exactly when displacement is at its extremes (the turning points), and maximum exactly when displacement passes through zero — a quarter-period phase shift between the two.

1.2.5 · Energy of a Simple Harmonic Oscillator
1.19
$$E = K + U = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$$
Total mechanical energy — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $K$kinetic energy, $\tfrac12mv^2$
  • $U$potential energy stored in the spring, $\tfrac12kx^2$ (from integrating $F=-kx$, Eq. 1.18)
Derivation sketch

Start from $m\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt = -kx$, multiply through by $\mathrm dx = v\,\mathrm dt$ to get $mv\,\mathrm dv = -kx\,\mathrm dx$, then integrate both sides — the left becomes $\tfrac12mv^2$, the right $-\tfrac12kx^2$, and together they sum to a constant: the total energy $E$.

1.23
$$E = \tfrac12 kA^2$$
Total energy in terms of amplitude alone
▸
Why this matters

Substituting the general solution into $K+U$, the time-dependent $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ terms combine to exactly $1$ (Eq. 1.21–1.23), leaving a constant — confirming energy conservation directly, and showing energy scales with the square of the amplitude. Double the amplitude, quadruple the energy.

1.2.6 · Why Small Oscillations Are Always SHM
1.26–1.27
$$U(x) \approx \frac{x^2}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dx^2}\right)_{x=0}, \qquad F = -x\left(\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dx^2}\right)_{x=0}$$
Taylor expansion near equilibrium — the universal justification for SHM
▸
Reasoning

Any smooth potential well $U(x)$ can be Taylor-expanded about its minimum (Eq. 1.25). The constant term is irrelevant (potential energy has no absolute zero), and the first-derivative term vanishes exactly because $x=0$ is a minimum. What survives, to leading order, is a term proportional to $x^2$ — i.e. a parabolic well, which always produces a linear restoring force. This is the actual reason SHM is universal: any system in stable equilibrium behaves like a harmonic oscillator for sufficiently small displacements, whether it's a pendulum, a diatomic molecule, or a marble in a bowl.

1.3 · The Pendulum
1.29, 1.31
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{g}{l}\sin\theta \;\; \xrightarrow{\text{small }\theta} \;\; \frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{g}{l}\theta$$
Simple pendulum equation of motion, exact then small-angle
▸
Parameters
  • $\theta$angular displacement from vertical (rad)
  • $l$length of the pendulum (m)
  • $g$acceleration due to gravity
Why the sine needs replacing

The exact equation has $\sin\theta$, not $\theta$, so it isn't literally SHM. Expanding $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/3! + \cdots$ (Eq. 1.30) and keeping only the first term — valid once $\theta$ is small enough that higher powers are negligible — recovers the SHM form exactly.

Result: $\omega = \sqrt{g/l}$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$ (Eq. 1.32). Note $\omega$ depends on $g$ and $l$ only — not mass, not amplitude.

1.43a–c
$$E = \tfrac12\alpha v^2 + \tfrac12\beta x^2$$
The universal energy signature of SHM
▸
Parameters
  • $\alpha$the "inertia" constant (mass for a spring, $\alpha=m$; moment of inertia for a rotating system)
  • $\beta$the "stiffness" constant (spring constant $k$; or $mg/l$ for a pendulum)
Why this generalizes everything

Both the mass-on-spring energy ($\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$) and the pendulum energy ($\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12\frac{mg}{l}x^2$) are special cases of this one form. Differentiating it with respect to time always reproduces the SHM equation with $\omega=\sqrt{\beta/\alpha}$ — meaning you never need to redo this derivation for a new system: just identify its $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and read off $\omega$ directly.

1.4 · Oscillations in Electrical Circuits
1.54
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2q}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{1}{LC}q$$
The LC circuit — SHM in an electrical system
▸
Parameters
  • $q$charge on the capacitor (C)
  • $L$inductance (H)
  • $C$capacitance (F)
Origin

Kirchhoff's voltage law, $V_C + V_L = 0$, with $V_C = q/C$ and $V_L = L\,\mathrm dI/\mathrm dt = L\,\mathrm d^2q/\mathrm dt^2$ (since $I=\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt$). Same mathematical form as Eq. 1.6, with $\omega = \sqrt{1/LC}$.

The correspondence (Eq. 1.57–1.58): $q \leftrightarrow x$, $L \leftrightarrow m$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$. Every mechanical SHM result translates directly — this is why electrical circuits can be built as analogue simulations of mechanical systems.

Chapter 2 adds one term to the master equation: a velocity-proportional damping force. Everything here is organized around a single comparison — $\gamma^2/4$ (the damping) versus $\omega_0^2$ (the spring-like restoring term) — and which one wins determines whether the system oscillates at all.
Methods · how problems in this chapter are solved
method
Write the damped equation and classify the damping
“A damping force −bv acts …”, “is the motion oscillatory?”, “what value of b gives critical damping?”
▸
Recognize it

SHM plus a force proportional to velocity. The first question is always which regime it is in.

Steps
  1. Write Newton's second law with damping: $m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x$.

    The damping force: $-b\dot x$. It is proportional to the velocity $\dot x$ and always opposes it. $b$ (kg/s) is how strong it is.

  2. Divide by $m$ to get $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0$.

    New names: $\gamma = b/m$ is the damping rate (s⁻¹). $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ is the natural angular frequency squared.

    Why bother: in this standard form, every damped system (spring, pendulum, circuit) uses the same results.

  3. Compare $\gamma/2$ with $\omega_0$ to classify.

    Meaning: $\gamma/2$ is how fast the motion dies away. $\omega_0$ is how fast it wants to swing.

    Light ($\gamma/2 < \omega_0$): it swings back and forth while slowly shrinking.

    Critical ($\gamma/2 = \omega_0$): it returns to rest as fast as possible without overshooting.

    Heavy ($\gamma/2 > \omega_0$): it creeps back slowly without swinging.

  4. For light damping, write the frequency and the decay.

    Frequency: $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$, slightly lower than $\omega_0$.

    Motion: $x = A_0 e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. The cosine is the swinging; $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ is the shrinking.

Picture
Uses

2.2 damping force · 2.5 standard form · 2.7–2.8 light · 2.16 critical · 2.13 heavy

Common mistakes
  • Amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$, not $e^{-\gamma t}$. The energy decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$.
  • Critical damping is $\gamma = 2\omega_0$, not $\gamma = \omega_0$.
Go deeper

Where the regimes come from: trying $x = e^{pt}$ turns the equation into $p^2 + \gamma p + \omega_0^2 = 0$, so $p = -\gamma/2 \pm\sqrt{\gamma^2/4 - \omega_0^2}$. A negative quantity under the root gives oscillation; zero gives the repeated root (critical); positive gives two decaying exponentials (heavy). The same quadratic decides everything.

method
Get γ, τ or Q from decay data
“The amplitude falls to half in …”, “after n oscillations the energy is …”, “find the Q of …”
▸
Recognize it

You are given how fast something dies away, and asked for a damping parameter (or the reverse).

Steps
  1. Decide: is the data about amplitude or energy?

    Why it matters: amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$, energy as $e^{-\gamma t}$. Mixing them up gives an answer off by a factor of 2.

    Where the 2 comes from: energy is proportional to amplitude squared, and squaring $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ gives $e^{-\gamma t}$.

  2. Write the ratio, e.g. $A(t)/A_0 = e^{-\gamma t/2}$.

    Why a ratio: the starting amplitude $A_0$ cancels, so you only need the fraction given (for example “falls to half”).

  3. Take the natural log and solve for $\gamma$.

    How: $\ln$ undoes $e$: $\ln(A/A_0) = -\gamma t/2$. Multiply both sides by $-2/t$: $\gamma = (2/t)\ln(A_0/A)$.

    Example: amplitude halves in 3 s: $\gamma = (2/3)\ln 2 \approx 0.46$ s⁻¹.

  4. Get $\tau$ or $Q$: $\tau = 1/\gamma$, $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$.

    $\tau$: the time for the energy to drop to about 37% ($1/e$).

    $Q$: large $Q$ means many oscillations before the motion fades.

Uses

2.9 log decrement · 2.18–2.19 energy decay · 2.21–2.23 Q

Common mistakes
  • Mixing amplitude and energy decay (factor of 2).
  • Using $\log_{10}$ instead of $\ln$.
Go deeper

Physical meaning of $Q$: about $2\pi$ times the energy stored divided by the energy lost per cycle, or roughly the number of oscillations (times $\pi$) before the energy falls to $1/e$. A tuning fork has $Q$ in the thousands; a car suspension is near critical.

2.2 · The Equation of Motion with Damping
2.2
$$F_d = -bv$$
Damping force, proportional to velocity
▸
Parameters
  • $b$damping constant — force per unit velocity (N s m⁻¹)
  • $v$velocity of the mass

This linear-in-$v$ form is an approximation (valid for velocities that aren't too large), but it's the one that keeps the equation of motion linear and solvable — a damping force proportional to $v^2$ would be far harder to handle analytically.

2.5
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2} + \gamma\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$$
The damped harmonic oscillator equation — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $\omega_0^2$$=k/m$ — the natural frequency: what the system would oscillate at with zero damping
  • $\gamma$$=b/m$ — the damping parameter (Eq. 2.4)
Origin

Newton's second law with both forces present: $ma = -kx - bv$ (Eq. 2.3a), rearranged and divided through by $m$.

Everything else in this chapter is one of three cases determined by comparing $\gamma^2/4$ to $\omega_0^2$.

2.7–2.8
$$x = A_0\exp(-\gamma t/2)\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4$$
Light damping — boxed; oscillation with a decaying envelope
▸
Condition

$\gamma^2/4 < \omega_0^2$ — damping is weak enough that $\omega$ stays real, so the system still oscillates, just at a slightly lower frequency than $\omega_0$, with an amplitude that decays as $\exp(-\gamma t/2)$.

Reading the pieces
  • $A_0$initial amplitude
  • $\exp(-\gamma t/2)$the decaying envelope — not the oscillation itself, just the ceiling it decays within
  • $\cos(\omega t+\phi)$the oscillation inside that envelope, at the (slightly reduced) angular frequency $\omega$
2.9
$$\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{\gamma T}{2}\right), \qquad \ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right) = \frac{\gamma T}{2}$$
Logarithmic decrement — ratio of successive amplitude peaks
▸
Parameters
  • $A_n$amplitude of the $n$-th peak; $A_{n+1}$ the next one, one period $T$ later
Use

Since successive peaks always shrink by the same fractional amount, measuring any two consecutive peaks from a graph gives you $\gamma$ directly — a standard way to extract the damping parameter from experimental data.

2.13
$$x = A\exp[(-\gamma/2+\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2})t] + B\exp[(-\gamma/2-\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2})t]$$
Heavy (over) damping — no oscillation at all
▸
Condition

$\gamma^2/4 > \omega_0^2$ — damping dominates so strongly that the system just relaxes back to equilibrium, sluggishly, with two different exponential decay rates and no crossing back and forth.

Why no oscillation

Both exponents are real and negative (since $\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2} < \gamma/2$ always), so both terms are pure exponential decays — nothing in the solution oscillates.

2.16
$$x = A\exp(-\gamma t/2) + Bt\exp(-\gamma t/2)$$
Critical damping — fastest return to equilibrium, no overshoot
▸
Condition

$\gamma^2/4 = \omega_0^2$ exactly — the boundary case between light and heavy damping.

Why this case is special

Of all three damping regimes, this is the one that returns the system to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without ever oscillating past it. That's why it's the design target for shock absorbers, door closers, and analogue meter needles — anything that needs to settle quickly without bouncing.

2.3 · Rate of Energy Loss
2.18–2.19

Derivation sketch

Substitute the light-damping solution into $E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$; for very light damping the oscillating $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ terms again combine to $1$ (as in Ch. 1), leaving a clean exponential decay envelope.

The quality factor Q — boxed in the text
▸
Why it exists

$Q$ is a dimensionless "figure of merit" that works for any kind of oscillator — mechanical, electrical, or otherwise — regardless of what units its damping is measured in. Comparing the decay time $\tau$ (how long energy takes to dissipate) to the period $T$ (how long one oscillation takes) is the essential idea: many oscillations before dying out means high $Q$.

  • high $Q$many oscillations before the energy is significantly reduced (quartz crystal: $Q\sim10^6$)
  • low $Q$only a few oscillations, or none (paperweight on a rubber band: $Q\sim10$)

method
Write the equation of a travelling wave
“Obtain an equation describing the wave” — PS6 Problem 1
▸
Recognize it

You are given an amplitude, a frequency (or period), a speed (or wavelength), a direction, and something about the start.

Steps
  1. Choose the direction. Toward $+x$: use $kx - \omega t$. Toward $-x$: use $kx + \omega t$.

    What decides it: the sign between the $x$ term and the $t$ term.

    Why: follow one crest. A crest is where the angle inside the cosine is zero, so $kx - \omega t = 0$. Solve for $x$: $x = (\omega/k)\,t$.

    Read that result: as time $t$ increases, the crest's position $x$ increases. The crest moves toward $+x$.

    With a plus sign: $kx + \omega t = 0$ gives $x = -(\omega/k)\,t$, so the crest moves toward $-x$.

  2. Find $\omega$ from the frequency: $\omega = 2\pi f$ (or $2\pi/T$).

    What $f$ means: the source (the hand shaking the end of the string) goes up and down $f$ times per second. Every point on the string copies this: watch any single point and it completes $f$ full up-and-down cycles each second.

    Why we need $\omega$ instead: the cosine takes an angle, measured in radians. One full up-and-down cycle is one full turn of the angle, which is $2\pi$ radians.

    So: $f$ cycles per second × $2\pi$ radians per cycle $= 2\pi f$ radians per second. That is $\omega$.

    Example: $f = 10$ Hz gives $\omega = 2\pi \times 10 = 20\pi \approx 62.8$ rad/s.

  3. Find $\lambda$, then $k$: $\lambda = v/f$, then $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Check that $\omega/k = v$.

    What $\lambda$ means: freeze the wave at one instant, like a photograph. The wavelength $\lambda$ is the distance from one crest to the next.

    Why $\lambda = v/f$: during one period (one up-and-down of a point), the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength. Distance ÷ time gives speed: $v = \lambda / T = f\lambda$. Divide both sides by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.

    Why we need $k$: the cosine needs an angle, so the $x$ term must also be in radians. Moving one wavelength along the string is one full turn, $2\pi$ radians. So radians per metre is $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.

    Check: $\omega/k$ must come out equal to the wave speed $v$. If it does not, one of the numbers is wrong.

    Example: $v = 50$ m/s, $f = 10$ Hz: $\lambda = 5.0$ m, $k = 2\pi/5 = 0.4\pi$ rad/m, and $20\pi/0.4\pi = 50$ ✓.

  4. Find the phase constant $\phi$ from the starting condition.

    What $\phi$ does: it shifts the wave left or right so it starts in the right place.

    How to find it: put $x = 0$ and $t = 0$ into $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$. Everything inside the cosine vanishes except $\phi$, leaving $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$.

    Set it equal to what the problem says: “maximum upward displacement” means $y(0,0) = +A$. So $A\cos\phi = A$; divide by $A$: $\cos\phi = 1$, so $\phi = 0$.

    Other common starts: at the bottom, $\phi = \pi$; passing through zero, $\phi = \pm\pi/2$.

  5. Check by setting $x = 0$: you should get back the motion of the source.

    Why this works: at $x = 0$ the wave formula describes the end of the string, which is the source.

    Example: $y(0,t) = 0.15\cos(20\pi t)$: amplitude 0.15 m, 10 Hz, at the top at $t = 0$. That is exactly what the problem described ✓.

    If it does not match: look for a missing $2\pi$ or a wrong sign.

Uses

sine sinusoidal wave · defs definitions · speed speed · shape direction rule

Common mistakes
  • Writing $f$ where $\omega$ belongs: the argument of cos must be in radians.
  • Getting the direction backwards: $x - vt$ moves toward $+x$.
Worked examples

PS6 · 1 (King 5-2): string driven at 10 Hz, 0.15 m, 50 m/s.

Go deeper

Route B, from the source. The end at $x=0$ does SHM, $y(0,t) = A\cos\omega t$. A point at distance $x$ does the same thing a time $x/v$ later, so $y(x,t) = y(0, t - x/v) = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. This shows why a wave has the form it does: a delay that grows with distance turns oscillation in time into a pattern in space.

method
Show a function solves the wave equation
“Show that the following are solutions to the wave equation” — PS6 Problem 2
▸
Recognize it

You are handed $y(x,t)$ (or $\psi(x,y,z,t)$) and asked to verify it, or to find the condition that makes it work.

Steps
  1. Take two time derivatives: $\partial y/\partial t$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2$.

    What “partial” means: differentiate with respect to $t$ and treat $x$ as a fixed number.

    Example: $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$. The derivative of the inside, $kx - \omega t$, with respect to $t$ is $-\omega$. Chain rule: $\partial y/\partial t = -\omega A\cos(kx - \omega t)$.

    Again: $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t)$, which is $-\omega^2 y$.

  2. Take two space derivatives: $\partial y/\partial x$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$.

    Now: differentiate with respect to $x$ and treat $t$ as a fixed number.

    Example: the derivative of the inside with respect to $x$ is $k$. So $\partial y/\partial x = kA\cos(kx - \omega t)$, and $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = -k^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t) = -k^2 y$.

  3. Substitute both into $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$.

    Left side becomes $-\omega^2 y$. Right side becomes $v^2 \cdot (-k^2 y)$. So the equation reads $-\omega^2 y = -v^2 k^2 y$.

  4. Simplify to get the condition, usually $\omega/k = v$.

    Divide both sides by $-y$: $\omega^2 = v^2 k^2$. Take the square root: $\omega/k = v$.

    What it means: the function is a solution exactly when its own speed, $\omega/k$, equals the speed $v$ in the wave equation.

Uses

1d wave equation · complex complex form · 3d 3D

Common mistakes
  • Losing the chain-rule factor ($\omega$ or $k$) on each derivative.
  • Shortcut worth stating: if $y$ depends on $x$ and $t$ only through $x \pm vt$, it is a solution automatically.
Go deeper

Why the condition is always $\omega/k = v$: the wave equation contains $v$ and nothing else, so it can only accept shapes that move at $v$. A sinusoid moves at $\omega/k$, so it qualifies exactly when the two speeds agree. In 3D the same step gives $k_1^2+k_2^2+k_3^2 = \omega^2/v^2$: the speed is fixed, the direction is free.

method
Convert between λ, f, k and v
“Calculate the frequency of a wave of wavelength …” — PS6 Problem 3
▸
Recognize it

One of $\lambda$, $f$, $k$ is given; the medium tells you $v$.

Steps
  1. Find the speed from the medium.

    Why the medium: the speed is set by what the wave travels through, not by what made it.

    Values: light, radio, X-rays (all electromagnetic): $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Sound in air: about 340 m/s. A string: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.

  2. Convert to SI units (metres, seconds, hertz).

    Why: $v = f\lambda$ only gives correct numbers when all three are in SI units.

    Example: $500\ \text{nm} = 500 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$. $15\ \text{kHz} = 15\times10^3\ \text{Hz}$.

  3. Apply $v = f\lambda$ and solve for the unknown.

    To find $f$: divide both sides by $\lambda$: $f = v/\lambda$.

    To find $\lambda$: divide both sides by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.

  4. Check the size of the answer.

    Typical values: visible light is about $4$–$8\times10^{14}$ Hz. Sound at 20 Hz has $\lambda \approx 17$ m; at 15 kHz, about 2 cm.

    Why: most mistakes here are a wrong power of ten, and a quick size check catches them.

method
Read the wave off a given equation
“A wave is described by y = 0.02 sin(3x − 12t). Find its speed, wavelength …”
▸
Recognize it

An equation is given and the physical quantities are asked for. The reverse of the first method.

Steps
  1. Match the equation to $A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$ and read off $A$, $k$, $\omega$.

    How: the number in front is the amplitude $A$, the largest displacement. The number multiplying $x$ is the wavenumber $k$: how fast the phase advances along the string, in radians per metre ($k\lambda = 2\pi$). The number multiplying $t$ is the angular frequency $\omega$: how fast the phase advances in time, in radians per second ($\omega T = 2\pi$). Anything added inside is the phase constant $\phi$, where the cycle starts.

    Example: $y = 0.02\sin(3x - 12t)$: $A = 0.02$ m, $k = 3$ rad/m, $\omega = 12$ rad/s.

  2. Convert to $\lambda$, $f$, $T$, $v$.

    Use the definitions in reverse: wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$ (cycles per second), period $T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega$, speed of the pattern $v = \omega/k$.

    Example: $\lambda = 2\pi/3 \approx 2.1$ m, $f = 12/2\pi \approx 1.9$ Hz, $v = 12/3 = 4$ m/s.

  3. Read the direction from the signs.

    Rule: opposite signs on the $x$ and $t$ terms ($3x - 12t$) mean the wave moves toward $+x$. Same signs mean toward $-x$.

  • $v$wave speed (m s⁻¹)
Origin

At $t=0$ the shape is $f(x)$. At time $t$, the same value of $f$ occurs where $x - vt$ has the same value, i.e. at $x$ shifted by $vt$. So the whole shape slides a distance $vt$ to the right. (More: the characteristic variable u.) Direction rule: $x - vt$ → moves toward $+x$; $x + vt$ → moves toward $-x$.

sine
$$y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$$
The sinusoidal (harmonic) travelling wave — the standard form used in problems
▸
Parameters
  • $A$amplitude, the maximum displacement (m)
  • $k$wavenumber: radians of phase per metre (rad m⁻¹)
  • $\omega$angular frequency: radians of phase per second (rad s⁻¹)
  • $\phi$phase constant (rad), set by the starting condition
What it says

This one formula gives the displacement $y$ of every point of the medium at every moment. Put in a position $x$ and a time $t$; out comes how far that point is from rest.

Where it comes from
  1. The source does SHM (Ch. 1): $y(0,t) = A\cos\omega t$.
  2. The wave carries that motion along at speed $v$. A point at distance $x$ does the same thing, a time $x/v$ later: $y(x,t) = A\cos\big(\omega(t - x/v)\big)$.
  3. Multiply out and use $\omega/v = k$: $y = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. Cosine is even, so this equals $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$.
  4. Add $\phi$ so the wave can start at any point of its cycle.
Reading the parts
  • $A$: how big the wave is.
  • $kx$: turns position into an angle. Moving one wavelength adds $2\pi$.
  • $\omega t$: turns time into an angle. Waiting one period adds $2\pi$.
  • The minus sign: the wave moves toward $+x$. A plus sign would send it toward $-x$.
  • $\phi$: where in its cycle the wave starts.
How it fits with the rest
  • It is one case of the general travelling wave $f(x - vt)$: shape.
  • Fix $x$ and it is SHM in time: 1.14. Fix $t$ and it is a cosine in space: time and space pattern.
  • Its speed is $\omega/k$: speed.
  • It satisfies the wave equation exactly when $\omega/k = v$: 1D.
  • The complex form $Ae^{i(\omega t - kx)}$ is the same wave, easier to differentiate: complex.
Where it comes up
  • Writing a wave from given facts: method · PS6 · 1.
  • Reading $A$, $\lambda$, $f$, $v$ off a given wave: method.
  • Checking solutions of the wave equation: method (PS6 · 2).
Finding the phase constant

Put $x = 0,\ t = 0$: $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. At the top: $\phi = 0$. At the bottom: $\phi = \pi$. Passing zero: $\phi = \pm\pi/2$ (check the direction with $\partial y/\partial t$).

Picture
Watch out
  • $k$ here is the wavenumber, not the spring constant.
  • $\omega$, not $f$, goes inside the cosine: $\omega = 2\pi f$.
  • Sine or cosine is only a choice of $\phi$: $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$.
defs
$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}$$
Converting between the space pattern and the time pattern
▸
Parameters
  • $\lambda$wavelength: distance between neighbouring crests (m)
  • $f$frequency: oscillations per second (Hz = s⁻¹)
  • $T$period: time for one oscillation (s)
Origin

One full cycle is $2\pi$ radians. Moving one wavelength along $x$ advances the phase by $2\pi$, so $k\lambda = 2\pi$. Waiting one period advances it by $2\pi$, so $\omega T = 2\pi$, and with $T=1/f$, $\omega = 2\pi f$.

speed
$$v = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k}$$
Wave speed — links every space quantity to every time quantity
▸
Origin

In one period $T$ each crest moves forward exactly one wavelength, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda$. Substituting $f=\omega/2\pi$ and $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ gives $v=\omega/k$.

Picture
Use

Any two of $v$, $f$ (or $\omega$), $\lambda$ (or $k$) give the third. The speed is set by the medium; the frequency is set by the source; the wavelength follows from both.

The wave equation
1D
$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$$
The one-dimensional wave equation — the master equation of the chapter
▸
Parameters
  • $\partial/\partial t$partial derivative: differentiate in $t$ holding $x$ fixed
  • $\partial/\partial x$partial derivative: differentiate in $x$ holding $t$ fixed
  • $v$wave speed
Reading it

Left side: the acceleration of one point of the medium. Right side: the curvature of the shape at that point. Where the string curves most, it accelerates most. This is Newton's second law applied to a small piece of the medium, just as $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ was for one mass.

Why any moving shape solves it

Let $u = x - vt$. Chain rule: $\partial y/\partial x = f'(u)$ and $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = f''(u)$; $\partial y/\partial t = -v f'(u)$ and $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = v^2 f''(u)$. So $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$ for any shape $f$. Same for $x+vt$.

Checking a candidate

1. Take $\partial^2y/\partial t^2$. 2. Take $\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. 3. Substitute both and see what condition makes the two sides equal (usually $\omega/k = v$). A sum of solutions with the same $v$ is also a solution (the equation is linear).

complex
$$y = A\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$$
Complex form — same wave, easier derivatives
▸
Reading it

The physical wave is the real part, $A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. Each $\partial/\partial t$ brings down $i\omega$; each $\partial/\partial x$ brings down $-ik$. So $\partial^2/\partial t^2 \to -\omega^2$ and $\partial^2/\partial x^2 \to -k^2$. Direction: $\omega t - kx$ moves toward $+x$; $\omega t + kx$ toward $-x$.

A wave reflects from a fixed end; the reflected wave and the incoming wave add, and the sum is a standing wave: a fixed shape that swells and shrinks in place. method
Waves on a string: speed, wavelength, and the length for a given note
“Calculate the wavelength … What value of L is required to obtain a fundamental frequency of …” — PS7 Problem 1(a), (b)
▸
Recognize it

You are given a tension $T$ and a mass per unit length $\mu$ (so the speed is known), plus a frequency, and asked for a wavelength or a length.

Picture
Steps
  1. Find the wave speed from the string. $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.

    Recall: the speed of a transverse wave on a string is set only by the tension and the mass per unit length, string (Ch. 5).

    Why first: every other quantity is reached through $v$. Check units: $\sqrt{\text{N}/(\text{kg m}^{-1})} = \sqrt{\text{kg m s}^{-2}\cdot\text{m}/\text{kg}} = \text{m s}^{-1}$.

  2. On a long string (no reflections), get the wavelength from $v = f\lambda$.

    Recall: one wavelength passes a point per period, so $v = f\lambda$ speed.

    Operation: divide both sides by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.

    Why “long”: a long wire means the wave never comes back from an end, so there is no standing wave and any frequency is allowed.

  3. On a string fixed at both ends, draw the mode first. The fundamental is one loop: $L = \lambda/2$.

    Recall: a fixed end cannot move, so it is a node; neighbouring nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart nodes.

    Model: with nodes only at the two ends, exactly one half-wavelength fits: $L = \lambda_1/2$. In general $L = n\lambda_n/2$ modes.

  4. Combine to get the length. $f_1 = v/\lambda_1 = v/(2L)$, so $L = v/(2f_1)$.

    Where it comes from: substitute $\lambda_1 = 2L$ into $f = v/\lambda$ to get $f_1 = v/(2L)$ f_n. Multiply both sides by $2L$ and divide both sides by $2f_1$: $L = v/(2f_1)$.

    Shortcut: if you already have $\lambda$ from the long-string step at the same frequency, $L = \lambda/2$, because the fundamental is half a wavelength.

  5. Check the size.

    Instrument strings are tens of centimetres to a metre or two. A length of kilometres or millimetres means a power of ten has gone wrong (often grams instead of kilograms in $\mu$).

Uses

string speed · speed $v=f\lambda$ · modes · f_n

Common mistakes
  • Using $T$ as the period: in $v=\sqrt{T/\mu}$ it is the tension in newtons.
  • Setting $L = \lambda$ for the fundamental. One loop is half a wavelength.
  • $\mu$ in g/m: convert to kg/m first.
Worked example (different numbers from the problem set)
  1. Given: $T = 100$ N, $\mu = 0.010$ kg m⁻¹, $f = 440$ Hz.
  2. Speed from the string: $v = \sqrt{100/0.010} = \sqrt{10^4} = 100$ m s⁻¹.
  3. Long string: divide $v = f\lambda$ by $f$: $\lambda = 100/440 = 0.227$ m.
  4. Fixed ends, fundamental: $L = \lambda/2 = 0.114$ m. Same result from $L = v/(2f_1) = 100/880$ m ✓.
Go deeper

The same frequency can be a travelling wave on a long wire or a standing wave on a short one. The wavelength is the same in both cases ($\lambda = v/f$, and $v$ depends only on the wire); what the fixed ends add is the requirement that the length be a whole number of half-wavelengths.

method
From a vibrating string to the sound it makes
“What are the frequency and wavelength of the sound wave produced by the wire …? Explain any differences.” — PS7 Problem 1(c)
▸
Recognize it

A string vibrates in some mode and you are asked about the sound wave in the air.

Steps
  1. The frequency carries over unchanged. $f_{\text{sound}} = f_{\text{string}}$.

    Why: the string (through the bridge and body of the instrument) pushes the air back and forth once per cycle of its own motion. Each push makes one compression in the air, so the air oscillates at the same rate. The frequency belongs to the source.

  2. The speed changes: use the speed of sound in air. $v_{\text{air}} \approx 340$ m s⁻¹.

    Recall: the speed belongs to the medium sound. On the string the speed is $\sqrt{T/\mu}$; in the air it is about 340 m s⁻¹ whatever made the sound.

  3. Find the wavelength in air. Divide $v_{\text{air}} = f\lambda_{\text{air}}$ by $f$: $\lambda_{\text{air}} = v_{\text{air}}/f$.

    Why it differs from the string: both waves share $f$, so $\lambda_{\text{air}}/\lambda_{\text{string}} = v_{\text{air}}/v_{\text{string}}$. Whichever medium is faster has the longer wavelength.

  4. Explain the difference in one sentence.

    Same frequency (set by the source), different speed (set by each medium), therefore different wavelength. State this in words: the problem asks for it.

Uses

sound · speed · convert λ, f, v

Common mistakes
  • Using the string’s wavelength for the sound.
  • Changing the frequency between string and air.
  • Using $c$ (light) for sound.
Worked example (different numbers from the problem set)
  1. A string vibrates at $f = 200$ Hz with $v_{\text{string}} = 80$ m s⁻¹, so $\lambda_{\text{string}} = 80/200 = 0.40$ m.
  2. Sound: $f = 200$ Hz (unchanged).
  3. $\lambda_{\text{air}} = 340/200 = 1.7$ m.
  4. Ratio: $1.7/0.40 = 4.25 = 340/80$, the ratio of the speeds ✓.

method
Identify mode numbers from two successive modes
“Successive normal modes with wavelengths of … Identify the mode numbers and determine the length of the string.” — PS7 Problem 2(a)
▸
Recognize it

Two neighbouring modes ($n$ and $n+1$) of the same string are given, by wavelength or by frequency; $n$ and $L$ are unknown.

Picture
Steps
  1. Write the mode condition for each. $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, so $2L = n\lambda_n$ and $2L = (n+1)\lambda_{n+1}$.

    Recall: for a string fixed at both ends, mode $n$ fits $n$ half-wavelengths: $L = n\lambda_n/2$ modes. Multiply both sides by 2: $2L = n\lambda_n$.

    Why two equations: there are two unknowns, $n$ and $L$, and two given wavelengths.

  2. Decide which wavelength has the lower mode number. The longer wavelength is the lower $n$.

    Why: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ gets smaller as $n$ grows (more loops in the same length, so each loop is shorter).

  3. Set the two expressions for $2L$ equal and solve for $n$.

    Where it comes from: both equal $2L$, so $n\lambda_n = (n+1)\lambda_{n+1}$. Expand the right side: $n\lambda_n = n\lambda_{n+1} + \lambda_{n+1}$. Subtract $n\lambda_{n+1}$ from both sides: $n(\lambda_n - \lambda_{n+1}) = \lambda_{n+1}$. Divide both sides by $(\lambda_n - \lambda_{n+1})$: $$n = \frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{\lambda_n - \lambda_{n+1}}.$$

    Check: $n$ must come out a whole number. If it does not, recheck which wavelength is which.

  4. Substitute $n$ back to get $L$. $L = n\lambda_n/2$.

    Check with the other mode: $(n+1)\lambda_{n+1}/2$ must give the same $L$.

  5. Faster route (optional): $\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}} - \dfrac{1}{\lambda_n} = \dfrac{1}{2L}$.

    Where it comes from: $1/\lambda_n = n/(2L)$. Subtract the expression for $n$ from the one for $n+1$: $(n+1)/(2L) - n/(2L) = 1/(2L)$. So the spacing of $1/\lambda$ between neighbours gives $L$ directly, and then $n = 2L/\lambda_n$. With frequencies the same idea reads $f_{n+1} - f_n = f_1$ spacing.

Uses

modes · spacing

Common mistakes
  • Giving the shorter wavelength the lower mode number.
  • Assuming the first given mode is the fundamental: “successive” means neighbours, not $n = 1, 2$.
Worked example (different numbers from the problem set)
  1. Given: successive modes with $\lambda = 0.60$ m and $0.40$ m.
  2. Longer is lower: $\lambda_n = 0.60$ m, $\lambda_{n+1} = 0.40$ m.
  3. $n = 0.40/(0.60 - 0.40) = 0.40/0.20 = 2$. So the modes are $n = 2$ and $n = 3$.
  4. $L = n\lambda_n/2 = 2(0.60)/2 = 0.60$ m. Check: $3(0.40)/2 = 0.60$ m ✓.
  5. Faster route: $1/0.40 - 1/0.60 = 2.50 - 1.67 = 0.833$ m⁻¹ $= 1/(2L)$, so $2L = 1.20$ m and $L = 0.60$ m ✓.

“The cold spots in a microwave oven are found to have a separation of … What is the frequency?” — PS7 Problem 2(b)
▸
Recognize it

A standing wave is set up (between walls, mirrors, fixed ends) and you are told how far apart the still points (or the hot points) are.

Picture
Steps
  1. Identify what is being measured. Cold spots are nodes; hot spots are antinodes.

    Model: microwaves reflect from the metal walls of the oven, and the incoming and reflected waves form a standing wave. At a node the field is always zero, so no heating: a cold spot. At an antinode the field swings most: a hot spot.

  2. Recall the spacing: neighbouring nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart. Multiply both sides by 2: $\lambda = 2d$.

    Where it comes from: in $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$ the nodes are where $\sin kx = 0$, i.e. $kx = 0, \pi, 2\pi, \dots$; consecutive ones differ by $\Delta x = \pi/k = \lambda/2$ nodes. The same holds between neighbouring antinodes.

    The trap: the distance between nodes is half a wavelength, not a wavelength.

  3. Use the speed of the wave in that medium. Microwaves are electromagnetic: $v = c = 3.00\times10^8$ m s⁻¹.

    Recall: all electromagnetic waves travel at $c$ in vacuum (and very nearly in air) EM.

  4. Divide $c = f\lambda$ by $\lambda$: $f = c/\lambda$. Convert cm to m first.

    Check the size: microwave ovens run at a few gigahertz (about $2.45\times10^9$ Hz). A textbook value may differ from a real oven; report what the numbers give.

Harmonics: their frequencies, how many are audible, and stopping a string with a finger
“Frequencies of the second and third harmonics? … total number of harmonics the violinist can hear? … where to place their finger?” — PS7 Problem 3
▸
Recognize it

A string’s fundamental $f_1$ is given and you are asked about its higher modes, a frequency limit, or a shorter vibrating length.

Steps
  1. Harmonic frequencies: multiply the fundamental. $f_n = nf_1$.

    Recall: $f_n = nv/(2L)$ f_n, and $f_1 = v/(2L)$; dividing one by the other gives $f_n/f_1 = n$. The second harmonic is $n = 2$, the third $n = 3$. Naming: the fundamental is the first harmonic.

  2. Does the wave speed change between harmonics? No. Say why.

    Recall: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ depends only on the string string. Going to a higher harmonic does not change the tension or the mass per length. What changes is the wavelength ($\lambda_n = 2L/n$) and the frequency, in opposite proportion, so $f_n\lambda_n = v$ stays the same.

  3. Counting harmonics below a limit: divide and round down. $n_{\max} = \lfloor f_{\max}/f_1 \rfloor$.

    Where it comes from: harmonic $n$ is audible when $nf_1 \le f_{\max}$. Divide both sides by $f_1$: $n \le f_{\max}/f_1$. The harmonics are $n = 1, 2, \dots, n_{\max}$, so their number is the largest whole number not exceeding $f_{\max}/f_1$ (round down: the next one is above the limit). Convert kHz to Hz first.

    State your convention: whether you count the fundamental (it is harmonic 1, so normally yes).

  4. Finger on the string: the vibrating length changes, the speed does not.

    Model: pressing a finger down makes a new fixed point, a node. The string now vibrates only between the finger and the far end (the bridge). Tension and $\mu$ are unchanged, so $v$ is unchanged.

    Equation: the new fundamental is $f' = v/(2L')$, and the open string gave $f_1 = v/(2L)$. Both equal $v$ after multiplying out: $2Lf_1 = 2L'f'$. Divide both sides by $2f'$: $$L' = L\,\frac{f_1}{f'}.$$ A higher note needs a shorter length.

    Answer the question asked: $L'$ is the length still vibrating (finger to bridge). The finger sits $L - L'$ from the other end (the nut, where the string starts). Say which end you measure from.

Uses

f_n · string · modes

Common mistakes
  • Rounding up when counting harmonics.
  • Saying the speed rises with the harmonic: it is the frequency that rises; the wavelength shortens to compensate.
  • Answering $L'$ when the question asks for the distance from the end (or the reverse) without saying which.
Worked example (different numbers from the problem set)
  1. Given: string $L = 0.33$ m with fundamental $f_1 = 300$ Hz; listener hears up to 10 kHz; want the note 360 Hz.
  2. Harmonics: $f_2 = 600$ Hz, $f_3 = 900$ Hz; $v = 2Lf_1 = 2(0.33)(300) = 198$ m s⁻¹ for all of them.
  3. Count: $10\,000/300 = 33.3$, round down: 33 harmonics ($n = 1$ to $33$; the 34th, 10.2 kHz, is too high).
  4. Finger: $L' = 0.33 \times 300/360 = 0.275$ m vibrating; the finger is $0.33 - 0.275 = 0.055$ m $= 5.5$ cm from the nut.

Count the modes of a laser cavity inside a frequency band
“How many normal frequencies will occur within the range Δν? How long would the cavity have to be so that only one mode frequency occurred?” — PS7 Problem 4
▸
Recognize it

Two mirrors a distance $L$ apart, light of frequency $\nu_0$, and a band of width $\Delta\nu$ in which amplification happens.

Picture
Steps
  1. Model the cavity as a string fixed at both ends. The mirrors are nodes.

    Why: at a good mirror the light’s electric field is (nearly) zero, the same condition as a fixed end of a string. So the allowed wavelengths are again $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ modes, and with speed $c$ the mode frequencies are $\nu_n = nc/(2L)$ cavity.

    Notation: King writes frequency as $\nu$ (Greek nu) here. It is the same quantity as $f$.

  2. Find the spacing between neighbouring modes. $\nu_{n+1} - \nu_n = c/(2L)$.

    Where it comes from: subtract $nc/(2L)$ from $(n+1)c/(2L)$. The spacing does not depend on $n$: the modes form an evenly spaced comb (the vertical bars in King’s figure).

    Why $\nu_0$ hardly matters: $n = 2L\nu_0/c$ is around a million; you never need it. Only the spacing decides how many modes fall in the band. $\nu_0$ tells you where the band sits.

  3. Count how many spacings fit in the band. $N \approx \Delta\nu \big/ \dfrac{c}{2L} = \dfrac{2L\,\Delta\nu}{c}$.

    Why a division: the band is $\Delta\nu$ wide and the comb has one tooth every $c/(2L)$. Divide the width by the tooth spacing count.

    Whole numbers: the count of teeth must be an integer. Depending on where the comb sits relative to the band, a ratio of, say, 4.3 gives 4 or 5 modes. Give the whole-number part and say that one more may fit depending on alignment.

  4. For a single mode, make the spacing at least as wide as the band. $c/(2L) \ge \Delta\nu$.

    Why: if neighbouring teeth are farther apart than the band is wide, at most one can lie inside it.

    Operations: multiply both sides by $2L$ and divide both sides by $2\Delta\nu$: $L \le c/(2\Delta\nu)$. A shorter cavity spreads the modes out.

  5. Convert units (cm to m) and check the size.

    Spacings for lab lasers are hundreds of MHz; single-mode cavities are a few centimetres or shorter.

Energy of a plucked string: work done against the tension
“Pulled aside a distance h at its center and released. What is the energy of the subsequent oscillations?” — PS7 Problem 5(a)
▸
Recognize it

A string under tension $T$ is pulled into a shape and released from rest; the energy is asked for, with the hint to use work against the tension.

Picture
Steps
  1. Model the shape. Draw it: two straight segments from the ends to the pulled point.

    Picture: pulled aside a height $h$ at a point a distance $a$ from one end, the string is two straight lines, of horizontal lengths $a$ and $L - a$, meeting at height $h$.

  2. Find the length of each segment with Pythagoras: $\sqrt{a^2 + h^2}$.

    Recall: the slanted segment is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $a$ (along the string) and $h$ (sideways).

  3. Approximate for a small pull ($h \ll a$): $\sqrt{a^2+h^2} \approx a + \dfrac{h^2}{2a}$.

    Recall: the binomial approximation $(1+\varepsilon)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \varepsilon/2$ for small $\varepsilon$ stretch.

    Operations: factor out $a$: $\sqrt{a^2+h^2} = a\sqrt{1 + h^2/a^2}$. Apply the approximation with $\varepsilon = h^2/a^2$: $a(1 + h^2/(2a^2)) = a + h^2/(2a)$.

    So each segment is longer than its straight length by $h^2/(2\times\text{its horizontal length})$.

  4. Add the extensions to get the total stretch $\Delta L$.

    $\Delta L = \dfrac{h^2}{2a} + \dfrac{h^2}{2(L-a)}$. For a pull at the centre, both horizontal lengths are $L/2$; substitute that yourself.

  5. Work against the tension: $W = T\,\Delta L$.

    Why: work is force times the distance moved along the force. Lengthening the string by $\Delta L$ against a constant tension $T$ costs $T\Delta L$ (the problem tells you to treat $T$ as unchanged by the small stretch).

  6. That work is the energy of the oscillation.

    Why: the string is released from rest, so at release all of its energy is this stored work (potential energy) and none is kinetic. With no damping, energy is conserved, so the total stays equal to $T\Delta L$ while it moves back and forth between potential (stretch) and kinetic (motion) energy.

Uses

stretch · standing wave

Common mistakes
  • Using $\tfrac12 kx^2$ (a spring): the string’s restoring force is the tension, which here is constant.
  • Forgetting one of the two segments.
  • Dropping the factor 2 in the binomial approximation.
Worked example (different numbers from the problem set)
  1. Pulled at $a = L/4$ (a quarter of the way along), height $h$.
  2. Short segment: extension $h^2/(2\cdot L/4) = 2h^2/L$.
  3. Long segment ($3L/4$): extension $h^2/(2\cdot 3L/4) = 2h^2/(3L)$.
  4. Total: $\Delta L = 2h^2/L + 2h^2/(3L) = 8h^2/(3L)$ (common denominator $3L$).
  5. Energy: $E = T\Delta L = 8Th^2/(3L)$. Units: N·m²/m = N·m = J ✓.
Go deeper

The same answer comes from the general formula for the potential energy of a string, $U = \tfrac12 T\int_0^L (\partial y/\partial x)^2\,dx$, which is the extension formula above applied to each small piece: a piece of length $dx$ with slope $\partial y/\partial x$ is stretched by about $\tfrac12(\partial y/\partial x)^2dx$. For two straight segments the slope is constant on each, which reduces the integral to the sum above. (This is the form French uses; it is an extension beyond what the problem needs.)

Incoming plus partly reflected wave: rewrite as standing plus travelling, then the max/min ratio
“The travelling wave $y_1 = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$ combines with the reflected wave $y_2 = RA\cos(\omega t + kx)$ … Show that the standing wave can be represented by $y = 2RA\cos\omega t\cos kx + (1-R)A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. ▸
Recognize it

Two waves of the same $\omega$ and $k$, opposite directions, unequal amplitudes. "Show that" means: reach the given expression by algebra, saying why each move is made.

Steps
  1. Superpose. $y = y_1 + y_2$.

    Recall: the principle of superposition: the displacement due to two waves is the sum of the displacements (superposition).

  2. Split the larger wave. Write $A\cos(\omega t - kx) = RA\cos(\omega t - kx) + (1-R)A\cos(\omega t - kx)$.

    Why: The expression the problem asks for begins with a standing wave, $2RA\cos\omega t\cos kx$. A standing wave is two equal waves moving in opposite directions (standing and travelling waves), and the reflected wave, amplitude $RA$, can pair only with an equal share $RA$ of the incoming wave. So write $A = RA + (1-R)A$: the first piece pairs with the reflection and stands; the leftover $(1-R)A$ has no partner and keeps travelling. This changes nothing, since the pieces add back to $A$.

  3. Combine the equal pair with sum-to-product.

    Recall (sum to product): a sum of two cosines equals 2 times the cosine of the average of the two angles, times the cosine of half their difference (sum to product). Our two angles are $\omega t - kx$ and $\omega t + kx$. Average: $\frac{(\omega t - kx) + (\omega t + kx)}{2} = \omega t$; the $kx$ terms cancel. Half the difference: $\frac{(\omega t - kx) - (\omega t + kx)}{2} = -kx$; the $\omega t$ terms cancel. Cosine is even, $\cos(-kx) = \cos kx$. So $\cos(\omega t - kx) + \cos(\omega t + kx) = 2\cos\omega t\cos kx$; multiply by $RA$ and add back the travelling leftover $(1-R)A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. The point of the step: in the product, $x$ and $t$ sit in separate factors, which is what a standing wave looks like. This gives the form the problem asks for.

  4. Maximum amplitude: where the two parts move together.

    At $x$ with $\cos kx = 1$ (e.g. $x = 0$): $y = 2RA\cos\omega t + (1-R)A\cos\omega t = (1+R)A\cos\omega t$. Same at $\cos kx = -1$ with a sign. Amplitude $(1+R)A$.

  5. Minimum amplitude: where the standing part has a node.

    At $\cos kx = 0$ (e.g. $kx = \pi/2$) only the travelling part is left: amplitude $(1-R)A$.

    Why these are the extremes (for a complete answer): at any $x$ the motion is a sum of two oscillations at frequency $\omega$, of sizes $A$ and $RA$, whose relative phase is $2kx$. Such a sum is largest when they are in step, $A + RA$, and smallest when opposed, $A - RA$. The points above are exactly those.

  6. Divide. $\dfrac{(1+R)A}{(1-R)A} = \dfrac{1+R}{1-R}$.

    $A$ cancels. Check the limits: $R = 0$ gives 1, $R \to 1$ gives an infinite ratio (true nodes).

A standing wave: two equal waves travelling in opposite directions, added
▸
Parameters
  • $A$amplitude of each travelling wave (m); the standing wave swings up to $2A$
  • $k$wavenumber, $2\pi/\lambda$ (rad m⁻¹)
  • $\omega$angular frequency, $2\pi f$ (rad s⁻¹)
Where it comes from
  1. A wave sent toward a fixed end reflects. Both waves are on the string at once: $y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ toward $+x$, $y_2 = A\sin(kx + \omega t)$ toward $-x$ (direction rule).
  2. Recall superposition: the wave equation is linear, so the sum of two solutions is a solution, and the displacement is $y = y_1 + y_2$.
  3. Recall the sum identity $\sin P + \sin Q = 2\sin\frac{P+Q}{2}\cos\frac{P-Q}{2}$.
  4. Set $P = kx + \omega t$, $Q = kx - \omega t$: then $\frac{P+Q}{2} = kx$ and $\frac{P-Q}{2} = \omega t$. Substituting gives $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$.
  5. Check at the fixed end $x = 0$: $\sin 0 = 0$, so $y = 0$ for all $t$ ✓.
Reading it

The $x$ part and the $t$ part have separated. $\sin kx$ is a fixed shape; $\cos\omega t$ makes the whole shape grow, shrink, flip and return, every point in step. There is no $x - vt$: nothing travels. Each point does SHM at the same $\omega$, with its own amplitude $2A|\sin kx|$.

nodes
$$x_{\text{node}} = 0,\ \tfrac{\lambda}{2},\ \lambda,\ \tfrac{3\lambda}{2},\dots \qquad x_{\text{antinode}} = \tfrac{\lambda}{4},\ \tfrac{3\lambda}{4},\dots$$
Nodes and antinodes: neighbouring nodes are half a wavelength apart
▸
Origin

Nodes are where the amplitude $2A\sin kx$ is zero. Recall: $\sin\theta = 0$ exactly when $\theta = m\pi$ ($m$ a whole number). So $kx = m\pi$; divide both sides by $k = 2\pi/\lambda$: $x = m\lambda/2$. Antinodes are where $|\sin kx| = 1$, i.e. $kx = \pi/2 + m\pi$, giving $x = \lambda/4 + m\lambda/2$.

Spacings to remember

node to node: $\lambda/2$ · antinode to antinode: $\lambda/2$ · node to nearest antinode: $\lambda/4$.

Normal modes of a string fixed at both ends
modes
$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$
Allowed wavelengths: a whole number of half-wavelengths fits in L
▸
Parameters
  • $L$length between the fixed ends (m)
  • $n$mode number: the number of loops (half-wavelengths)
Where it comes from
  1. Fixed ends cannot move: $y(0,t) = 0$ and $y(L,t) = 0$ for every $t$ (boundary conditions).
  2. In $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$ the end $x = 0$ is already a node.
  3. At $x = L$ we need $\sin kL = 0$, so $kL = n\pi$ ($n = 0$ would be no wave at all).
  4. Substitute $k = 2\pi/\lambda$: $2\pi L/\lambda = n\pi$. Multiply both sides by $\lambda$ and divide both sides by $n\pi$: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$.
Picture

$L = n\cdot\lambda_n/2$: mode $n$ is $n$ loops end to end, with $n+1$ nodes (counting both ends) and $n$ antinodes. See the figure under standing.

f_n
$$f_n = \frac{nv}{2L} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = nf_1$$
Normal-mode frequencies of a string (the harmonic series)
▸
Parameters
  • $f_n$frequency of mode $n$ (Hz)
  • $f_1$fundamental frequency, $v/(2L)$ (Hz)
  • $v$wave speed on the string (m s⁻¹)
  • $T$tension (N), not the period
  • $\mu$mass per unit length (kg m⁻¹)
Where it comes from
  1. Divide $v = f\lambda$ by $\lambda$: $f = v/\lambda$.
  2. Substitute $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ modes: $f_n = nv/(2L)$.
  3. Substitute $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ string.
  4. With $n = 1$, $f_1 = v/(2L)$, so $f_n = nf_1$.
Reading it

The string chooses its own frequencies: only whole multiples of $f_1$. Tighter string → higher; longer or heavier string → lower. The speed $v$ is the same for every mode: it belongs to the string. Higher modes have shorter wavelengths and higher frequencies in exact proportion.

Names: mode $n$ is the $n$th harmonic; the fundamental is the first harmonic; the $n$th harmonic is the $(n-1)$th overtone.

spacing
$$f_{n+1} - f_n = \frac{v}{2L} = f_1, \qquad \frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}} - \frac{1}{\lambda_n} = \frac{1}{2L}$$
Neighbouring modes are evenly spaced
▸
Origin

Subtract $f_n = nv/(2L)$ from $f_{n+1} = (n+1)v/(2L)$: the $n$ terms cancel, leaving $v/(2L)$. Likewise $1/\lambda_n = n/(2L)$. Use: two successive modes of an unknown string give $L$ directly (method).

Other standing-wave systems
cavity
$$\nu_n = \frac{nc}{2L}, \qquad \Delta\nu_{\text{modes}} = \frac{c}{2L}$$
Normal modes of an optical (laser) cavity: two mirrors a distance L apart
▸
Parameters
  • $\nu_n$frequency of mode $n$ (Hz); King writes $\nu$ for frequency here, the same as $f$
  • $c$$3.00\times10^8$ m s⁻¹
  • $L$mirror separation (m)
Origin

The mirrors act like fixed ends (field nearly zero there), so $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ as for a string; the speed is $c$, so $\nu_n = c/\lambda_n = nc/(2L)$. Subtracting neighbours gives the spacing $c/(2L)$.

Reading it

For visible light and $L$ of centimetres, $n$ is about a million, but the spacing is what matters: an evenly spaced comb of allowed frequencies.

count
$$N \approx \frac{\Delta\nu}{c/(2L)} = \frac{2L\,\Delta\nu}{c}$$
Number of cavity modes inside a band of width Δν
▸
Origin

A band $\Delta\nu$ wide, teeth every $c/(2L)$: divide width by spacing. Take the whole-number part; one more tooth can fit depending on where the comb lines up with the band.

Single mode

Require $c/(2L) \ge \Delta\nu$; rearranged, $L \le c/(2\Delta\nu)$.

stretch
$$W = T\,\Delta L, \qquad \sqrt{a^2 + h^2} \approx a + \frac{h^2}{2a}\ \ (h \ll a)$$
Energy stored by stretching a string against a constant tension
▸
Parameters
  • $T$tension (N), assumed unchanged by the small stretch
  • $\Delta L$increase in length (m)
  • $a$horizontal length of a straight segment (m)
  • $h$sideways displacement (m)
Where it comes from
  1. Work is force times distance along the force; lengthening by $\Delta L$ against $T$ costs $T\Delta L$.
  2. Recall the binomial approximation: $(1+\varepsilon)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \varepsilon/2$ for small $\varepsilon$ (first term of the Taylor expansion).
  3. Factor $a$ out of the square root: $\sqrt{a^2+h^2} = a\sqrt{1+h^2/a^2}$. Apply step 2 with $\varepsilon = h^2/a^2$: $a + h^2/(2a)$.
Use

Energy of a plucked string. Released from rest with no damping, this work is the total energy of the oscillation.

Partial reflection (PS8)
SWR
$$y = 2RA\cos\omega t\cos kx + (1-R)A\cos(\omega t - kx), \qquad \frac{A_{\max}}{A_{\min}} = \frac{1+R}{1-R}$$
Incoming plus partly reflected wave, and the standing wave ratio
▸
Parameters
  • $A$amplitude of the incoming wave (m)
  • $R$amplitude reflection coefficient: reflected amplitude ÷ incoming amplitude, $0 \le R \le 1$ (no units)
  • $A_{\max}, A_{\min}$largest and smallest amplitude of motion along $x$
Where it comes from
  1. Add the waves: $y = A\cos(\omega t - kx) + RA\cos(\omega t + kx)$.
  2. Split the incoming wave into a part that matches the reflected one and a remainder: $A = RA + (1-R)A$.
  3. The matching parts form a standing wave by sum to product: 2 times the cosine of the average of the two angles times the cosine of half their difference.
  4. Largest motion where $\cos kx = \pm1$: both pieces in step, amplitude $2RA + (1-R)A = (1+R)A$. Smallest where $\cos kx = 0$: only the travelling piece, $(1-R)A$.
Reading it

$R = 1$ gives an infinite ratio (true nodes); $R = 0$ gives 1 (no pattern at all). See partial reflection and the worked steps in method.

Strings of different thickness (PS8)
μ(d)
$$\mu = \rho\,\frac{\pi d^2}{4}, \qquad f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \frac{1}{Ld}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\pi\rho}}$$
Mass per unit length of a round wire, and the fundamental in terms of its diameter
▸
Parameters
  • $\rho$density of the material (kg m⁻³)
  • $d$diameter of the wire (m)
  • $\mu$mass per unit length (kg m⁻¹)
  • $T$tension (N)
  • $L$vibrating length, nut to bridge (m)
Where it comes from
  1. A length $\ell$ of wire is a cylinder of volume $\ell\cdot\pi d^2/4$, so its mass is $\rho\,\ell\,\pi d^2/4$. Divide by $\ell$: $\mu = \rho\pi d^2/4$.
  2. Substitute into $f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}$ f_n: $\sqrt{\mu} = \frac d2\sqrt{\pi\rho}$, and the factor $\frac12$ cancels the $2$ in $2L$.
Reading it

At fixed tension, length and material, $f_1 \propto 1/d$: twice as thick, an octave lower. At fixed tension, length and frequency, $\mu$ must stay the same, so $\rho d^2$ stays the same: a lighter material needs a thicker string.

Sound in pipes (PS8)
p(ξ)
$$p = -B\,\frac{\partial \xi}{\partial x}$$
Pressure change in a sound wave from the displacement of the air
▸
Parameters
  • $\xi$displacement of the air layer at $x$ along the pipe (m); Greek "xi"
  • $p$pressure above atmospheric (Pa)
  • $B$bulk modulus of air (Pa): pressure change per fractional change in volume
Where it comes from
  1. Model: a slab of air in a pipe of cross-section $S$, between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$. Its volume is $V = S\,\Delta x$.
  2. When the wave passes, the left face moves to $x + \xi(x)$ and the right face to $x + \Delta x + \xi(x+\Delta x)$. The new thickness is $\Delta x + \xi(x+\Delta x) - \xi(x)$.
  3. So the change in volume is $\Delta V = S\,[\xi(x+\Delta x) - \xi(x)]$, and the fractional change is $\Delta V/V = [\xi(x+\Delta x) - \xi(x)]/\Delta x \to \partial\xi/\partial x$ as the slab becomes thin.
  4. Recall: the bulk modulus is defined by $p = -B\,\Delta V/V$ (squeezing, $\Delta V \lt 0$, raises the pressure). Substitute step 3.
Reading it

Pressure follows the slope of the displacement, not its size: sound's two descriptions. For a standing wave $\xi = \xi_0\sin kx\cos\omega t$, $p = -Bk\xi_0\cos kx\cos\omega t$: where $\sin kx = 0$ (no motion), $|\cos kx| = 1$ (largest pressure swing), and the other way round.

pipes
$$\text{open–open: } f_n = \frac{nv}{2L} \qquad \text{closed–open: } f_n = \frac{(2n-1)\,v}{4L}$$
Normal modes of an air column
▸
Parameters
  • $v$speed of sound, about 340 m s⁻¹ in air
  • $L$length of the column (m)
  • $n$$1, 2, 3, \dots$
Where it comes from
  1. Open–open: a displacement antinode at each end (ends). Neighbouring antinodes are $\lambda/2$ apart, as neighbouring nodes are, so $L = n\lambda/2$, the same as a string: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, $f_n = nv/(2L)$.
  2. Closed–open: a node at the closed end, an antinode at the open end. Node to nearest antinode is $\lambda/4$; adding half-wavelengths keeps the ends of the same type, so $L = \lambda/4, 3\lambda/4, 5\lambda/4, \dots = (2n-1)\lambda/4$. Divide $v$ by $\lambda_n = 4L/(2n-1)$.
Reading it

The closed–open pipe has only odd multiples of its fundamental $v/(4L)$, and its fundamental is an octave below that of an open pipe of the same length. In practice the antinode sits slightly outside an open end (the "end correction", about $0.6$ times the pipe radius); King's problems ignore it.

Quantum standing waves (PS8)
box
$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \quad p_n = \frac{h}{\lambda_n} = \frac{nh}{2L}, \quad E_n = \frac{p_n^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}$$
Particle in an infinite well: allowed wavelengths, momenta and energies
▸
Parameters
  • $L$width of the well (m)
  • $h$Planck's constant, $6.6\times10^{-34}$ J s
  • $m$mass of the particle (kg); electron $9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg
  • $E_n$allowed energy of level $n$ (J); divide by $1.6\times10^{-19}$ for eV
Where it comes from
  1. Model: infinitely high walls at $x = 0$ and $x = L$: the particle cannot be outside, so its wave is zero at both walls. These are the boundary conditions of a string fixed at both ends.
  2. So a whole number of half-wavelengths fits: $n\lambda/2 = L$ modes. Divide both sides by $n/2$.
  3. Recall de Broglie: $\lambda = h/p$ (matter waves). Rearranged, $p = h/\lambda$; substitute $\lambda_n$.
  4. Inside the well the potential energy is zero, so all the energy is kinetic: $E = \tfrac12mv^2 = p^2/(2m)$ (multiply and divide $\tfrac12 mv^2$ by $m$). Substitute $p_n$ and square.
Reading it

Energies grow as $n^2$: $E_2 = 4E_1$, $E_3 = 9E_1$. The lowest is not zero. A narrower well raises every level as $1/L^2$. See confinement makes things discrete.
