amplitudeThe largest displacement from equilibrium, $A$ (m). The motion goes between $+A$ and $-A$.
angular frequencyRadians of phase per second, $\omega$ (rad/s). Since one cycle is $2\pi$ rad, $\omega = 2\pi f$.
antinodeA point of a standing wave that swings with the largest amplitude, midway between two nodes.
boundary conditionA condition the wave must satisfy at the edge of the medium for all time; a fixed end requires zero displacement.
chain ruleDerivative of a function of a function: $\frac{d}{dt}f(g(t)) = f'(g)\,g'(t)$. Differentiating $\cos(\omega t)$ brings out the factor $\omega$.
critical damping$\gamma = 2\omega_0$: the fastest return to equilibrium without overshooting.
damping rate$\gamma = b/m$ (s⁻¹): how fast friction removes energy. Energy decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$, amplitude as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
Doppler broadeningThe spread of frequencies emitted by atoms because they move toward or away at different thermal speeds; it gives a spectral line its width $\Delta\nu$.
envelopeThe slowly changing curve that bounds an oscillation, e.g. $\pm A_0e^{-\gamma t/2}$ for a damped oscillator.
equilibriumThe position where the net force is zero and the system can rest.
frequencyNumber of complete cycles per second, $f$ (Hz = s⁻¹). $f = 1/T$.
fundamentalThe lowest normal mode, $n = 1$: one loop, $\lambda = 2L$, $f_1 = v/(2L)$. Also called the first harmonic.
harmonicThe $n$th harmonic is the normal mode with frequency $nf_1$. The fundamental is the first harmonic.
heavy damping$\gamma/2 > \omega_0$: the system creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
light damping$\gamma/2 < \omega_0$: the system oscillates while its amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.
log decrement$\ln(A_n/A_{n+1}) = \gamma T/2$: the natural log of the ratio of successive peaks.
mass per unit lengthMass of one metre of string, $\mu$ (kg m⁻¹).
mediumWhat the wave travels through (string, air, water). The medium sets the wave speed.
mode numberThe whole number $n$ labelling a normal mode: the number of half-wavelengths (loops) between the fixed ends.
natural frequency$\omega_0$: the angular frequency the system oscillates at with no damping or driving, e.g. $\sqrt{k/m}$.
nodeA point of a standing wave that never moves. Neighbouring nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart; a fixed end is a node.
normal modeA pattern in which every point of the system oscillates at the same single frequency. For a fixed–fixed string, mode $n$ has $n$ loops and frequency $nf_1$.
optical cavityTwo facing mirrors a distance $L$ apart in which light forms standing waves; its mode frequencies are $nc/(2L)$.
overtoneAny mode above the fundamental, counted from it: the first overtone is the second harmonic.
partial derivativeDerivative with respect to one variable while the others are held fixed. $\partial y/\partial t$: watch one point over time. $\partial y/\partial x$: the slope of the frozen shape.
periodTime for one complete cycle, $T$ (s). $T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega$.
phaseThe full angle inside the cosine, e.g. $kx-\omega t+\phi$. Points with the same phase are at the same stage of the cycle (e.g. all crests).
phase constantThe constant $\phi$ added inside the cosine. It slides the wave so it matches the starting condition.
plane waveA 3D wave whose crests are flat parallel planes, $\psi = A\sin(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)$, travelling along $\mathbf k$.
quadrantWhich quarter of the circle an angle lies in. $\tan$ cannot tell opposite quadrants apart, so check the signs of $\cos\phi$ and $\sin\phi$.
quality factor$Q = \omega_0/\gamma$: how many oscillations (roughly, ×$\pi$) happen before the energy fades. High $Q$ = lightly damped.
radianUnit of angle: $2\pi$ rad = one full cycle = 360°. Arguments of sin and cos in physics are in radians.
restoring forceA force that points back toward equilibrium, e.g. $F = -kx$. It is what makes a system oscillate.
SHMSimple harmonic motion: oscillation in which the restoring force is proportional to displacement, so $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ and $x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$.
sinusoidalShaped like a sine or cosine curve.
small-angle approximationFor small $\theta$ in radians, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and $\cos\theta\approx 1-\theta^2/2$. Turns nearly-SHM systems into exact SHM.
sourceWhat makes the wave (the hand moving the string end, a speaker). The source sets the frequency.
space patternThe shape of the whole wave at one frozen instant, like a photograph. It repeats every wavelength $\lambda$; its rate in space is the wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.
standing waveA wave pattern that stays in place: a fixed shape whose size oscillates, $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$. The sum of two equal waves travelling in opposite directions.
starting conditionWhere the system is and how fast it moves at one moment (usually $t=0$). They fix the constants $A$ and $\phi$.
superpositionWhen two waves occupy the same medium the displacement is their sum. Allowed because the wave equation is linear.
Taylor expansionWriting a function near a point as a sum of powers: $f(x)\approx f(0)+f'(0)x+\tfrac12f''(0)x^2+\dots$. Keeping the first terms is the basis of every small-oscillation approximation.
tensionThe pulling force along a string, $T$ (N). Sets the wave speed with $\mu$: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. Not the period.
time patternHow one point of the medium moves as time passes, watched at a fixed place. It repeats with period $T$; its rate is the frequency $f$ or angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$. Set by the source.
travelling waveA disturbance whose shape moves through a medium at speed $v$, $y = f(x \mp vt)$, while each point of the medium only oscillates in place.
wave equation$\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. Newton's second law for a small piece of the medium; any shape moving at $v$ satisfies it.
wave vector$\mathbf k = (k_1,k_2,k_3)$: points in the direction of travel; its length is the wavenumber $2\pi/\lambda$.
wavelengthDistance between neighbouring crests (or any two points in step), $\lambda$ (m). $\lambda = v/f$.
wavenumberRadians of phase per metre, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ (rad/m). Some books instead use $1/\lambda$; check which.

Waves: the core quantities
concept
$$\text{time pattern} \;\leftrightarrow\; \text{space pattern}$$
Two ways to look at the same wave
▸
Definition

A wave depends on two variables, position $x$ and time $t$. You can look at it two ways. Time pattern: stand at one place and watch that point move up and down as time passes. Space pattern: freeze time and look at the whole shape along $x$, like a photograph.

Picture
The two graphs

Time pattern: graph $y$ against $t$ at fixed $x$. It repeats every period $T$; its rates are $f$ and $\omega$. Space pattern: graph $y$ against $x$ at fixed $t$. It repeats every wavelength $\lambda$; its rate is $k$. For $y = A\cos(kx-\omega t)$: at $x = 0$ you get $A\cos\omega t$ (SHM in time); at $t = 0$ you get $A\cos kx$ (a cosine in space).

How they connect

The wave speed links them. In one period $T$ the shape slides forward one wavelength $\lambda$, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda = \omega/k$. Time quantities ($T, f, \omega$) come from the source; space quantities ($\lambda, k$) follow from the source and the medium together.

concept
$$u = x - vt$$
Characteristic variable — why $f(x - vt)$ moves at speed $v$
▸
Definition

For a travelling wave $y = f(x - vt)$, call the combination inside $u = x - vt$. The height at a point depends only on $u$: any position and time with the same $u$ have the same height. Each feature of the shape (a crest, a trough, a point halfway up) belongs to one value of $u$.

Connection to phase

For a sine wave, the phase is the whole angle inside the cosine. Factor out $k$:

$$\underbrace{kx - \omega t}_{\text{phase}} = k\Big(\underbrace{x - \tfrac{\omega}{k}t}_{u}\Big) = k\,u, \qquad v = \frac{\omega}{k}.$$
  • Phase is the characteristic variable converted to radians. $u$ is measured in metres; multiplying by $k$ (radians per metre) turns it into an angle.
  • Same phase ⇔ same $u$ ⇔ same feature. A crest is a point of constant phase; following constant phase is following constant $u$.
  • Phase velocity. The speed at which a point of constant phase moves is $\omega/k$. That is the speed $v$ in $u = x - vt$, which is why it is called the phase velocity.
Names

Characteristic variable (or characteristic coordinate) is the standard term: d'Alembert's solution writes $\xi = x - vt$, $\eta = x + vt$, and every solution of the wave equation is $f(x - vt) + g(x + vt)$. Related names: comoving coordinate (position in a frame moving with the wave), phase (for a sine wave, the whole argument $kx - \omega t = k(x - vt)$), and retarded time $t - x/v$ (the same idea measured in time, as in PS6 2(i)). The letter $u$ is just our label.

Catching up

Follow the crest, say at $u = 0$. As time passes, $vt$ grows and pulls $u$ down. To keep $u = 0$, $x$ must grow by the same amount: $x = vt$. The crest is wherever $x$ has caught up with $vt$, so it moves forward at speed $v$. Every other feature does the same, so the whole shape slides without changing.

Riding along

Move toward $+x$ at speed $v$, starting at the origin. At time $t$ you are at $vt$, so a point at $x$ is a distance $x - vt$ ahead of you: $u$ is position measured from you. Since $y = f(u)$, the shape seen from your moving position never changes. A travelling wave is a fixed shape seen from a frame it is not moving with.

Direction

$u = x + vt$: to keep $u$ fixed as $t$ grows, $x$ must decrease, so the shape moves toward $-x$.

In problems

Sine waves: $kx - \omega t = k\left(x - \tfrac{\omega}{k}t\right)$, so $u$ appears with $v = \omega/k$; this is why the wave speed is $\omega/k$. Checking the wave equation: substitute $u$ and use the chain rule, $\partial u/\partial x = 1$ and $\partial u/\partial t = -v$; every $f(x \mp vt)$ solves it. concept
$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$$
Wavenumber — how fast the phase advances along $x$
▸
Definition

The wavenumber $k$ is the number of radians of phase the wave goes through per metre of distance, at a frozen instant. One wavelength is one full cycle, $2\pi$ radians, so $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Units: rad/m (often written m⁻¹).

Intuition

$k$ is the space version of $\omega$. $\omega$ says how quickly one point cycles in time; $k$ says how quickly the shape cycles as you walk along it. Large $k$: crests packed tightly (short wavelength). Small $k$: crests spread out (long wavelength).

Where it appears
  • Inside the wave: $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$. It multiplies $x$ so that $kx$ is an angle in radians.
  • Wave speed: $v = \omega/k$.
  • Wave equation: each $\partial/\partial x$ of $\cos(kx-\omega t)$ brings out a factor $k$, so $\partial^2y/\partial x^2 = -k^2 y$.
  • Reading an equation: $k$ is the number multiplying $x$.
Picture

At $t = 0$, $y = A\cos kx$. Walking from $x$ to $x + \lambda$ increases $kx$ by $k\lambda = 2\pi$: exactly one full cycle, so the shape repeats. That is why $k\lambda = 2\pi$.

Example

PS6 Problem 1: $\lambda = 5.0$ m, so $k = 2\pi/5.0 = 0.4\pi \approx 1.26$ rad/m. The phase advances 1.26 rad for every metre along the string.

Watch out
  • Not the spring constant. Chapter 1 uses $k$ for the spring constant (N/m) in $F = -kx$. Same letter, unrelated quantity. In Chapter 5, $k$ is almost always the wavenumber.
  • Two “wavenumbers”. Spectroscopy uses $\tilde\nu = 1/\lambda$ (cycles per metre). $k = 2\pi\tilde\nu$. State which you mean.
In 3D

The wavenumber becomes the wave vector $\mathbf k = (k_1, k_2, k_3)$: it points in the direction of travel, and its length is $2\pi/\lambda$.

Wavelength — the repeat length of the wave in space
▸
Definition

The distance between two neighbouring points that are at the same stage of the cycle, e.g. crest to crest. Units: m.

Intuition

In the time it takes one point to complete one oscillation (one period), the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength. That is the whole content of $v = f\lambda$.

What sets it

Neither the source nor the medium alone: the source fixes $f$, the medium fixes $v$, and $\lambda = v/f$ follows. When a wave enters a new medium, $f$ stays the same and $\lambda$ changes with $v$.

Typical sizes
  • Visible light: 400–700 nm.
  • Audible sound in air: about 17 mm (20 kHz) to 17 m (20 Hz).
  • AM radio: hundreds of metres.

Angular frequency — radians of phase per second
▸
Definition

How many radians of phase one point of the oscillation goes through per second. One cycle is $2\pi$ rad, so $\omega = 2\pi f$. Units: rad/s (often written s⁻¹).

Why radians

Calculus works cleanly only in radians: $\frac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t = -\omega\sin\omega t$. With $f$ inside the cosine you would carry $2\pi$ through every derivative. That is why equations use $\omega$ and $k$ rather than $f$ and $1/\lambda$.

Where it appears
  • SHM: $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ (spring) or $\sqrt{g/l}$ (pendulum).
  • Waves: the number multiplying $t$ in $\cos(kx - \omega t)$.
  • Wave speed: $v = \omega/k$.
Three omegas
  • $\omega_0$: natural angular frequency, with no damping (Ch. 2).
  • $\omega$ in a damped oscillator: $\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$, slightly lower.
  • $\omega$ of a driving force: whatever the driver imposes (Ch. 3).

Frequency — cycles per second
▸
Definition

The number of complete cycles per second. Units: hertz, Hz = s⁻¹.

What sets it

For a wave, the source. Shake the end of a string 10 times a second and every point of the string moves 10 times a second. Frequency does not change when the wave passes into a different medium.

Watch out

Problems give $f$ in Hz, but equations need $\omega$ in rad/s: multiply by $2\pi$ before putting it inside a cosine.

Phase and phase constant — where in the cycle you are
▸
Definition

The phase is the whole angle inside the cosine. It says where in its cycle a point is: phase 0 is a crest (for cosine), $\pi$ a trough, $\pi/2$ crossing zero. The phase constant $\phi$ is the fixed part, set by the starting condition.

Intuition

Points with the same phase are doing the same thing at the same moment, e.g. all the crests. Following a point of constant phase is following a crest, and that crest moves at $v$: set $kx - \omega t = \text{const}$, differentiate, $dx/dt = \omega/k$.

Finding the phase constant

Evaluate at the given moment, usually $x = 0, t = 0$, where the phase is just $\phi$: $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. Maximum: $\phi = 0$. Minimum: $\phi = \pi$. Zero and moving up or down: $\phi = \pm\pi/2$ (check with the velocity).

Picture
Sine vs cosine

$\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$: a sine is a cosine with phase constant $-\pi/2$. Choosing one or the other only changes $\phi$.

Wave speed — how fast the shape moves
▸
Definition

The speed at which the wave shape (a crest, say) travels through the medium. Units: m/s.

How the three forms relate
$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k}$$
  1. $\lambda/T$ is the definition: in one period $T$ a crest moves one wavelength $\lambda$; speed is distance ÷ time.
  2. $f\lambda$ counts cycles: $f = 1/T$, so $\lambda/T = \lambda\cdot\tfrac{1}{T} = f\lambda$. (cycles per second) × (metres per cycle) = metres per second.
  3. $\omega/k$ counts radians: $\omega = 2\pi f$ and $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, so $\dfrac{\omega}{k} = \dfrac{2\pi f}{2\pi/\lambda} = f\lambda$. The $2\pi$ cancels: (rad/s) ÷ (rad/m) = m/s.
What each variable does
  • $f$, $\omega$: time — how fast one point oscillates; set by the source.
  • $\lambda$, $k$: space — how the shape repeats along the medium.
  • $v$: set by the medium. So $\lambda = v/f$ follows; entering a new medium, $f$ stays and $\lambda$ changes.
Which form to use
  • Given $f$ or $\lambda$ in Hz and metres: $v = f\lambda$ (PS6 · 3).
  • Given an equation $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$: read $k$, $\omega$ and use $v = \omega/k$ (PS6 · 2).
What sets it

The medium. On a string, $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ (tension over mass per length). Light in vacuum, $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Sound in air, about 340 m/s.

Watch out

Wave speed is not the speed of the medium. Each point of a string moves up and down with velocity $\partial y/\partial t$, which changes all the time and is unrelated to $v$. The shape moves along; the string does not.

Sine, cosine and tangent — what they actually describe
▸
The picture

Put a point on a circle of radius 1 and let it go around. The angle $\theta$ says how far around it has gone. Then $\cos\theta$ is the point's horizontal position and $\sin\theta$ is its height. That is the whole definition.

Why they describe oscillation

Let the point go around at a steady rate, so $\theta = \omega t$. Watch only its shadow on the horizontal axis: it slides back and forth between $+1$ and $-1$, fastest in the middle, stopping at the ends. That shadow is $\cos\omega t$, and it is exactly SHM. Simple harmonic motion is the shadow of steady circular motion. This is why sin and cos, and nothing else, describe springs, pendulums and waves.

Same motion, two shadows
  • $\cos$ is the shadow on the horizontal axis; $\sin$ is the shadow on the vertical axis. One circle, viewed from two sides a quarter-turn apart.
  • So $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$: the vertical shadow is the horizontal one a quarter cycle later. You were right that they are the same thing shifted; the circle is why.
  • When one is at its extreme, the other is passing through zero. For an oscillator: at the turning point the position is extreme and the speed is zero.
  • Each is the other's rate of change: $\frac{d}{d\theta}\cos\theta = -\sin\theta$, $\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$. The velocity of the horizontal shadow is set by the vertical position. That is why $x = A\cos\omega t$ has velocity $-A\omega\sin\omega t$.
  • $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ is Pythagoras on the triangle in the figure: the point is always distance 1 from the centre.
Tangent
  • $\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta$ = height ÷ horizontal position = the slope of the radius line.
  • The name: extend the radius until it hits the vertical line touching the circle at $x = 1$ (a tangent line). The height where it hits is $\tan\theta$.
  • Tangent is not an oscillation. As the radius turns toward vertical ($\theta \to \pi/2$), its slope grows without limit; past vertical it comes back from $-\infty$. It repeats every $\pi$, not $2\pi$.
  • Small angles: $\tan\theta \approx \sin\theta \approx \theta$ (radians), since for a tiny angle the arc, the height and the tangent segment are all nearly the same length.
Where tangent shows up in this course
  • Finding a phase from two shadows. A ratio of a sine part to a cosine part is a tangent. From $x_0 = A\cos\phi$ and $v_0 = -\omega A\sin\phi$: $\tan\phi = -v_0/(\omega x_0)$. See fit A and phi.
  • Driven oscillators (Ch. 3): the lag of the response behind the driving force is given by $\tan\delta = \gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$, again a ratio of two parts.
  • Slopes and angles: the slope of a string at a point is $\partial y/\partial x = \tan$ of the angle it makes with the horizontal.
  • Because $\tan$ repeats every $\pi$, $\tan^{-1}$ gives one of two angles; check the quadrant with the signs of the sine and cosine parts.

Standing waves and normal modes (Ch. 6)
concept
$$\text{incoming} + \text{reflected} = \text{standing}$$
Standing wave
▸
Definition

A wave pattern that does not travel: a fixed shape $\sin kx$ whose size oscillates in time, $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$. It is the sum of two equal travelling waves going in opposite directions.

Intuition

Shake a rope tied to a wall. Your wave goes in, comes back inverted, and meets the next one you send. At some points the two always cancel (nodes); midway between they always reinforce (antinodes). The crests no longer move along the rope; the rope just pulses up and down in loops.

Where it appears

Guitar and violin strings, organ pipes, laser cavities, microwave ovens, and (later in the course) the electron in a box, whose allowed energies come from the same “whole number of half-wavelengths” rule.

Often confused with

A travelling wave, $f(x - vt)$, where the shape moves. In a standing wave energy does not flow along the string on average; it sloshes between the stretch near nodes and the motion at antinodes.

concept
$$\text{node: } \sin kx = 0 \qquad \text{antinode: } |\sin kx| = 1$$
Nodes and antinodes
▸
Definition

A node is a point of a standing wave that never moves. An antinode is a point that swings with the largest amplitude, $2A$.

Facts to use

Neighbouring nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart; a node and the nearest antinode are $\lambda/4$ apart. A fixed end is always a node.

Measurement

Nodes are what you can see or measure: still points on a string, cold spots in a microwave oven. Twice their spacing is the wavelength.

concept
$$n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$
Normal mode, mode number, harmonic
▸
Definition

A normal mode is a way the whole system can vibrate with every point moving at one single frequency. For a string fixed at both ends, mode $n$ has $n$ loops, wavelength $2L/n$ and frequency $nf_1$. The mode number $n$ counts the loops.

Names

$n = 1$: the fundamental (first harmonic), the lowest frequency, which sets the pitch. $n = 2, 3, \dots$: the second, third, … harmonics. An overtone counts from the fundamental: the second harmonic is the first overtone.

Why only certain frequencies

Both ends must be nodes, and nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart, so only wavelengths that fit a whole number of halves into $L$ survive. Other wavelengths would need the end to move. Fixing the ends turns a continuous range of possible waves into a discrete list: this is the first appearance of “quantization” in the course.

Superposition

A general motion of the string (a pluck, a bow stroke) is a sum of normal modes, each oscillating at its own frequency. The mix of harmonics is what makes a violin and a flute sound different at the same pitch.

concept
$$y(0,t) = 0, \quad y(L,t) = 0$$
Boundary conditions
▸
Definition

Conditions the wave must meet at the edges of the medium, for all time. A fixed end means zero displacement there.

Why they matter

The wave equation alone allows any wavelength. The boundary conditions select which ones are allowed, and that selection is where the formulas $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ and $f_n = nf_1$ come from. Mirrors in a laser and metal walls in a microwave oven impose the same condition on light.

method
Show a system is SHM and find ω
“Show that … executes simple harmonic motion”, “find the period / frequency of small oscillations”
▸
Recognize it

A system sits in stable equilibrium and is displaced a little: a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a floating body, a circuit. Any question about its period or frequency is this method.

Steps
  1. Draw the system moved a small distance $x$ from equilibrium.

    Why displaced: at equilibrium the forces cancel, so you cannot see the restoring force. Move it a little first.

    Choose a positive direction: every sign in the following steps depends on this choice.

  2. Find the forces (or torques) on it.

    Where to draw them: each force at the point where it acts: gravity at the centre of mass, tension along the string, the spring force where the spring attaches.

    Which parts matter: only the components along the direction of motion.

    For something that swings or rotates: take torques about the pivot. The pivot force passes through the pivot, so it has no torque and drops out.

  3. Apply Newton's second law: $m\ddot x = F_{\text{net}}$ (or $I\ddot\theta = \tau_{\text{net}}$).

    What it says: mass × acceleration = total force. $\ddot x$ means the second time derivative of $x$, the acceleration.

    Result: an equation linking the acceleration to the position. This is the equation of motion.

  4. Approximate if the force is not exactly proportional to $x$.

    Why: SHM needs force $\propto x$ exactly. Some forces are not: the pendulum has $\sin\theta$.

    The fix: for small angles in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$. Replace it, and the force becomes proportional to $\theta$.

  5. Match the form $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ and read off $\omega$.

    How: divide both sides by $m$ so $\ddot x$ is alone. Whatever multiplies $-x$ is $\omega^2$.

    Example: $\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\,\theta$, so $\omega^2 = g/l$. Take the square root: $\omega = \sqrt{g/l}$.

  6. Get the period and frequency: $T = 2\pi/\omega$, $f = 1/T$.

    Why $2\pi$: $\omega$ is in radians per second, and one oscillation is $2\pi$ radians.

    Check: $\omega$ should contain only constants like $k$, $m$, $g$, $l$. If the amplitude appears, an approximation was missed.

Fit $A$ and $\phi$ to the starting conditions

Recognize it

You already know $\omega$ and are told where the system starts and how fast it moves at one moment.

Steps
  1. Write position and velocity: $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, $v = -\omega A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$.

    Why both: the problem gives a starting position and a starting velocity, so we need a formula for each.

    Where $v$ comes from: differentiate $x$ with respect to time. The chain rule brings out the factor $\omega$.

  2. Set $t = 0$ in both.

  3. Find $A$: $A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}$.

    How: divide the sine equation by the cosine equation; $A$ cancels.

    Careful: a calculator gives only one of two possible angles. Pick the one where $\cos\phi$ has the same sign as $x_0$.

1.2 · A Mass on a Spring
1.2
$F = -kx$
Hooke's law — the restoring force
▸
Parameters

  • $k$spring constant — force per unit displacement (N m⁻¹)
  • $x$displacement from equilibrium (m)
Origin

The minus sign is the entire physical content: the force always points back toward equilibrium, regardless of which side $x$ is displaced to. This — force proportional to displacement, directed opposite to it — is the defining property of every simple harmonic oscillator, not just springs.

1.6
$$\dfrac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\omega^2 x$$
The equation of SHM — boxed in the text; the master equation of the chapter
▸
Parameters
  • $\omega$angular frequency of oscillation (rad s⁻¹)
  • $x$displacement, as a function of time
Derivation
1. Newton's second law: $F = ma = -kx$.
2. Acceleration is the second derivative of displacement: $a = \mathrm d^2x/\mathrm dt^2$.
3. Substitute: $m\,\mathrm d^2x/\mathrm dt^2 = -kx$, then divide by $m$ and write the constant $k/m$ as $\omega^2$.

Every equation in this chapter is either deriving this equation for a new physical system, or solving it. If you can recognize a system reduces to this form, you already know its entire behavior.

1.2.5 · Energy of a Simple Harmonic Oscillator
1.19
$$E = K + U = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$$
Total mechanical energy — boxed in the text
▸
Parameters
  • $K$kinetic energy, $\tfrac12mv^2$
  • $U$potential energy stored in the spring, $\tfrac12kx^2$ (from integrating $F=-kx$, Eq. 1.18)
Derivation sketch

Start from $m\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt = -kx$, multiply through by $\mathrm dx = v\,\mathrm dt$ to get $mv\,\mathrm dv = -kx\,\mathrm dx$, then integrate both sides — the left becomes $\tfrac12mv^2$, the right $-\tfrac12kx^2$, and together they sum to a constant: the total energy $E$.

1.23
$$E = \tfrac12 kA^2$$
Total energy in terms of amplitude alone
▸
Why this matters

Substituting the general solution into $K+U$, the time-dependent $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ terms combine to exactly $1$ (Eq. 1.21–1.23), leaving a constant — confirming energy conservation directly, and showing energy scales with the square of the amplitude. Double the amplitude, quadruple the energy.

1.2.6 · Why Small Oscillations Are Always SHM
1.26–1.27
$$U(x) \approx \frac{x^2}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dx^2}\right)_{x=0}, \qquad F = -x\left(\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dx^2}\right)_{x=0}$$
Taylor expansion near equilibrium — the universal justification for SHM
▸
Reasoning

Any smooth potential well $U(x)$ can be Taylor-expanded about its minimum (Eq. 1.25). The constant term is irrelevant (potential energy has no absolute zero), and the first-derivative term vanishes exactly because $x=0$ is a minimum. What survives, to leading order, is a term proportional to $x^2$ — i.e. a parabolic well, which always produces a linear restoring force. This is the actual reason SHM is universal: any system in stable equilibrium behaves like a harmonic oscillator for sufficiently small displacements, whether it's a pendulum, a diatomic molecule, or a marble in a bowl.

1.3 · The Pendulum
1.29, 1.31
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{g}{l}\sin\theta \;\; \xrightarrow{\text{small }\theta} \;\; \frac{\mathrm d^2\theta}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{g}{l}\theta$$
Simple pendulum equation of motion, exact then small-angle
▸
Parameters
  • $\theta$angular displacement from vertical (rad)
  • $l$length of the pendulum (m)
  • $g$acceleration due to gravity
Why the sine needs replacing

The exact equation has $\sin\theta$, not $\theta$, so it isn't literally SHM. Expanding $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/3! + \cdots$ (Eq. 1.30) and keeping only the first term — valid once $\theta$ is small enough that higher powers are negligible — recovers the SHM form exactly.

Result: $\omega = \sqrt{g/l}$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$ (Eq. 1.32). Note $\omega$ depends on $g$ and $l$ only — not mass, not amplitude.

1.43a–c
$$E = \tfrac12\alpha v^2 + \tfrac12\beta x^2$$
The universal energy signature of SHM
▸
Parameters
  • $\alpha$the "inertia" constant (mass for a spring, $\alpha=m$; moment of inertia for a rotating system)
  • $\beta$the "stiffness" constant (spring constant $k$; or $mg/l$ for a pendulum)
Why this generalizes everything

Both the mass-on-spring energy ($\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$) and the pendulum energy ($\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12\frac{mg}{l}x^2$) are special cases of this one form. Differentiating it with respect to time always reproduces the SHM equation with $\omega=\sqrt{\beta/\alpha}$ — meaning you never need to redo this derivation for a new system: just identify its $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and read off $\omega$ directly.

1.4 · Oscillations in Electrical Circuits
1.54
$$\frac{\mathrm d^2q}{\mathrm dt^2} = -\frac{1}{LC}q$$
The LC circuit — SHM in an electrical system
▸
Parameters
  • $q$charge on the capacitor (C)
  • $L$inductance (H)
  • $C$capacitance (F)
Origin

Kirchhoff's voltage law, $V_C + V_L = 0$, with $V_C = q/C$ and $V_L = L\,\mathrm dI/\mathrm dt = L\,\mathrm d^2q/\mathrm dt^2$ (since $I=\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt$). Same mathematical form as Eq. 1.6, with $\omega = \sqrt{1/LC}$.

Chapter 2 adds one term to the master equation: a velocity-proportional damping force. Everything here is organized around a single comparison — $\gamma^2/4$ (the damping) versus $\omega_0^2$ (the spring-like restoring term) — and which one wins determines whether the system oscillates at all.
Methods · how problems in this chapter are solved
method
Write the damped equation and classify the damping
“A damping force −bv acts …”, “is the motion oscillatory?”, “what value of b gives critical damping?”
▸
Recognize it

SHM plus a force proportional to velocity. The first question is always which regime it is in.

Steps
  1. Write Newton's second law with damping: $m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x$.

    The damping force: $-b\dot x$. It is proportional to the velocity $\dot x$ and always opposes it. $b$ (kg/s) is how strong it is.

  2. Divide by $m$ to get $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0$.

    New names: $\gamma = b/m$ is the damping rate (s⁻¹). $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ is the natural angular frequency squared.

    Why bother: in this standard form, every damped system (spring, pendulum, circuit) uses the same results.

  3. Compare $\gamma/2$ with $\omega_0$ to classify.

    Meaning: $\gamma/2$ is how fast the motion dies away. $\omega_0$ is how fast it wants to swing.

    Light ($\gamma/2 < \omega_0$): it swings back and forth while slowly shrinking.

    Critical ($\gamma/2 = \omega_0$): it returns to rest as fast as possible without overshooting.

    Heavy ($\gamma/2 > \omega_0$): it creeps back slowly without swinging.

  4. For light damping, write the frequency and the decay.

    Frequency: $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$, slightly lower than $\omega_0$.

    Motion: $x = A_0 e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. The cosine is the swinging; $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ is the shrinking.

method
Get γ, τ or Q from decay data
“The amplitude falls to half in …”, “after n oscillations the energy is …”, “find the Q of …”
▸
Recognize it

You are given how fast something dies away, and asked for a damping parameter (or the reverse).

Steps
  1. Decide: is the data about amplitude or energy?

    Why it matters: amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$, energy as $e^{-\gamma t}$. Mixing them up gives an answer off by a factor of 2.

    Where the 2 comes from: energy is proportional to amplitude squared, and squaring $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ gives $e^{-\gamma t}$.

  2. Write the ratio, e.g. $A(t)/A_0 = e^{-\gamma t/2}$.

    Why a ratio: the starting amplitude $A_0$ cancels, so you only need the fraction given (for example “falls to half”).

  3. Take the natural log and solve for $\gamma$.

    How: $\ln$ undoes $e$: $\ln(A/A_0) = -\gamma t/2$. Multiply both sides by $-2/t$: $\gamma = (2/t)\ln(A_0/A)$.

    Example: amplitude halves in 3 s: $\gamma = (2/3)\ln 2 \approx 0.46$ s⁻¹.

  4. Get $\tau$ or $Q$: $\tau = 1/\gamma$, $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$.

    $\tau$: the time for the energy to drop to about 37% ($1/e$).

    $Q$: large $Q$ means many oscillations before the motion fades.

Uses

  • Mixing amplitude and energy decay (factor of 2).
  • Using $\log_{10}$ instead of $\ln$.

2.7–2.8

Light damping — boxed; oscillation with a decaying envelope
▸

  • $A_0$initial amplitude
  • $\exp(-\gamma t/2)$the decaying envelope — not the oscillation itself, just the ceiling it decays within

2.9
$$\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{\gamma T}{2}\right), \qquad \ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right) = \frac{\gamma T}{2}$$

▸

  • $A_n$amplitude of the $n$-th peak; $A_{n+1}$ the next one, one period $T$ later

2.13
$$x = A\exp[(-\gamma/2+\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2})t] + B\exp[(-\gamma/2-\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2})t]$$
Heavy (over) damping — no oscillation at all
▸
Condition

Why no oscillation

Both exponents are real and negative (since $\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2} < \gamma/2$ always), so both terms are pure exponential decays — nothing in the solution oscillates.

2.16
$$x = A\exp(-\gamma t/2) + Bt\exp(-\gamma t/2)$$

▸
Condition

Why this case is special

Of all three damping regimes, this is the one that returns the system to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without ever oscillating past it. That's why it's the design target for shock absorbers, door closers, and analogue meter needles — anything that needs to settle quickly without bouncing.

2.3 · Rate of Energy Loss
2.18–2.19
$$E(t) = E_0\exp(-\gamma t) = E_0\exp(-t/\tau)$$

▸
Parameters

Derivation sketch

Substitute the light-damping solution into $E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2$; for very light damping the oscillating $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ terms again combine to $1$ (as in Ch. 1), leaving a clean exponential decay envelope.

2.21, 2.23
$$Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma} = \frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy dissipated per radian}}$$
The quality factor Q — boxed in the text
▸
Why it exists

$Q$ is a dimensionless "figure of merit" that works for any kind of oscillator — mechanical, electrical, or otherwise — regardless of what units its damping is measured in. Comparing the decay time $\tau$ (how long energy takes to dissipate) to the period $T$ (how long one oscillation takes) is the essential idea: many oscillations before dying out means high $Q$.

Practical readings
  • high $Q$many oscillations before the energy is significantly reduced (quartz crystal: $Q\sim10^6$)
  • low $Q$only a few oscillations, or none (paperweight on a rubber band: $Q\sim10$)
2.24–2.25

The damped oscillator equation, rewritten in terms of Q
▸
Why rewrite it this way

Same physics as Eq. 2.5, with $\gamma$ replaced by $\omega_0/Q$ — useful because $Q$ is often the quantity actually measured or quoted for a real oscillator. Note that even a fairly low $Q=5$ only shifts $\omega$ from $\omega_0$ by about 0.5%, confirming that $\omega\approx\omega_0$ is an excellent approximation whenever damping is light.

method
Write the equation of a travelling wave
“Obtain an equation describing the wave” — PS6 Problem 1
▸
Recognize it

You are given an amplitude, a frequency (or period), a speed (or wavelength), a direction, and something about the start.

Steps
  1. Choose the direction. Toward $+x$: use $kx - \omega t$. Toward $-x$: use $kx + \omega t$.

    What decides it: the sign between the $x$ term and the $t$ term.

    Why: follow one crest. A crest is where the angle inside the cosine is zero, so $kx - \omega t = 0$. Solve for $x$: $x = (\omega/k)\,t$.

    Read that result: as time $t$ increases, the crest's position $x$ increases. The crest moves toward $+x$.

    With a plus sign: $kx + \omega t = 0$ gives $x = -(\omega/k)\,t$, so the crest moves toward $-x$.

  2. Find $\omega$ from the frequency: $\omega = 2\pi f$ (or $2\pi/T$).

    What $f$ means: the source (the hand shaking the end of the string) goes up and down $f$ times per second. Every point on the string copies this: watch any single point and it completes $f$ full up-and-down cycles each second.

    Why we need $\omega$ instead: the cosine takes an angle, measured in radians. One full up-and-down cycle is one full turn of the angle, which is $2\pi$ radians.

    So: $f$ cycles per second × $2\pi$ radians per cycle $= 2\pi f$ radians per second. That is $\omega$.

    Example: $f = 10$ Hz gives $\omega = 2\pi \times 10 = 20\pi \approx 62.8$ rad/s.

  3. Find $\lambda$, then $k$: $\lambda = v/f$, then $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Check that $\omega/k = v$.

    What $\lambda$ means: freeze the wave at one instant, like a photograph. The wavelength $\lambda$ is the distance from one crest to the next.

    Why $\lambda = v/f$: during one period (one up-and-down of a point), the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength. Distance ÷ time gives speed: $v = \lambda / T = f\lambda$. Divide both sides by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.

    Why we need $k$: the cosine needs an angle, so the $x$ term must also be in radians. Moving one wavelength along the string is one full turn, $2\pi$ radians. So radians per metre is $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.

    Check: $\omega/k$ must come out equal to the wave speed $v$. If it does not, one of the numbers is wrong.

    Example: $v = 50$ m/s, $f = 10$ Hz: $\lambda = 5.0$ m, $k = 2\pi/5 = 0.4\pi$ rad/m, and $20\pi/0.4\pi = 50$ ✓.

  4. Find the phase constant $\phi$ from the starting condition.

    What $\phi$ does: it shifts the wave left or right so it starts in the right place.

    How to find it: put $x = 0$ and $t = 0$ into $y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$. Everything inside the cosine vanishes except $\phi$, leaving $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$.

    Set it equal to what the problem says: “maximum upward displacement” means $y(0,0) = +A$. So $A\cos\phi = A$; divide by $A$: $\cos\phi = 1$, so $\phi = 0$.

    Other common starts: at the bottom, $\phi = \pi$; passing through zero, $\phi = \pm\pi/2$.

  5. Check by setting $x = 0$: you should get back the motion of the source.

    Why this works: at $x = 0$ the wave formula describes the end of the string, which is the source.

    Example: $y(0,t) = 0.15\cos(20\pi t)$: amplitude 0.15 m, 10 Hz, at the top at $t = 0$. That is exactly what the problem described ✓.

    If it does not match: look for a missing $2\pi$ or a wrong sign.

Uses

sine sinusoidal wave · defs definitions · speed speed · shape direction rule

Common mistakes
  • Writing $f$ where $\omega$ belongs: the argument of cos must be in radians.
  • Getting the direction backwards: $x - vt$ moves toward $+x$.
Worked examples

PS6 · 1 (King 5-2): string driven at 10 Hz, 0.15 m, 50 m/s.

Go deeper

Route B, from the source. The end at $x=0$ does SHM, $y(0,t) = A\cos\omega t$. A point at distance $x$ does the same thing a time $x/v$ later, so $y(x,t) = y(0, t - x/v) = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. This shows why a wave has the form it does: a delay that grows with distance turns oscillation in time into a pattern in space.

method
Show a function solves the wave equation
“Show that the following are solutions to the wave equation” — PS6 Problem 2
▸
Recognize it

You are handed $y(x,t)$ (or $\psi(x,y,z,t)$) and asked to verify it, or to find the condition that makes it work.

Steps
  1. Take two time derivatives: $\partial y/\partial t$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2$.

    What “partial” means: differentiate with respect to $t$ and treat $x$ as a fixed number.

    Example: $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$. The derivative of the inside, $kx - \omega t$, with respect to $t$ is $-\omega$. Chain rule: $\partial y/\partial t = -\omega A\cos(kx - \omega t)$.

    Again: $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t)$, which is $-\omega^2 y$.

  2. Take two space derivatives: $\partial y/\partial x$, then $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$.

    Now: differentiate with respect to $x$ and treat $t$ as a fixed number.

    Example: the derivative of the inside with respect to $x$ is $k$. So $\partial y/\partial x = kA\cos(kx - \omega t)$, and $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = -k^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t) = -k^2 y$.

  3. Substitute both into $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$.

    Left side becomes $-\omega^2 y$. Right side becomes $v^2 \cdot (-k^2 y)$. So the equation reads $-\omega^2 y = -v^2 k^2 y$.

  4. Simplify to get the condition, usually $\omega/k = v$.

    Divide both sides by $-y$: $\omega^2 = v^2 k^2$. Take the square root: $\omega/k = v$.

    What it means: the function is a solution exactly when its own speed, $\omega/k$, equals the speed $v$ in the wave equation.

Uses

1d wave equation · complex complex form · 3d 3D

Common mistakes
  • Losing the chain-rule factor ($\omega$ or $k$) on each derivative.
  • Shortcut worth stating: if $y$ depends on $x$ and $t$ only through $x \pm vt$, it is a solution automatically.
Go deeper

Why the condition is always $\omega/k = v$: the wave equation contains $v$ and nothing else, so it can only accept shapes that move at $v$. A sinusoid moves at $\omega/k$, so it qualifies exactly when the two speeds agree. In 3D the same step gives $k_1^2+k_2^2+k_3^2 = \omega^2/v^2$: the speed is fixed, the direction is free.

method
Convert between λ, f, k and v
“Calculate the frequency of a wave of wavelength …” — PS6 Problem 3
▸
Recognize it

One of $\lambda$, $f$, $k$ is given; the medium tells you $v$.

Steps
  1. Find the speed from the medium.

    Why the medium: the speed is set by what the wave travels through, not by what made it.

    Values: light, radio, X-rays (all electromagnetic): $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Sound in air: about 340 m/s. A string: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.

  2. Convert to SI units (metres, seconds, hertz).

    Why: $v = f\lambda$ only gives correct numbers when all three are in SI units.

    Example: $500\ \text{nm} = 500 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$. $15\ \text{kHz} = 15\times10^3\ \text{Hz}$.

  3. Apply $v = f\lambda$ and solve for the unknown.

    To find $f$: divide both sides by $\lambda$: $f = v/\lambda$.

    To find $\lambda$: divide both sides by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.

  4. Check the size of the answer.

    Typical values: visible light is about $4$–$8\times10^{14}$ Hz. Sound at 20 Hz has $\lambda \approx 17$ m; at 15 kHz, about 2 cm.

    Why: most mistakes here are a wrong power of ten, and a quick size check catches them.

Uses

speed speed · em EM · sound sound · wavenum wavenumber

Common mistakes
  • Using $c$ for sound.
  • Not stating which “wavenumber” ($1/\lambda$ or $2\pi/\lambda$) you assumed.
Go deeper

The frequency belongs to the source and does not change when a wave enters a new medium; the speed belongs to the medium; the wavelength adjusts so that $v = f\lambda$ still holds.

method
Read the wave off a given equation
“A wave is described by y = 0.02 sin(3x − 12t). Find its speed, wavelength …”
▸
Recognize it

An equation is given and the physical quantities are asked for. The reverse of the first method.

Steps
  1. Match the equation to $A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$ and read off $A$, $k$, $\omega$.

    How: the number in front is the amplitude $A$. The number multiplying $x$ is $k$. The number multiplying $t$ is $\omega$.

    Example: $y = 0.02\sin(3x - 12t)$: $A = 0.02$ m, $k = 3$ rad/m, $\omega = 12$ rad/s.

  2. Convert to $\lambda$, $f$, $T$, $v$.

    Use the definitions in reverse: $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, $f = \omega/2\pi$, $T = 1/f$, $v = \omega/k$.

    Example: $\lambda = 2\pi/3 \approx 2.1$ m, $f = 12/2\pi \approx 1.9$ Hz, $v = 12/3 = 4$ m/s.

  3. Read the direction from the signs.

    Rule: opposite signs on the $x$ and $t$ terms ($3x - 12t$) mean the wave moves toward $+x$. Same signs mean toward $-x$.

Uses

sine · defs · speed

Common mistakes
  • Reading $k$ as $\lambda$ or $\omega$ as $f$: the numbers inside the sine are angular quantities.
Go deeper

Describing a travelling wave
shape
$$y = f(x - vt) \quad\text{or}\quad y = f(x + vt)$$
Any shape moving without change — the definition of a travelling wave
▸
Parameters
  • $y$displacement of the medium at position $x$, time $t$ (m)
  • $f$any function: the shape of the wave
  • $v$wave speed (m s⁻¹)
Origin

At $t=0$ the shape is $f(x)$. At time $t$, the same value of $f$ occurs where $x - vt$ has the same value, i.e. at $x$ shifted by $vt$. So the whole shape slides a distance $vt$ to the right. (More: the characteristic variable u.) Direction rule: $x - vt$ → moves toward $+x$; $x + vt$ → moves toward $-x$.

sine
$$y = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$$
The sinusoidal (harmonic) travelling wave — the standard form used in problems
▸
Parameters
  • $A$amplitude, the maximum displacement (m)
  • $k$wavenumber: radians of phase per metre (rad m⁻¹)
  • $\omega$angular frequency: radians of phase per second (rad s⁻¹)
  • $\phi$phase constant (rad), set by the starting condition
What it says

This one formula gives the displacement $y$ of every point of the medium at every moment. Put in a position $x$ and a time $t$; out comes how far that point is from rest.

Where it comes from
  1. The source does SHM (Ch. 1): $y(0,t) = A\cos\omega t$.
  2. The wave carries that motion along at speed $v$. A point at distance $x$ does the same thing, a time $x/v$ later: $y(x,t) = A\cos\big(\omega(t - x/v)\big)$.
  3. Multiply out and use $\omega/v = k$: $y = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. Cosine is even, so this equals $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$.
  4. Add $\phi$ so the wave can start at any point of its cycle.
Reading the parts
  • $A$: how big the wave is.
  • $kx$: turns position into an angle. Moving one wavelength adds $2\pi$.
  • $\omega t$: turns time into an angle. Waiting one period adds $2\pi$.
  • The minus sign: the wave moves toward $+x$. A plus sign would send it toward $-x$.
  • $\phi$: where in its cycle the wave starts.
How it fits with the rest
  • It is one case of the general travelling wave $f(x - vt)$: shape.
  • Fix $x$ and it is SHM in time: 1.14. Fix $t$ and it is a cosine in space: time and space pattern.
  • Its speed is $\omega/k$: speed.
  • It satisfies the wave equation exactly when $\omega/k = v$: 1D.
  • The complex form $Ae^{i(\omega t - kx)}$ is the same wave, easier to differentiate: complex.
Where it comes up
  • Writing a wave from given facts: method · PS6 · 1.
  • Reading $A$, $\lambda$, $f$, $v$ off a given wave: method.
  • Checking solutions of the wave equation: method (PS6 · 2).
Finding the phase constant

Put $x = 0,\ t = 0$: $y(0,0) = A\cos\phi$. At the top: $\phi = 0$. At the bottom: $\phi = \pi$. Passing zero: $\phi = \pm\pi/2$ (check the direction with $\partial y/\partial t$).

Picture
Watch out
  • $k$ here is the wavenumber, not the spring constant.
  • $\omega$, not $f$, goes inside the cosine: $\omega = 2\pi f$.
  • Sine or cosine is only a choice of $\phi$: $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$.
defs
$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}$$
Converting between the space pattern and the time pattern
▸
Parameters
  • $\lambda$wavelength: distance between neighbouring crests (m)
  • $f$frequency: oscillations per second (Hz = s⁻¹)
  • $T$period: time for one oscillation (s)
Origin

One full cycle is $2\pi$ radians. Moving one wavelength along $x$ advances the phase by $2\pi$, so $k\lambda = 2\pi$. Waiting one period advances it by $2\pi$, so $\omega T = 2\pi$, and with $T=1/f$, $\omega = 2\pi f$.

speed
$$v = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k}$$
Wave speed — links every space quantity to every time quantity
▸
Origin

In one period $T$ each crest moves forward exactly one wavelength, so $v = \lambda/T = f\lambda$. Substituting $f=\omega/2\pi$ and $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ gives $v=\omega/k$.

Picture
Use

Any two of $v$, $f$ (or $\omega$), $\lambda$ (or $k$) give the third. The speed is set by the medium; the frequency is set by the source; the wavelength follows from both.

▸

The wave equation
1D
$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$$
The one-dimensional wave equation — the master equation of the chapter
▸
Parameters
  • $\partial/\partial t$partial derivative: differentiate in $t$ holding $x$ fixed
  • $\partial/\partial x$partial derivative: differentiate in $x$ holding $t$ fixed
  • $v$wave speed
Reading it

Left side: the acceleration of one point of the medium. Right side: the curvature of the shape at that point. Where the string curves most, it accelerates most. This is Newton's second law applied to a small piece of the medium, just as $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ was for one mass.

Why any moving shape solves it

Let $u = x - vt$. Chain rule: $\partial y/\partial x = f'(u)$ and $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = f''(u)$; $\partial y/\partial t = -v f'(u)$ and $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = v^2 f''(u)$. So $\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2$ for any shape $f$. Same for $x+vt$.

Checking a candidate

1. Take $\partial^2y/\partial t^2$. 2. Take $\partial^2y/\partial x^2$. 3. Substitute both and see what condition makes the two sides equal (usually $\omega/k = v$). A sum of solutions with the same $v$ is also a solution (the equation is linear).

complex
$$y = A\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$$

▸
Reading it

The physical wave is the real part, $A\cos(\omega t - kx)$. Each $\partial/\partial t$ brings down $i\omega$; each $\partial/\partial x$ brings down $-ik$. So $\partial^2/\partial t^2 \to -\omega^2$ and $\partial^2/\partial x^2 \to -k^2$. Direction: $\omega t - kx$ moves toward $+x$; $\omega t + kx$ toward $-x$.

A wave reflects from a fixed end; the reflected wave and the incoming wave add, and the sum is a standing wave: a fixed shape that swells and shrinks in place. Because the ends cannot move, only certain wavelengths fit, so the string (or a laser cavity, or a microwave oven) has a list of allowed frequencies, its normal modes. method
Waves on a string: speed, wavelength, and the length for a given note
“Calculate the wavelength … What value of L is required to obtain a fundamental frequency of …” — PS7 Problem 1(a), (b)
▸
Recognize it

You are given a tension $T$ and a mass per unit length $\mu$ (so the speed is known), plus a frequency, and asked for a wavelength or a length.

Steps
  1. Find the wave speed from the string. $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.

    Recall: the speed of a transverse wave on a string is set only by the tension and the mass per unit length, string (Ch. 5).

    Why first: every other quantity is reached through $v$. Check units: $\sqrt{\text{N}/(\text{kg m}^{-1})} = \sqrt{\text{kg m s}^{-2}\cdot\text{m}/\text{kg}} = \text{m s}^{-1}$.

  2. On a long string (no reflections), get the wavelength from $v = f\lambda$.

    Recall: one wavelength passes a point per period, so $v = f\lambda$ speed.

    Operation: divide both sides by $f$: $\lambda = v/f$.

    Why “long”: a long wire means the wave never comes back from an end, so there is no standing wave and any frequency is allowed.

  3. On a string fixed at both ends, draw the mode first. The fundamental is one loop: $L = \lambda/2$.

    Recall: a fixed end cannot move, so it is a node; neighbouring nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart nodes.

    Model: with nodes only at the two ends, exactly one half-wavelength fits: $L = \lambda_1/2$. In general $L = n\lambda_n/2$ modes.

  4. Combine to get the length. $f_1 = v/\lambda_1 = v/(2L)$, so $L = v/(2f_1)$.

    Where it comes from: substitute $\lambda_1 = 2L$ into $f = v/\lambda$ to get $f_1 = v/(2L)$ f_n. Multiply both sides by $2L$ and divide both sides by $2f_1$: $L = v/(2f_1)$.

    Shortcut: if you already have $\lambda$ from the long-string step at the same frequency, $L = \lambda/2$, because the fundamental is half a wavelength.

  5. Check the size.

    Instrument strings are tens of centimetres to a metre or two. A length of kilometres or millimetres means a power of ten has gone wrong (often grams instead of kilograms in $\mu$).

  • Setting $L = \lambda$ for the fundamental. One loop is half a wavelength.

  4. Fixed ends, fundamental: $L = \lambda/2 = 0.114$ m. Same result from $L = v/(2f_1) = 100/880$ m ✓.
Go deeper

The same frequency can be a travelling wave on a long wire or a standing wave on a short one. The wavelength is the same in both cases ($\lambda = v/f$, and $v$ depends only on the wire); what the fixed ends add is the requirement that the length be a whole number of half-wavelengths.

“What are the frequency and wavelength of the sound wave produced by the wire …? Explain any differences.” — PS7 Problem 1(c)
▸
Recognize it

A string vibrates in some mode and you are asked about the sound wave in the air.

Steps

  2. The speed changes: use the speed of sound in air. $v_{\text{air}} \approx 340$ m s⁻¹.

    Why it differs from the string: both waves share $f$, so $\lambda_{\text{air}}/\lambda_{\text{string}} = v_{\text{air}}/v_{\text{string}}$. Whichever medium is faster has the longer wavelength.

  4. Explain the difference in one sentence.

    Same frequency (set by the source), different speed (set by each medium), therefore different wavelength. State this in words: the problem asks for it.

method
Identify mode numbers from two successive modes
“Successive normal modes with wavelengths of … Identify the mode numbers and determine the length of the string.” — PS7 Problem 2(a)
▸
Recognize it

Two neighbouring modes ($n$ and $n+1$) of the same string are given, by wavelength or by frequency; $n$ and $L$ are unknown.

Steps
  1. Write the mode condition for each. $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, so $2L = n\lambda_n$ and $2L = (n+1)\lambda_{n+1}$.

    Recall: for a string fixed at both ends, mode $n$ fits $n$ half-wavelengths: $L = n\lambda_n/2$ modes. Multiply both sides by 2: $2L = n\lambda_n$.

    Why two equations: there are two unknowns, $n$ and $L$, and two given wavelengths.

  2. Decide which wavelength has the lower mode number. The longer wavelength is the lower $n$.

    Why: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ gets smaller as $n$ grows (more loops in the same length, so each loop is shorter).

  3. Set the two expressions for $2L$ equal and solve for $n$.

    Where it comes from: both equal $2L$, so $n\lambda_n = (n+1)\lambda_{n+1}$. Expand the right side: $n\lambda_n = n\lambda_{n+1} + \lambda_{n+1}$. Subtract $n\lambda_{n+1}$ from both sides: $n(\lambda_n - \lambda_{n+1}) = \lambda_{n+1}$. Divide both sides by $(\lambda_n - \lambda_{n+1})$: $$n = \frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{\lambda_n - \lambda_{n+1}}.$$

    Check: $n$ must come out a whole number. If it does not, recheck which wavelength is which.

  4. Substitute $n$ back to get $L$. $L = n\lambda_n/2$.

    Check with the other mode: $(n+1)\lambda_{n+1}/2$ must give the same $L$.

  5. Faster route (optional): $\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}} - \dfrac{1}{\lambda_n} = \dfrac{1}{2L}$.

    Where it comes from: $1/\lambda_n = n/(2L)$. Subtract the expression for $n$ from the one for $n+1$: $(n+1)/(2L) - n/(2L) = 1/(2L)$. So the spacing of $1/\lambda$ between neighbours gives $L$ directly, and then $n = 2L/\lambda_n$. With frequencies the same idea reads $f_{n+1} - f_n = f_1$ spacing.

Harmonics: their frequencies, how many are audible, and stopping a string with a finger
“Frequencies of the second and third harmonics? … total number of harmonics the violinist can hear? … where to place their finger?” — PS7 Problem 3
▸
Recognize it

A string’s fundamental $f_1$ is given and you are asked about its higher modes, a frequency limit, or a shorter vibrating length.

Steps
  1. Harmonic frequencies: multiply the fundamental. $f_n = nf_1$.

    Recall: $f_n = nv/(2L)$ f_n, and $f_1 = v/(2L)$; dividing one by the other gives $f_n/f_1 = n$. The second harmonic is $n = 2$, the third $n = 3$. Naming: the fundamental is the first harmonic.

  2. Does the wave speed change between harmonics? No. Say why.

    Recall: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ depends only on the string string. Going to a higher harmonic does not change the tension or the mass per length. What changes is the wavelength ($\lambda_n = 2L/n$) and the frequency, in opposite proportion, so $f_n\lambda_n = v$ stays the same.

  3. Counting harmonics below a limit: divide and round down. $n_{\max} = \lfloor f_{\max}/f_1 \rfloor$.

    Where it comes from: harmonic $n$ is audible when $nf_1 \le f_{\max}$. Divide both sides by $f_1$: $n \le f_{\max}/f_1$. The harmonics are $n = 1, 2, \dots, n_{\max}$, so their number is the largest whole number not exceeding $f_{\max}/f_1$ (round down: the next one is above the limit). Convert kHz to Hz first.

    State your convention: whether you count the fundamental (it is harmonic 1, so normally yes).

  4. Finger on the string: the vibrating length changes, the speed does not.

    Model: pressing a finger down makes a new fixed point, a node. The string now vibrates only between the finger and the far end (the bridge). Tension and $\mu$ are unchanged, so $v$ is unchanged.

    Equation: the new fundamental is $f' = v/(2L')$, and the open string gave $f_1 = v/(2L)$. Both equal $v$ after multiplying out: $2Lf_1 = 2L'f'$. Divide both sides by $2f'$: $$L' = L\,\frac{f_1}{f'}.$$ A higher note needs a shorter length.

    Answer the question asked: $L'$ is the length still vibrating (finger to bridge). The finger sits $L - L'$ from the other end (the nut, where the string starts). Say which end you measure from.

Count the modes of a laser cavity inside a frequency band
“How many normal frequencies will occur within the range Δν? How long would the cavity have to be so that only one mode frequency occurred?” — PS7 Problem 4
▸
Recognize it

Two mirrors a distance $L$ apart, light of frequency $\nu_0$, and a band of width $\Delta\nu$ in which amplification happens.

Steps
  1. Model the cavity as a string fixed at both ends. The mirrors are nodes.

    Why: at a good mirror the light’s electric field is (nearly) zero, the same condition as a fixed end of a string. So the allowed wavelengths are again $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ modes, and with speed $c$ the mode frequencies are $\nu_n = nc/(2L)$ cavity.

    Notation: King writes frequency as $\nu$ (Greek nu) here. It is the same quantity as $f$.

  2. Find the spacing between neighbouring modes. $\nu_{n+1} - \nu_n = c/(2L)$.

    Where it comes from: subtract $nc/(2L)$ from $(n+1)c/(2L)$. The spacing does not depend on $n$: the modes form an evenly spaced comb (the vertical bars in King’s figure).

    Why $\nu_0$ hardly matters: $n = 2L\nu_0/c$ is around a million; you never need it. Only the spacing decides how many modes fall in the band. $\nu_0$ tells you where the band sits.

  3. Count how many spacings fit in the band. $N \approx \Delta\nu \big/ \dfrac{c}{2L} = \dfrac{2L\,\Delta\nu}{c}$.

    Why a division: the band is $\Delta\nu$ wide and the comb has one tooth every $c/(2L)$. Divide the width by the tooth spacing count.

    Whole numbers: the count of teeth must be an integer. Depending on where the comb sits relative to the band, a ratio of, say, 4.3 gives 4 or 5 modes. Give the whole-number part and say that one more may fit depending on alignment.

  4. For a single mode, make the spacing at least as wide as the band. $c/(2L) \ge \Delta\nu$.

    Why: if neighbouring teeth are farther apart than the band is wide, at most one can lie inside it.

    Operations: multiply both sides by $2L$ and divide both sides by $2\Delta\nu$: $L \le c/(2\Delta\nu)$. A shorter cavity spreads the modes out.

  5. Convert units (cm to m) and check the size.

    Spacings for lab lasers are hundreds of MHz; single-mode cavities are a few centimetres or shorter.

method
Energy of a plucked string: work done against the tension
“Pulled aside a distance h at its center and released. What is the energy of the subsequent oscillations?” — PS7 Problem 5(a)
▸
Recognize it

A string under tension $T$ is pulled into a shape and released from rest; the energy is asked for, with the hint to use work against the tension.

Steps
  1. Model the shape. Draw it: two straight segments from the ends to the pulled point.

    Picture: pulled aside a height $h$ at a point a distance $a$ from one end, the string is two straight lines, of horizontal lengths $a$ and $L - a$, meeting at height $h$.

  2. Find the length of each segment with Pythagoras: $\sqrt{a^2 + h^2}$.

    Recall: the slanted segment is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $a$ (along the string) and $h$ (sideways).

  3. Approximate for a small pull ($h \ll a$): $\sqrt{a^2+h^2} \approx a + \dfrac{h^2}{2a}$.

    Recall: the binomial approximation $(1+\varepsilon)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \varepsilon/2$ for small $\varepsilon$ stretch.

    Operations: factor out $a$: $\sqrt{a^2+h^2} = a\sqrt{1 + h^2/a^2}$. Apply the approximation with $\varepsilon = h^2/a^2$: $a(1 + h^2/(2a^2)) = a + h^2/(2a)$.

    So each segment is longer than its straight length by $h^2/(2\times\text{its horizontal length})$.

  4. Add the extensions to get the total stretch $\Delta L$.

    $\Delta L = \dfrac{h^2}{2a} + \dfrac{h^2}{2(L-a)}$. For a pull at the centre, both horizontal lengths are $L/2$; substitute that yourself.

  5. Work against the tension: $W = T\,\Delta L$.

    Why: work is force times the distance moved along the force. Lengthening the string by $\Delta L$ against a constant tension $T$ costs $T\Delta L$ (the problem tells you to treat $T$ as unchanged by the small stretch).

  6. That work is the energy of the oscillation.

    Why: the string is released from rest, so at release all of its energy is this stored work (potential energy) and none is kinetic. With no damping, energy is conserved, so the total stays equal to $T\Delta L$ while it moves back and forth between potential (stretch) and kinetic (motion) energy.

Uses

stretch · standing wave

Common mistakes
  • Using $\tfrac12 kx^2$ (a spring): the string’s restoring force is the tension, which here is constant.
  • Forgetting one of the two segments.
  • Dropping the factor 2 in the binomial approximation.
Worked example (different numbers from the problem set)
  1. Pulled at $a = L/4$ (a quarter of the way along), height $h$.
  2. Short segment: extension $h^2/(2\cdot L/4) = 2h^2/L$.
  3. Long segment ($3L/4$): extension $h^2/(2\cdot 3L/4) = 2h^2/(3L)$.
  4. Total: $\Delta L = 2h^2/L + 2h^2/(3L) = 8h^2/(3L)$ (common denominator $3L$).
  5. Energy: $E = T\Delta L = 8Th^2/(3L)$. Units: N·m²/m = N·m = J ✓.
Go deeper

The same answer comes from the general formula for the potential energy of a string, $U = \tfrac12 T\int_0^L (\partial y/\partial x)^2\,dx$, which is the extension formula above applied to each small piece: a piece of length $dx$ with slope $\partial y/\partial x$ is stretched by about $\tfrac12(\partial y/\partial x)^2dx$. For two straight segments the slope is constant on each, which reduces the integral to the sum above. (This is the form French uses; it is an extension beyond what the problem needs.)

A standing wave: two equal waves travelling in opposite directions, added
▸
Parameters
  • $A$amplitude of each travelling wave (m); the standing wave swings up to $2A$
  • $k$wavenumber, $2\pi/\lambda$ (rad m⁻¹)
  • $\omega$angular frequency, $2\pi f$ (rad s⁻¹)
Where it comes from
  1. A wave sent toward a fixed end reflects. Both waves are on the string at once: $y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ toward $+x$, $y_2 = A\sin(kx + \omega t)$ toward $-x$ (direction rule).
  2. Recall superposition: the wave equation is linear, so the sum of two solutions is a solution, and the displacement is $y = y_1 + y_2$.
  3. Recall the sum identity $\sin P + \sin Q = 2\sin\frac{P+Q}{2}\cos\frac{P-Q}{2}$.
  4. Set $P = kx + \omega t$, $Q = kx - \omega t$: then $\frac{P+Q}{2} = kx$ and $\frac{P-Q}{2} = \omega t$. Substituting gives $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$.
  5. Check at the fixed end $x = 0$: $\sin 0 = 0$, so $y = 0$ for all $t$ ✓.
Reading it

The $x$ part and the $t$ part have separated. $\sin kx$ is a fixed shape; $\cos\omega t$ makes the whole shape grow, shrink, flip and return, every point in step. There is no $x - vt$: nothing travels. Each point does SHM at the same $\omega$, with its own amplitude $2A|\sin kx|$.

▸
Parameters
  • $T$tension (N), assumed unchanged by the small stretch
  • $\Delta L$increase in length (m)
  • $a$horizontal length of a straight segment (m)
  • $h$sideways displacement (m)
Where it comes from
  1. Work is force times distance along the force; lengthening by $\Delta L$ against $T$ costs $T\Delta L$.
  2. Recall the binomial approximation: $(1+\varepsilon)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \varepsilon/2$ for small $\varepsilon$ (first term of the Taylor expansion).
  3. Factor $a$ out of the square root: $\sqrt{a^2+h^2} = a\sqrt{1+h^2/a^2}$. Apply step 2 with $\varepsilon = h^2/a^2$: $a + h^2/(2a)$.
Use

Energy of a plucked string. Released from rest with no damping, this work is the total energy of the oscillation.

Origin

Mode $n$ has period $T_1/n$, so after $T_1$ every mode has made a whole number of cycles and the full shape is back. Equivalently: a disturbance travels to an end and back (distance $2L$ at speed $v$) and returns upright after two reflections. Here $T_1$ is a period and the $T$ under the root is the tension.
